“...and when one of them meets the other half. The actual half of herself…, they are lost in an amazement of love and friendship and intımacy and one will not be out of the other's sight, as I may say, for even a moment…”

Plato, The Symposium.

Before time began, or, before it was measured in any discernible way, some lucky creatures of this earth were born as pairs.

Born first, somewhat unluckily, separately, they would then find one another and all but fuse together.

This union of mind, body, and soul, was envied by those who were around to witness it. And news of their joy would be spread the world over until the universe herself heard tell of it and started to grow curious.

And so it watched this strange anomaly that had occurred in the world, and observed all the creatures who were lucky enough to be part of a set.

The universe, apathetic to the goings on of mammal or man, soon began to take pleasure in the show of love and devotion these pairs performed.

For there were no two alike. And their love was shown in many varied, delicious ways. Though all were alike in their veneration of the other, and their being only whole when together.

But as the universe witnessed, so did the Gods. Jealous and proud that they were, they soon turned their mighty eyes to this rarefied union of souls.

Though few, their happiness, and therefore their power, was enough to enrage the envy of the Gods. And soon they rained down their wrath and tore these lucky pairs asunder.

Now apart, they suffered and pined for their lost half and wandered the earth in search of them. Few found their other before they simply died of broken hearts and destroyed hope.

And soon, these σύνψυχος soulmates ceased to ever appear again upon Earth’s green lands.

For millennia, then, the Gods triumphed in their defeat of those souls who had the hubris to worship but the other and not Them.

But the universe, a wily beast, unsympathetic to most but tricksy when riled, held steady for long enough that the Gods soon died, having gorged themselves to death on what little praise was left to Them.

And she, now alone in power, alongside her great friend, The Mother Earth, waited and planned and soon created one special soul, cleaved in two.

Though, unbeknownst to the universe, the Gods, greedy and jealous as They were, had left behind old magick. For they had been cunning enough to foresee their own demise in the burgeoning disinterest of their once venerating public.

The magick, so ancient that even the universe herself could not begin to unravel it, meant that though these soul mates could be born they would spend lifetimes apart.

And so the show would go on, built of tragedy rather than romance. One human born with no knowledge of the other but perpetual longing built into her bones that would inevitably etch into them vicious rage and violent fear that would continue throughout each of her lives.

The other, receiving perhaps a worse fate, was born to remember their other half whenever they first encountered her in any lifetime.

This second partner would feel similar longings to her other half, though the pain of it would be tempered by the recollection of their soulmate, of the lives they had lived together, whenever their eyes found her across fields, streets, ballrooms, hospitals.

She would strive to make the other fall for them as they always should have; sometimes she would be successful, other times not. But success would not make her soul mate recognise her. Such was the power of the Gods’ curse.

But still they would try, they would risk everything for their other half. If only for a glimpse of them, for a touch of their hand, for a kiss so yearned for after centuries of searching.

But the universe, tired of seeing these humans suffer when she had created them for great joy, had a trick up her sleeve.

She could not unravel the magick but she could see a way through it.

For suffering had been as the Gods had intended, but this soul that had been conjured by the universe and her friend, had enough power in it to be only once joined.

The trick, the loophole in this ancient magick, was that it needed to be earned. For Gods of such power and majesty, had no knowledge of earning so they had not considered it when creating this curse.

They underestimated the devotion that humans, or creatures of any kind, could have for one another. Thinking they alone would and should be worshipped above all else.

The soul then, split between two yearning bodies, had to earn its right to be whole again; proving through devotion that they deserved to be together again.

And the two who shared this new soul had such great capacity for devotion such as this earth had never seen.

And once they had earned their togetherness, both partners would remember every lifetime together and live, as the universe had intended, with such joy that the world would alight with it.

And so the universe, and her dear friend Mother Earth, had to but wait, and watch, and hope.

It is the burden of a writer to contain within just a few words the sorrows and joys of a lifetime.

Each choice of word must carry with it the memory of childhood or the hopes for a future. And everything, you understand, in between.

And so, each word is chosen with as much care as the universe bestowed upon one soul made for two.

Though an author should be forgiven if their words do not adequately arouse the sheer magnitude of this gift given to two worthy individuals.

Rio Vidal was born of a Spanish fisherman and his wife. They had come to the Americas to seek a new life for themselves in the newly found settlement of St. Augustine on the southeast coast.

Soon after they set foot upon the New World, Rio was made sister to three brothers. She was the calmest of her family, except on the rare occasion when her feelings became too much to bear and they came bursting out of her like fire upon bracken.

So, fiery only when necessary, Rio Vidal was also beautiful and full of sorrow.

As such, she was flighty too, and would often disappear off to the woods in search of she knew not what. But something. Something she could never find.

The Spanish colony of St. Augustine, where we now find ourselves, was freshly formed as Rio was freshly coming of age.

Her father, though loving, had warned her that he could no longer protect her from the inevitable chains of matrimony. He’d refused every suitor that came her way for the better part of seven years. But, now approaching twenty one, he’d warned her that he would accept the hand of the next eligible man who offered.

Rio, knowing not why this thought repulsed her more than it frightened her, could do nothing but accept her fate.

So now, deep in the thick, unwelcoming forest, she hid herself amongst the pinewood trees and the lupine flowers that guided her way through. These lonely forest walks were her only freedom.

And since they’d arrived on these foreign shores the trees had called to her as if they knew something she didn’t.

The day was warm, much warmer than any day she had experienced in her home country. And Rio felt a trickle of sweat creep down her back as she moved further from the sounds of the shore and the people who worked across it.

St. Augustine was a burgeoning colony, almost now a full township. Almost a thousand strong. It was full of soldiers, fishermen, priests with ideas to evangelise.

And then wives and daughters they brought with them.

They were theslrgest settlement for miles around, at least, largest new settlement. For this land was not theirs, but instead stolen from the indigenous population.

Rio had seen little of them but was wise enough to know that her own people were the savages and that they should not be here.

Despite this, she also knew her mission was similar to that of the religious men here. She was to help the settlement to grow by giving them, God willing, new men to strengthen and guard it.

And so her father would soon allow her to be married and she would be one of many new brides swollen with child before the year was out.

But for now, unattached and so heavily burdened, she moved through the forest seeking peace and that something she could not name.

The quiet of the forest soon seeped into her loud mind and Rio, for a little while at least, was able to hear the soft sounds of her heart calling that familiar, indecipherable song.

All around her the very trees began to respond. They seemed to lean in as if to listen more intently, or else to shelter her more closely for the time they could be her guardians.

But peace, or quiet of any kind, did never last long in the surrounds of St. Augustine.

“Who goes there?”

Rio turned to the unfamiliar voice, so unheard of in these woods she traveled so often alone in. She realised she did not recognise either the voice or the words.

“Who are you? Declare yourself!”

A female voice, a voice that made Rio’s heart purr with excitement and the trees whisper their own.

She turned to where the voice had come from and laid eyes, for the first time, upon her soul’s counterpart.

And Rio Vidal, in the middle of a forest in a still-unknown country, realised all this time what she’d been missing.

It wasn’t something it was someone.

“What is your name, girl?”

The woman above her stood tall, her posture as rigid as the trees that flanked her. But her hair, her hair was loose about her shoulders, tied back only at her crown and twisted to form a single braid.

A queen, Rio thought as she blindly stumbled towards her, feeling a sudden, urgent need to be close.

The woman staggered back and Rio lurched towards her as if to catch her but found her fall to be already broken by a tall pine.

“Stay away!” The woman reprimanded, her blue eyes wild with fright as Rio advanced with her arms outstretched.

She could not understand her words but she could read their meaning well enough.

So she stopped not two feet from the woman but found she could not lower her arms for want of still being able to touch her.

But the woman before her did not want to be touched and soon it became abundantly clear why.

Her pale hands had flown to the soft swell of her belly and Rio looked to it and felt something tug at her heartstrings. Its song faltered.

“Embarazada,” she whispered. Pregnant.

Rio knew little about the birthing process but she knew a lot about the months in between. Months of discomfort for the expectant mother, soothed more often by her friends and family and rarely by the man who had caused it.

She had a great deal of sympathy for these women. Women who often were forced into this position again and again. Only the lucky ones chose it.

And now, as she stood before a stranger her whole body seemed to recognise, she found herself begging God for her to be one of the lucky ones.

“Tell me your name,” the woman repeated, standing still against the tall pine. “Do you speak English?”

Rio could not understand what the woman was saying. Her schooling had focused mostly on religious instruction, and the best way to launder linen. She could read and write but took no great pleasure in either.

Besides, it was all in Spanish.

“Your name,” the woman repeated, her blue eyes darting over Rio and landing on her outstretched hands before flicking back again to her face.

In the time it takes a raindrop to decide to fall, Rio decided that her heart was no longer her own. Though, like rain, she had no way of knowing that she had been built for the fall. Made, from the very first, to fall for one thing, one person.

As her heart thrummed the rhythm it was always meant to, her eyes darted about the stranger before her.

She was tall, taller perhaps than half the woman in the village. Though no taller than Rio. Her skin, what she could see of it, her face, her hands, her neck, was pale. And her eyes, a kind of blue that Rio believed she had seen before.

Are you comfortable?

Because she felt it too.

“No.” Rio knew this word so adhered to it. “Stay. Do not leave me.”

The request did not need to be translated.

“Estoy aquí. Estás bien.”

Though she did not.

She did something both reckless and unimaginable.

She climbed onto the bed with Agatha and she sat behind her, cradling her as best her body could. Agatha’s mother protested with all her might until the doctor intervened and Rio was no longer the victim of untranslated furies.

“Estoy aquí. Estás bien.”

Her hands were taken by Agatha, those same pale fingers that had not been far from her own since the meeting in the forest, slipped between the gaps.

Agatha screamed through the delivery of a too-small baby. And Rio knew from the sight of the blood that came with it, that she had already lost Agatha.

“Estoy aquí. Estás bien.”

Agatha’s screams had faded now, sucked up into the heat of the room as the doctor and her mother took away her baby and left Rio holding her other half.

Above her heart, a golden stone, sewn in place there for the woman who had gifted her it.

Rio Vidal returned to the water that had so gifted her Agatha Harkness.

The universe's friend, the great mother, made sure to take her quickly, so as to put an end to the young woman’s immense suffering.
