Summary

Once in a generation, a significant event sweeps over humanity, altering the course of history.

When an enigmatic meteorite lands in the California desert, the scientific world erupts. British astrophysicist Dr. Agatha Harkness, a prodigy who once redefined how galaxies are understood, never doubts her place on the government-funded research team, especially now, after years of intellectual stagnation have left her desperate for a breakthrough. But when she is inexplicably excluded, her carefully built equilibrium is threatened to fall apart.

An ocean away, a local aerospace engineer faces the same unfair rejection. For Dr. Rio Vidal, whose life has been shaped by space, the exclusion feels personal, almost like destiny misfiring.

Refusing to accept the verdict, both scientists pursue an unlikely solution. Yet what begins as a pragmatic arrangement soon reveals a deeper resonance. As Agatha and Rio navigate ambition, bureaucracy, and the gravity between them, they begin to wonder whether rejection was never a cruel cosmic insult at all, but rather an alignment.

Because some paths aren’t written in the stars. They’re encoded within the dark matter, where two currents finally converge.

Agatha let out a slow, unimpressed hum into the rim of her teacup before taking another sip. "I can, actually," she said coolly, not even bothering to look up from her screen. "He is likely shagging someone on the Committee."

Ah, yes. It should have been.

The amusement vanished from Agatha's face so quickly it was almost chilling. Her posture stiffened, shoulders pulling tight beneath the silk of her blouse. "It's done." Her tone turned clipped, icy.

She pinched the bridge of her nose before slamming the lid of her laptop shut with rather more force than strictly necessary. The impact set the glass on the coffee table into a series of tiny, furious ripples of miniature quake waves.

"There is nothing you or I can do about it now," she said quietly, though the rigid set of her jaw betrayed exactly how badly she wanted that statement to be untrue.

They had just announced the recipients of several major academy grants, tied to the research programme for the massive meteorite that had crash-landed in California several weeks ago. The California Institute of Technology was assembling a team and had issued invitations to a handful of high-profile scholars. The funding was limited, and the project had no fixed timeline—the sort of research that could take years or even decades, depending on what they discovered. Agatha had been certain she'd be selected. Why wouldn't she be?

Agatha Harkness was a Doctor of astrophysics—not merely credentialed, but formidable. By her late twenties, she had already upended an entire subsection of the domain. While most people her age were fixing their CVs or figuring out how taxes worked, Agatha was pioneering a revolutionary method for analysing galactic data—disentangling light, distance, motion, and mass with such elegance that half the field quietly pretended they understood how she'd done it.

Her work became mandatory reading. Her algorithms were licensed across continents. Her name climbed onto lists reserved for prodigies and Nobel prospects. She was the rare kind of scientist who didn't just follow the edge of discovery—she dragged it forward. Her life's passion, the thread running through every paper, every sleepless night, every scribbled equation, had always been the same: proving and eventually utilising Einstein's theoretical wormholes. Not hypothetically or philosophically, but practically.

While most researchers stayed in their lane, Agatha took the entire highway: a blend of astronomy, quantum mechanics, theoretical and practical physics, chemistry, bioengineering, molecular biology, and whatever other disciplines were foolish enough to get in her way, revealing their applicability. She worked with the precision of a surgeon and the vision of a mad inventor. It made her extraordinary. It also made her terrifying. Yet, brilliance was not a shield against stagnation.

Somewhere in her early forties—right when everyone expected her to make her next great leap, that would finally earn her the Nobel—Agatha hit a wall. Every calculation felt like déjà vu. Every theory circled back into itself. Every attempt to reach the next rung of discovery collapsed before she could touch it. It was as though her mind—once unstoppable—had been caught in the dead grip of an energy dam, and nothing, absolutely nothing, could break that static fog.

She wasn't moving. Her research wasn't moving. The universe seemed to watch her every move with a held breath, waiting for her to either break through or break apart. She hid the panic, of course, behind her infamous demeanour. She pretended to work on a highly classified project, the details of which she couldn't share yet. But the truth was that Agatha was drowning in a silence she couldn't outthink or outwork. She needed an intervention, an inspiration, a muse. Anything. She needed something to shock her out of this block, to rewire the trapped current within her and set it free from its cage, reminding her that she was still capable of brilliance, not just the memory of it.

The meteorite felt like a lifeline—a spark, a jolt, the thing that might resuscitate her career and her mind. So, when the results rolled over, she wasn't just disappointed; she was devastated. It felt like she had glimpsed a faint ray of light somewhere over the horizon, but before she could even look more carefully to distinguish it, it vanished right beneath her fingertips. She felt like some invisible force had punched her in her gut, twisting the insides and ripping them out. It felt like a rug had been pulled from under her feet. But, of course, she would never show any of that to other people, so instead she opted for the controlled violence.

Never show the world what you feel, Agatha, her mother had always told her. The moment they see your vulnerability, they shall use it against you. Emotions make you weak. You are the Harkness, and are not weak—we rule the world of science, and the power of our minds is our legacy.

Agatha had learned to mask, manipulate, and deflect at a very young age. Being around someone who had moved through life, wielding those skills with an effortless grace, had made such personal transmutation feel natural. Growing up, she genuinely didn't even acknowledge there was another way to engage with the world. Other people cried, trusted too easily, spoke openly about their feelings, but Agatha had always looked at them with quiet confusion, as though they were willingly walking around without skin. Her mind could not comprehend that.

It had shaped her into the person she was today, and that person was powerful, influential, inspiring, and totally untouchable. Despite all the allocations, awards, and praise she had earned and kept receiving, there was one thing that all her might failed to protect from becoming collateral damage. Agatha was completely and utterly alone.

The way she had been raised to perceive the world had left a permanent mark on her essence. It made her suspicious of affection, instinctively guarded, emotionally disconnected, and, well, arrogant. She knew she was arrogant—she was not delusional. Agatha knew exactly what people thought of her. The cold British prodigy with the razor-sharp tongue and impossible standards. The woman who could reduce seasoned academics to nervous wrecks with a single unimpressed stare across a conference table.

Agatha knew her price; she was aware she turned heads. People were smitten by her status, her mind, and her appearance, so she had never known a lack of attraction and attention. What she did lack was genuine connections. No matter what she tried, she just could not force her system to open up and be emotionally available to build a relationship, be that an intimate or a platonic one. And perhaps it privately bothered her when she was younger, watching her colleagues experience falling in love, dating, moving in together, and starting families. But only a little—her mind had always prioritised science. Science made sense. Science rewarded precision, discipline, and brilliance. It did not demand vulnerability in return.

Agatha would never admit it aloud, but she did want those things. She wasn't a cold-hearted, pretentious, calculated cunt. Well, she was, but that was not all she was—it was merely the polished mask she offered the world because it kept her protected. Nobody will ever know that beneath that mask lived a human, someone who occasionally caught herself longing for something warm and domestic, someone who sometimes wondered what it might feel like to come home to another person who would take her coat and kiss her cheek. But the world worked in such a way that it didn't allow her to reveal the depth of her soul.

The moment they see your vulnerability, they shall use it against you. You are the Harkness, and are not weak. Her mother's words echoed in her mind. Even now, decades later, they still carried the same cold weight inside her chest. Self-preservation—she couldn't afford to risk her legacy just because she was shagging someone who wanted to know her better. And people who had been blessed with her generosity to allow them to join her in bed did want to know her better. It wasn't a problem when she was younger—casual hook-ups are always fun when you're both young, and the world is your oyster. Back then, everyone was reckless in one way or another and emotionally avoidant in equal measure. A night together could simply remain a night together; sometimes it could turn into a casual thing. It was easier when she was younger.

But time went by, Agatha was getting older and wiser, and her carefully picked-up sexual partners were getting wiser, too. So, it was only natural that the older she got, the more seriously her partners took their "relationship". And that became a problem. Because Agatha didn't do relationships. Emotional vulnerability, intimacy, commitment—those were all the ingredients for endangering her legacy. So, somewhere around her late thirties, she simply shut down from the entire social frequency. It was better that way, she told herself. All those sexual encounters only distracted her from work. Too much energy spent on talking, on figuring out the relationship dynamic, on fighting, and, in the end, on disappointing people when they inevitably discovered there was a wall within her soul they could not climb over, no matter how much affection and devotion they offered. She didn't need people to satisfy her sexual needs.

Now, the only person that Agatha had in her life was Jen. And since Jen was a handful, that was more than enough. If you had asked Agatha how she had even allowed another person so close to her, she would sigh in annoyance and say she hadn't. That stubborn bitch just bulldozed her way into her life, made camp, and stayed. No matter what Agatha did, no matter how mean she was, Jen simply wouldn't leave. Years of trying to weed her out. Years of dry remarks, emotional distance, unanswered texts, sharp-tongued insults that would've sent anyone else running for the hills. Somewhere along the way, Agatha just gave up, surrendering to that strange current of energy between them, and let it be. But in the privacy of her own mind, she was grateful to have a solid, loyal friend whom she could always count on, even though she would never make it known.

"Well, there goes my career-changing spark of opportunity," she muttered sarcastically between clenched teeth, rising to her feet and stalking toward the kitchen.

More tea. As if that would do anything. No amount of tea could soothe this level of aggravation, but she had been sober for the past twenty years, and no matter how vast the void of burnout was, she wasn't going to break her streak. She left that life behind. Left that Agatha behind.

"So, just go anyway," Jen suggested, shrugging nonchalantly.

Agatha raised her eyebrow, turning from her task of pouring the tea and looking at Jen as if she had grown three heads. "Come again?"

Jen, though, didn't look hesitant at all. As if all of that was something as easy as planning a holiday. "Surely, you can afford to go and start the research," she said.

That was true—Agatha was reasonably wealthy by academic standards. It helped that her mother, Doctor Evanora Harkness, was an evil schemer who stripped all three of her husbands of their wealth, invested most of it in various studies, and tripled the amount over the span of her lifetime. She was so busy making the name and the bank account that she hadn't noticed she was terminally ill until it was far too late. Despite their fractious relationship, Agatha inherited everything because Evanora was too preoccupied to make a will, and Agatha was her only child. So, yes, between the safety net in her bank account, the passive income from the investments, and her royalties, Agatha definitely felt more than comfortable.

"By the time you get the first set of results, you'd likely get the necessary support and additional financing." Jen went on, pausing for a second, before finally adding, "Not that you need it."

"I can't just go," Agatha argued. "Well, I could, but such research would require at least a couple of years to outline the work, as we have absolutely no clue what we are dealing with. A tourist visa won't allow me to stay that long. And I can't get a work one issued with an open date independently without being a part of the official research team."

It was, in her opinion, utter bollocks. She could afford to go. She was intelligent enough not just to be part of a bloody team, but to lead one. Yet, here she was, trapped in a cage—destined to make a change, but unable to move because of a stupid bureaucracy and a horny old bag, Karen Davis. A chill shiver ran down her spine. Gross.

"Then marry a Yank," Jen said simply, as if suggesting a different brand of biscuits—one you'd grab off the shelf without a second thought.

Agatha froze mid-pour. The stream of hot water wobbled dangerously close to the rim of the teapot before she righted it with a sharp, irritated motion. She stared at Jen over the steam curling up between them, her mind scrambling to catch up with the absurdity of the suggestion. She wished she possessed even a fraction of Jen's uncanny ability to remain emotionally unaffected by anything. She couldn't understand how she did that—the woman could propose arson with the same tone she used to ask for oat milk. But this… This wasn't a casual solution. This was pure nonsense.

"I beg your pardon?" Agatha's voice rose to a scandalised octave, sharp enough to cut through diamond.

Agatha Harkness would never pay for such a service. Ever. The very idea was an affront—an insult to her dignity, her intellect, her face. She was charming. Witty. Impeccably put together. Simply magnificent, frankly. Have you seen her? She had no trouble attracting women. None whatsoever. There was absolutely no universe in which Agatha Harkness would acquire a spouse the way one ordered a mail-order appliance.

Agatha inhaled sharply, already gathering momentum for a proper rebuttal, but Jen raised a palm, cutting her off with infuriating calm. The gesture was silent but authoritative, a request for patience that Agatha had never possessed. She pressed her lips together, jaw tightening, only because it gave her an extra minute to formulate her sophisticated insult better.

"You're stable—financially and mentally," Jen began, eyeing her critically. Then she squinted. "Well. Mostly stable mentally," she amended, rolling her eyes. Agatha bristled. "You're socially decent," Jen continued, unbothered. "You don't smoke. You don't drink. You don't do drugs. As a matter of fact, you don't do anything besides science." She gestured vaguely, as if Agatha's entire existence could be summarised by equations scrawled on a chalkboard and by the organised chaos of books, magazines, and notes spread across every surface of her flat. "And you're bloody attractive." Agatha sniffed, chin lifting a fraction in unconscious agreement. "I'm sure plenty of women would be willing to help you out," Jen went on, lips curling into a wicked smirk. "Not for the money—but because you could absolutely smite them with your witchy cosmic powers."

The room fell quiet, save for the soft tick of the kettle cooling on the counter. Agatha stared at her, expression carefully neutral—because admitting Jen might be right was entirely out of the question. Very well. If one were to think about it. Properly think about it…

There was, statistically speaking, likely some painfully earnest nerd lurking in their extended academic ecosystem who had never so much as grazed a woman's breast in their entire life. Someone who desperately needed to appear socially adequate. Respectable. Normal. Someone for whom a partner would function less as romance and more as… social armour. And Agatha wasn't just anybody. She was bloody Doctor Agatha Harkness. Anyone with half a functioning brain cell would seize the opportunity. The lucky candidate wouldn't merely gain a spouse—they would gain status. They would be elevated. Envied, even. People would whisper, Do you even know who she is married to? with a mixture of awe and disbelief. And in exchange, Agatha would be able to remain in the United States indefinitely. She would work. Research. Sink herself wholly back into the problem that mattered. No compromises. No interruptions. No bureaucratic chokehold on her future. Quite a mutually beneficial arrangement, frankly. Elegant. Efficient. Practically altruistic. Although Agatha would never admit out loud that Jen was right about anything. So instead, she merely hummed. Cool, noncommittal, and entirely deceptive.

Later that night, Agatha found herself seated in front of her laptop once more, legs tucked beneath her on the sofa, the room lit only by a single lamp in the corner of the sitting room. She turned the device slowly in her hands, visually inspecting the damage with a careful gaze, her fingertips tracing its surface as if it could miraculously repair under her touch. She did this more often than she cared to acknowledge—vandalised things in fits of temper, only to examine the aftermath later with detached regret. Objects bore the brunt of her frustration far more often than people did, but only because people could affect her reputation. By the time she came back to herself, the damage was already done. Her thumb brushed a tiny fracture at the corner of the screen. She sighed. Another thing touched by the hands of the Harkness—perished.

With a flick of her finger, she brought the laptop back to life and absently navigated to the academy Discord server. The familiar interface bloomed across the screen, a cascade of channels and unread messages waiting to be ignored. She paused, staring at it longer than necessary, as though the server might object to what she was about to do.

Right then, maybe there was, indeed, a woman out there, on the outskirts of their community, who needed a wife as desperately as Agatha. Someone equally cornered by circumstance, equally motivated by necessity rather than sentiment. Stranger arrangements had succeeded on far shakier foundations than this. What harm would it do to try? In the worst-case scenario, there would be no response at all. The post would vanish into the digital aether, ignored and forgotten, and Agatha could quietly pretend it had never happened. No loss of dignity. No witnesses. But there was—however slim—a chance that someone might come through.

Jaw set, she flexed her fingers, and without giving herself time time overthink it, typed:

Wanted: American wife.
Non-smoker, non-ugly.

There. Short. Decisive. Almost elegant in its bluntness. It conveyed precisely what was required and nothing more—no sentimentality, no room for misinterpretation. A statement of fact, not an invitation to debate. Perfect. She clicked on the message and created a thread beneath it. Agatha harboured no illusions about the likelihood of success. Frankly, she doubted there would be any takers at all. Still, thoroughness was a habit she had never outgrown, and she wasn't about to abandon it now simply because the circumstances were absurd. She bit the inside of her lower lip, considering for half a second, then began to type.

Additional details, for clarity:
Minimum age: thirty-five (35).
(If you are old enough to run a country, you are old enough to manage this arrangement.)

I offer a respectable name, established community standing, and long-term stability. All financial expenses will be covered in full. Your material needs will be met without discussion or inconvenience.

In return, I require the legal status of an American spouse for the duration of my research. Upon completion of said research, we may pursue an amicable divorce—unless, at that time, we mutually determine that the continuation of this marriage remains beneficial.

Discretion will be maintained—all necessary paperwork signed. Emotional entanglement is neither expected nor required.

Direct messages are open for further questions.
Serious inquiries only.

She reread it once, expression unreadable, almost clinical, and entirely unromantic. It sounded less like a personal ad and more like the terms of a temporary professorship—which, frankly, felt appropriate. Anyone offended by the tone was, by definition, not suited for the role. Satisfied, Agatha posted the thread and leaned back, exhaling through her nose. If nothing came of it, so be it. At least she'd done her due diligence.

Before doubt could creep in, Agatha carefully shut the laptop with a final, resolute click. She carried her empty cup to the sink and washed it slowly, as if restoring order to her personal universe, one mundane task at a time. Then she turned out the light and went to bed.

They dropped back onto the sofa with a heavy sigh, elbows braced against their knees as they bowed their head. Dark curls fell forward, shadowing their expression. "And it would need a ton of investment," they muttered. The words tasted bitter on their tongue, like defeat dipped in depression. "Like, a government-funding-level ton."

Alice was quiet for a beat. Then her eye lit up. "How about that trust fund that your millionaire grandmother prepared for you?" she suggested questioningly.

Rio let out a dry, humorless huff that barely even qualified as laughter. "Not an option." They dragged a hand down their neck, fingers pressing hard into soft tissue in an unsuccessful attempt to calm their fluttering pulse. "Her only requirement is the one thing I will apparently never achieve in this lifetime."

The self-deprecation landed flat and exhausted rather than dramatic. Rio stared at the floor for a moment before exhaling slowly through their nose. "I have to be married to use that money. Married." The word landed with dull finality, heavy and rude like the door being shut in your face. "She stored literal millions of dollars specifically for family building." Rio pinched the bridge of their nose, squeezing their eyes shut. Even saying it out loud made it sound ridiculous—like the plot of a badly written romance novel no one would ever greenlight.

Alice didn't respond right away. She stared into the middle distance, the way she did when her brain was downloading an idea at lightspeed. Rio recognized the look. It was the same one she got right before saying something unhinged. They had already begun sinking back into the cushion, preparing for another round of sulking, when suddenly, Alice's voice reappeared at the edge of their orbit.

"So," she said brightly, as if rejoining a conversation that had never paused, "we just need to find you a wife." She sounded very pleased with herself, as if she had just had a lightbulb go off over her head.

Rio snorted. "Right. As if I haven't been trying to meet someone for the past forty-four years." They waved a hand dismissively. "Not happening. Sorry, pal. Not in my cards."

Yet, Alice didn't back up. "No, I meant, we just need to find you a wife. On paper. Doesn't have to be a real relationship." She leaned forward, hands animated, mischief spread across her face, and grabbed their knee. Rio arched a brow, skeptical.

"Think about it," Alice continued, voice dropping into something conspiratorial. Her hand let them go and began to move as she spoke, mapping the idea in the air. "All you need is an arrangement—a previously agreed contract with mutual benefits for both parties." She grinned. "We just need to find a woman who is in desperate need of something you could provide, and that's the sealed deal."

The room went quiet.

Rio stared at her, their mind reluctantly beginning—against their will—to turn over the idea they'd nearly dismissed. Alice's plan, while not being completely far-fetched, lacked one monumental variable—another woman. Someone who needed something that badly that she'd agree to such a paper affair with them. Rio didn't have issues with self-esteem, per se. They were reasonably attractive, kept in shape—were one of those handsome faces on great shoulders—and quite smart. Women did notice them.

When Rio was younger, they would occasionally give that a try, yet all attempts to build something serious ended the same way—after getting to know them better, they all fled. Whether that was due to Rio's neurodivergent tendencies, or their refusal to pick one gender and perform consistency for other people's comfort, or something entirely else, they didn't know. They'd stopped trying to figure it out years ago. All they knew was the fact that nobody wanted to commit to them outside of a casual relationship based on sex. And when Rio was younger, it worked just fine—fun, simple, uncomplicated, pleasant. Who would say no to some good times with a hot lady with no emotional maintenance?

But time went by, and something in Rio rewired—they noticed one day that such connections stopped being appealing. Maybe that was because they began to better understand the concept of energy—how intimacy created currents, how bonding left residue, and such casual acts of energy bonding were now viewed as filthy and wrong within their entire system, or maybe Rio simply grew out of it. Whatever the reason, those shallow connections stopped feeding them, so they lost a place in their life. Rio wouldn't have minded starting a family, but there seemed to be no volunteers. So, they stopped trying, stopped hoping, and eventually stopped even considering such daydreams. They focused on their work instead—on equations that made sense, systems that obeyed rules, a universe that responded to effort and discipline. They were done begging the cosmos for something that clearly wasn't designed for them.

Quantum physicists liked to say that the universe listened—not in words, but in frequencies. That intention, like any informational input, disturbed the cosmic field. Given the right energy amplitude and resonance, even a thought could someday collapse into form. Not because the universe was kind, or because magic existed. But because of probability pushed hard enough in the right direction. Because intention wasn't creation—it was alignment.

So, Rio let the thought travel. They sent it outward, threaded through light and time, a gentle charge of intent released into the dark. Not with hope, exactly. Certainly not with expectation. Just… acknowledgment. A signal cast into the vastness, knowing it might never echo back. In fact, they didn't expect it to return at all. Didn't expect it to resolve into anything measurable. But the universe had a habit of answering when spoken to in its own language.
