Summary

Set in the early 19th Century, The Vidal Family’s standing comes into question when their sole heir and daughter, Rio Vidal, nears the age of seven-and-twenty, unmarried and far too preoccupied with the trouble of the neighbor’s adopted stable girl, Agatha.

In the face of societal rumor mongering and her daughter's peculiarities, Lady Vidal orchestrates a summer of masquerades to help encourage Rio to procure a respectable engagement.

But can one find love hiding behind a mask?

OR

Rio Vidal finds herself caught between the forbidden, unrealized feelings for a woman of the wrong class and the enchanting mystery of a masked suitor who promises everything society demands of her.

Chapter 1: Hide Your Face, So The World Will Never Find You

The city liked to whisper that girls raised beyond the echoes of its cobbles grew to possess an unnatural spirit. It was no wonder then, when Agatha first arrived, that Rio had looked at the girl as though she were a demon. 

A thin, bruised thing with calloused fingers caked in dirt, a mess of brown hair, and terrifying blue eyes. 

She must have been no more than fifteen, found on a path alone in the countryside, with no history to account for except her wit and apparent horsemanship. A charity case, many said, for the widow next door who had taken her in. 

Mrs Davis sat across the table and confided in her neighbor, as she so often did. 

“I'd hope your daughter might show her a kindness? The girl is,” Mrs Davis paused, “...antisocial.”

“Antisocial?” Lady Vidal inquired. 

“Antagonistic is perhaps a better capture of her character,” Mrs Davis added, “A terror truly. The staff is quite unsettled. The maids, especially. I'm certain there is a lady in there. She is merely struggling to find her footing. Poor thing.”

“Poor indeed,” Lady Vidal echoed, reaching for her tea, “Though, surely my daughter would be up to it. Lord knows she could use the company, given her own, well, antisocial tendencies as you so fondly put it.”

Rio rolled her eyes as she eavesdropped from beyond the receiving rooms' doors, a small groan spilling from her lips. 

Mrs Davis smiled, “Well, then it seems we have a match.”

“We certainly do not have a match!” Rio’s impulse gave way. 

“Victoria Vidal!” Her mother scorned, “What did I tell you about listening in to other’s conversations?”

“To ensure anything of interest was shared with you immediately,” Rio teased, swiping a finger sandwich from the table and popping it into her cheeks. 

“Sneaky little creature,” Mrs Davis laughed, “Perhaps we should cancel this whole thing. I see now that you both together may ruin us.”

Eight Years Later

Rio awoke to find her early morning peace broken by the clattering pangs of metal and a man’s voice slurring through angered phrases.

“Where is she?! Where is that wretched thing?!”

The chaos continued as she pulled herself from bed, yawning and rubbing her eyes while her mind fought through its fog. She wrapped herself in a light shawl and unlatched the window, swinging it open to face the commotion across the stone alleyway and side gardens that separated the Vidal House from that of Mrs Davis’s.

There, a short, balding gentleman in a soiled tailcoat appeared to be tripping over himself as he collected stones and hurled them at Mrs Davis’s house. Their weight pinged over and over again, colliding with the iron-wrought balconies that framed the second-story windows. 

Aiming for Agatha’s, no doubt. Luckily for the rest of the Davis household, she was the only one who occupied that wing. 

Rio usually would have delighted in the spectacle; another one of Agatha’s disgruntled acquaintances making a fool of themselves across the pavement. But this morning, well, frankly, it was simply too early. 

“Mr Jefferies, whatever could have happened for us to be graced with your delightful presence so soon in the day?” Rio called.

The old man’s head spun to chase the sound, losing his balance as he stumbled backwards into the hedges. 

At some point in time, he had been a man of notoriety, a promising second son of a Baron. 

Now, he was far more known for his smell; a sour, twisted air of gin and bile.  

“You! You and your, your, your…” Mr Jefferies stammered.

“My what?” Rio questioned.

“...your whore!” He screamed, fists and arms shaking as he struggled to untangle himself from the branches that clung to his coat.

“Now, now, Jeffie, we’ve been through this. I much prefer harlot,” Agatha stated as she squeezed her way into the frame.

“Wouldn't want to spend another season behind the iron, now would we, Jeffie?” Agatha added with a feigned pout.

“See you there!” Agatha waved, blowing a kiss in the process as Mr Jefferies gathered himself to mutter his curses back through the streets. 

Rio swatted her arm as she pushed past her, pulling the windows shut across them with an unamused huff.

“Just get out before my mother catches you,” she paused, “and better yet, cut me a portion.”

Agatha smiled before reaching into her coat and placing three quid on Rio’s dressing table. 

“For the hospitality.” She curtsied. 

Rio watched as Agatha climbed back through the window and descended the wisteria vines that climbed the walls of the Vidal House, just as she had countless times before, her incredible habit for attracting trouble matched only by her lack of boundaries.

They called to each other in silly habits and travelled memories and the trickling of keys along a square piano. When her father was in town, it would often be filled with gentlemen and their brandy, staying up through the night in animated discussions of policy and philosophy. In his absence, it was Rio’s sanctuary, her current readings and sketches thrown about the room in a haphazard picture of wandering thought. 

When she entered, Lady Vidal was pacing along the floor in an elegant blue half dress, her gaze focused on the floor, her hands animated in thought.

Her forehead was drawn into such a crease that her face seemed painted in nerves. She looked as though she were rehearsing.

“Mother?” 

“Oh!” Lady Vidal startled,  “Victoria, you're here. Please, come sit.”

“Everything is quite fine, dear,” she replied with a dismissive yet suspiciously high pitch. “I simply wanted to enjoy a moment with my only child. It's been months, it feels, since we have last spoken.”

Rio narrowed her eyes, her curiosity inevitably winning out over her desire to argue.

Chapter 2: Fool and King, Ghoul and Goose

Rio should have laughed at Agatha’s callous humour; usually, she would have, but in that moment, she found herself staring, studying her scrupulously as though searching for something that would save her from her own horrible restlessness pounding in her chest.

But there was just Agatha. 

And how infuriating she was.

Rio threw another vile swig of gin down her throat before she spoke.

“I am to marry,” she stated. “By the end of this season. My mother is insistent, and I, well, I cannot seem to come up with any way out of it. ”

“You only ever seem to want my company to escape the turmoil of your own status.” 

“That's not true.” 

“Is it not?” 

“No.”

The two women seemed caught in each other’s eyes, trying to piece together the other’s defensiveness, the silence thick and heavy and waiting. 

Agatha was the one who broke it. She shook her head and picked herself up off the floor. Rio merely watched her, uncertain of the conversation Agatha was clearly having as she dismissed herself.

Agatha lingered for only a second, the lateness of the evening still and silent across the room.

“Perhaps it is good that you will marry,” she said plainly, refusing to look again in Rio’s direction.

Rio’s eyes stung with the threat of tears, while the words twisted gently into the fresh wound already square along her chest.

Agatha inhaled, her back straightening.

And as Agatha’s steps disappeared down the hallway, Rio, once again, found her body aflame, left to writhe in her torment without protest. Only now, there was nowhere else for her to escape it.

Chapter 3: Every Face a Different Shade

The next morning, she found nothing of her but a horribly made bed and a nauseating pressure between her eyes.

“I am not a fool. And you’d do best to stop acting like one.”

The week moved on with a devastating certainty. The town chattered, and the house hummed in anticipation of the first masquerade. Her mother had made it so.

Meanwhile, Rio distracted herself as much as she could. She passed like a tide between her room and the library, depressed and poring over an assortment of revisited medical books and journals, sketching and resketching figures of cadavers, their musculature and organs splayed open in hideous, pinned structures that blurred the lines between science and man’s morbidity. 

Oh, how she wondered, if only she could trade places with them. 

Her insipid melodrama was interrupted only once at the insistence of her mother that she may go to the modiste to find a new dress for the occasion.

The shop was quaint with only one wide display window set within the storefront. A hand-painted sign reading “Madam Lilia Calderu” in yellow cursive font was hung with a ribbon within the glass. It was empty, it usually was, her fashions nowhere near as desired as those of other dress makers on account of her Italian influence. Many critiqued that her designs were too imposing and the waistlines far too structured for the delicate forms of their daughters; a signature that Rio had come to prefer. 

“Miss Vidal,” Madam Calderu welcomed from her counter, “to what do I owe the visit?”

She was a shorter woman with pronounced, exaggerated features, her face lined with age, and thick grey hair that twisted into itself in spirals. She dressed in bright colors of yellow and gold, and her fingers were always adorned with thick, paste stone rings. Rio paid her little mind, speaking over her shoulder and perusing the displays of fabrics that lined the shop's main wall. 

“I’m sure you know why I am here, Lilia. It is all anyone has been talking about.” Her voice was deep with displeasure. 

“Hmm, the masquerade then.”

“Yes.”

“I see,” Lilia said in near-sardonic observation.

This made Rio turn in curiosity, pivoting on her toes in a strange twist that crooked her chin behind her shoulder like that of a barn owl. The display was an assault on her posture and twisted her brown half-dress into a display of unflattering folds.

The act made Lilia jump within her skin. 

“What? No congratulations? No well wishes for my impending engagement?”

“Ha! I'd rather keep my tongue,” Madam Calderu commented with a laugh.

“Oh, Lilia,” Rio pouted as though bored, though truly it was the first real conversation she had had in days, “I'm not that awful.” 

“We both know that you are,” she replied.

“If I didn’t know better, I’d say it was not your taste that keeps this place empty, but your personality,” Rio added with a shrug. 

Madam Calderu merely waved her hands in dismissal before going back to her sewing, preferring not to interfere with Rio’s searching until called upon. 

The old woman had always amused Rio. She found her to be superstitious and reactive. Not shrill, but sharp-witted. Agatha had once said Madam Calderu’s job as a modiste was only half the story, that her real money came from reading the fortunes of gullible men and women who were too fascinated by the occult to realize they were being scammed. She believed it. Though, as her mind floated along the overwhelming aroma of Lilia’s tea leaves, she may have also believed Madam Calderu’s scams were not entirely dishonest. 

Rio’s fingers trailed along the endless hypnotizing spindles of threads and lace and pearlescent beads, but she passed through the colorful displays without an ounce of inspiration. They all seemed to mock her in their refinement or their richness or their playful patterns. The florals and the filigree were no better than labyrinths set to imprison her; the embroidery, the engraving of her coffin.

Instead, Rio narrowed her attention on a myriad of black fabrics, their gloom hidden away in the far corner of the shop like ghosts beneath a stairway. She crouched down with a slowness, half expecting to see the glow of yellow eyes peering back at her, but was met only with matte cloth. She pulled along its edge and stared into its darkness, stale and heavy as it absorbed all the light that it touched. She reached for another spool behind it, entranced by the movement within its weaving, a misalignment that gave the illusion of scales writhing through thick brush. She unspooled another, sheer and lightweight. She stared in wonderment as her fingers seemed to disappear behind the soft tulle as though caught within a plume of smoke and ash.

Madam Calderu peered across the shop in curiosity, “Those are old mourning fabrics, dear.”

Rio continued to palm through their dreariness as though they were as rich as the night sky reflected in pools of moonlight. Their blackness sparked in her a devious plan, a way to regain herself once more. 

“They’re perfect,” she said. “Absolutely perfect. I want you to fashion them into a ball gown for me. A collection of them. Is there enough material here?”

Madam Calderu stumbled on her words, “I- I suppose there is. But Miss Vidal, that is very unconventional, and the fabric, it is quite tedious and not designed for the complexities that a formal evening gown would generally require.”

“But you can do it,” Rio slinked back over to the counter with a daring, manipulative flash behind her eyes. “I mean, surely a woman of your talents is not limited by something so dreadfully boring as societal conventions, especially for something so extraordinary and limitless as a masquerade?”

Madam Caledu scoffed at Rio’s performance, “And what will your mother think?”

“My mother,” Rio’s voice groaned, “is lucky I do not drown myself before her little party. What I choose to wear is hardly the least of her concerns.”

Madam Calderu chuckled at this, “You will look as though you are attending your own funeral.”

“Oh, Lilia, you shame me to suggest I am so unimaginative and vengeful.” Rio feigned offense with a delicate hand to her chest. “No,” she continued, her words floating within a dangerous air, stating her request like it were a destiny. “I want you to make me the image of Death herself.”

Chapter 4: Take Your Fill, Let the Spectacle Astound You

The night of the first masquerade, the moon was merely a crescent with its face stained yellow. It appeared to lean in with a closeness, an ear pressed to the ground, as though it were eavesdropping through the windows and terraces and wide open doors of the Vidal House. 

Rio sat at her dressing table, her eyes shut in resignation as the wind began to carry the sound of the musician’s tuning through her windows, a part of her wishing that it would instead bring Agatha. 

A useless thought, spurred by habit and nothing else. 

She shook any lingering regret from her mind, dousing it with a spite-filled indifference, and opened her eyes to face herself. 

In the mirror, Rio saw eyelids painted dark and her olive skin left bare. Her raven hair was pinned off her shoulders in intricate braids behind her head, held in place with clasps of silver that left only a few framing twists free along her face. Around her neck was a set of black pearls. They fell in tiered waves, cascading into a single, lone teardrop pendant that hung like a lost star at her collarbone. Her neckline and shoulders were elegantly exposed; her bust detailed with an overlay of fabric embroidered in silver and nightly greys. They appeared to weave like the veins that bled from a blackened heart, down between her ribs, and through the centerline of her dress. Her arms were covered past her elbows in long, dark gloves, and on her fingers, she wore three rings: one on her thumb, one on her pointer, and one on her middle. They rested just past her first knuckle, and the metal curved into talons set within smoky stone, as though their sharpness could cut a mortal thread. 

“It is time, ma’am,” Dotty called from the door frame. 

Rio took a deep breath and adorned the final pieces of her costume. 

The first was a shawl that doubled as a hood, made of embroidered silk. Its threads were woven into invisible patterns that gave the illusion of a forest canopy, a silhouette of shadow beneath a moon that shrouded her face and poured exquisitely to rest within the crux of her arm. 

The second, and the last, was her mask.

It was painted white, its colour purer than ivory bone. Simple and elegant. The top half of a human skull, set with an eerie depth so that her nose was half-hidden by a false nasal ridge and her eyes nearly disappeared within the dark pools of the sockets. Their glassiness caught in terrifying reflections and flashed across the light. 

She tied the disguise in place with velvet ribbon and let her mind drift away with the creation of her new form. 

Lady Death. 

Rio’s spine straightened, and her heart pounded at the image of herself. She had believed she was beautiful, but tonight, she felt otherworldly within Madam Calderu’s creation. The satisfaction of it caught within her like a strange poison–a dissonance–that there may be pleasure within her end.

Lady Vidal waited for her daughter along the steps of the front hall. She was dressed in draping fabrics of white and gold, and her face was concealed in a mask of fresh flowers and laurels in the image of Demeter. 

“Good evening, Mother,” Rio called to her in a dark echo across the growing sound of guests and stringed melodies. 

“Victoria!” She gasped.

Rio descended the stairs in a slow, methodical creep. The train of her gown and shawl concealed her steps with a spectral quality, the soft tulle and silk slipping past itself like a mist along a dark sea.

She stretched her arms in display, “Do you like it?” 

“It is quite…” Lady Vidal swallowed as she looked for the words, “...breathtaking.” 

Rio laughed at this with a self-indulgent swish of her train, “I found it to be quite fitting for the occasion.”

“And so it is.” Lady Vidal smiled, taking her arm. “You look beautiful, my dear. Absolutely beautiful.”

Her mother’s compliments struck with an unexpected warmth that flushed beneath her skull as she let herself be led to the floor. 

The Vidal House glowed in endless white candles that had been lit along bronze sconces and crystal chandeliers. It sang with music and conversation and the pitting of soft steps along painted wood as men and women danced and laughed at its center, exchanging partners through the spins and twists of a quadrille. 

Rio had attended plenty of balls, but she was taken aback by how the guests seemed so uninhibited, as though loosened beneath their disguises. Even the new debutantes seemed to glow with a confidence outside of their chaperones' guidance. 

Her mother seemed to notice her daughter’s staring as they took their first turn about the room.

“Masquerades had gone a bit out of fashion quite a few years ago. The court believed it incited too much revelry and enabled horrible entanglements,” she paused, lowering her voice to whisper into her daughter’s ear, “They were right of course. But I say, why deny ourselves the pleasure? Everyone knows the things we try to push down have a way of rearing their ugly heads in more unseemly ways. Now, if you'll allow, I would like to discuss a few rules with you, as you refused me to do so this past week.” 

Rio did not speak.

Lady Vidal continued, “Everyone who has been invited here is under the suggestion that no one, and I mean, no one, is to reveal themselves or their identity unless betrothed. There shall be no discussion of family titles, inheritance, or anything that would reveal as such.”

Rio objected, though her interruption carried more wonderment than annoyance, “That is absurd, surely people will recognize each other-”

“If they do, they are not to say it,” Lady Vidal delighted in a grinning whisper, “This is a game, Victoria, just as much as it is practical. Let us have our fun.”

Rio stopped in her tracks.

“Our fun?” she questioned. 

Lady Vidal turned to face her, the confidence of her scheme on full display as her voice lowered in a rare show of discipline.

“I have let you have your sulking, but do not tell me you are not at least somewhat intrigued now that you are here. You may lie to yourself, but not to me.”

Her mother’s gaze bore into Rio with an accusation that seemed deeper than her attendance alone. The emotion looked strange within her eyes, as though a secret would slip from them at any moment. It unsettled Rio, like a wet rag pressed into her back. A startling reminder that, despite her mother’s talent at turning a blind eye, she was still capable of seeing. 

She threw herself from the feeling, unwilling to give her mother such depth of credit. 

“Let us take another turn about the room then,” Rio replied with a begrudging humor. “Perhaps I can find a suitable drink at the very least.”

Lady Vidal smiled knowingly, repositioning herself along her daughter’s side with a tight squeeze to her bicep as she praised the decision. 

“Very good.”

Rio and her mother fancied themselves in champagne and the conversation of the guests and their costumes. To Rio’s surprise, she did not attempt to pass her to any suitor, nor did she make any remarks about the men aside from shared ridicule of their efforts at disguise. Together, they must have counted at least six of the same domino cloak and several who were merely sporting their soldiers’ uniforms or usual finery with plain, colorless masks. 

The shift was small, so small that most people would not have noticed. A drop through the room that rippled in the small breakings of lively conversations into whispers, the turns of people’s heads, the slight misstep of a cotillion. Rio found herself caught in it, her eyes catching slightly like a glare along a windowpane just beyond her peripheral.

Her mother, who had the clearer view, seemed stunned in her own witness, her voice dropping in a salaciousness as she pointedly tapped her daughter’s shoulder and redirected her attention beyond the room.

“Oh my, Victoria, isn’t that one pretty.”

There, across the floor, a man had approached a collection of gentlemen, laughing and boasting in conversation with a casual air. But there was nothing casual about him. 

From behind, the man’s long hair was tied into a soldier’s ribbon, and he appeared to shimmer in a stylized, navy blue tailcoat, adorned with small crystals and threaded with enchanting patterns of luminescent, scrolling symbols that seemed of another time and place entirely. He moved with an air like silk through the night, and as Rio continued to watch him, his disguise seemed to reflect stranger and stranger fashion. His shirt was undone, billowing so that the white of his collar exposed the sharpness of his collarbone and an odd brooch necklace. It bordered on improper, but somehow felt justified in the strength of his proportions. It was tucked into a vest of glittering details that was altogether more similar in construction to that of a woman’s corset, and its boning created a slight definition along his torso. When he spoke, his hands were animated and enchanting, his fingers long and decorated in rings of blue and gold. And when he laughed, Rio stared at the makings of his mask–a veiled, blind mask that covered his eyes with the illusion of a bird's wings stretched across his gaze.

Rio couldn’t look away and, as though he knew she was watching, he turned and smiled at her, a quick wink of a smile, before returning to his conversation as though he were completely innocent of the way it had set her nerves to writhe.

“Who is that man?” Rio questioned without thinking.

Lady Vidal’s eyes flashed with a delighted smugness at her daughter’s interest.

“That is not the question you should be asking,” she replied.

“I-” Rio stammered over herself, arguing in silence with the infuriating bounds of her mother’s game and letting out a petulant huff. “Fine.”

She poured the rest of her champagne down her throat in an impolite display and pressed the empty flute into her mother’s chest. Without statement or dismissal, Rio readjusted the ends of her ghostly shawl back within the crooks of her elbows and proceeded to try and get a closer look, her curiosity overshadowing any potential commentary from her mother. 

Rio stalked delicately across the floor, keeping her distance along the wall and navigating between the briefest of conversations as she passed, saying frivolous things like “Your dress looks positively divine” and “Have you tried the lemon tarts?” But for all her efforts, it seemed the man slipped farther and farther away in the crowd. 

Desperate to cross the floor, she caught the attention of a young, pimpled man dressed in a putrid harlequin vest and mask. “Should you like to dance?”

He seemed stunned by the attention but took her hand nonetheless, as they were pulled into a dizzying gallopade. Rio let her feet move at a clumsy pace, unaided by the horrid leadership of her dance partner, setting her head to a swivel as she tried to relocate the mysterious gentleman. Still, the man had seemed to disappear altogether. When the music finally rested, she rushed her curtsey and left the poor boy in disbelief, not deigning herself to look again in his direction. 

She was at a loss, and frankly, a little inebriated, a fact that had become more apparent as she tried to steady her stomach from the spinning. 

With an agitated muttering, Rio grabbed another glass of champagne and saw herself to the back terrace, where she could at least curse more loudly and let herself get properly drunk without the witness of her mother. 

The moon hung much higher now, and Rio could not stop herself from staring at its mocking smile as she sipped the tart liquid from her glass. 

It served her right, she thought, to take interest in anything that this cursed party would present to her. She would be better off finding an old widower and be done with it. It was not that unusual for them to die so soon after the wedding. She could make it look like an accident…

“It is improper for an unmarried woman to drink at a social event, is it not?” A low voice sounded from behind her with a grit like honey and a roundness as though the speaker’s mouth were trying to hold a flower between their teeth. 

It was not an unpleasant sound; in truth, it held a nice vibration, but Rio rolled her eyes anyway, acting on her impulsive disdain without a second of consideration as she spun on her heels. 

“Well then, it is good I am not a wom...” 

Rio’s mouth was suddenly caught open at the sight of a white feathered mask.

“It is a shame,” the gentleman said with a smile. “You would have made quite an exquisite one.”

There was something strangely familiar about his manner of speaking, like something from a dream or a distant memory, but she couldn't place it. Her eyes quickly scanned the lower half of his face, and, up close, she could see that the man was clean-shaven and had a strong, masculine jaw that skewed ever so slightly in alignment when he spoke. His teeth were in good condition, and his lips, well, his lips…

Rio stammered backward as if offended by her own path of thinking, “Who are you?”

“That, I believe,” he continued to tease, “is strictly off limits. I will let you get away with your champagne, but I would surely not dishonor our host.”

Rio knew her mind had failed her before the man had stopped speaking, her body still trying to catch up from its own infuriating instinct. It left her mouth dry, and her stomach twisting, and all the while, she did not know to fight or to run. She wanted to rip the mask from his face just as soon as she wanted to hide hers. 

It was that she could not make sense of it that frightened her just as much as it intrigued her; that she had never felt like that for any man she had ever seen. 

If he even was a man. 

At present, he felt more like a creature. An angel. 

“Is everything alright?” the man asked. 

Rio inhaled, “Forgive me, I…”

“Let us get you inside. You must be struck with chill.” 

The man held out his arm, and Rio found herself taking it without question, subdued within her own sickening astonishment. 

Her mother must have seen them come in, for as fast as she re-entered the room, she heard her voice approaching steadily. 

“Victoria, there you are…”

“You’ll have to excuse me,” the gentleman said. “I am not good with mothers.” He released her arm and made an elegant show of bowing and pressing his lips along the back of her hand. “It has been lovely to make your acquaintance.”

Rio’s hand stayed frozen in the air, still balancing within the ghost of his palm long after he had left.

Chapter 5: True is False, Who is Who?

Rio paced restlessly at the front door of The Davis House, smoothing the creases repeatedly of her olive green dress and picking at the threads along the seams of her wrist-length gloves. 

She debated leaving, forgetting the whole thing, and keeping her pride intact. But her mind was still racing with childish embarrassment that only the distraction of anger might save her from. 

It was all she could do to stop thinking about herself. 

How foolish must she have looked? Standing there, in the wake of that man’s exit, flushed and trembling as docile as a kitten. And, oh, how her mother must have delighted in it, she was certain. She was sure it was all that she would be speaking of over breakfast, a meal Rio had decided she would now be completely avoiding for the foreseeable future. And where was Agatha? Certainly, none of this would have happened if that woman hadn’t practically abandoned her.

After a torturous time, which was in no way shorter nor longer than any reasonable time for a knock to be answered, a woman in a white apron came to answer the door. Rio believed her name was Sarah, or maybe it was Sophie. A young, far too jovial face, blocking her way. 

“Good morning, Miss Vidal,” she welcomed pleasantly. “Were we to be expecting you?”

Rio replied with growing impatience, “I am here to see Agatha. It is no concern of yours. We will be gone shortly.”

“I- well- oh dear,” Sarah stammered, clearly at a loss of what to do, “I am afraid she may be indecent. Let me wake her."

Rio caught the door with her palm. The sound made the poor thing jump. 

“You and I both know that you do not want to do that,” Rio stated with an intimidating drag of annoyance, “Just as well as you and I both know that Mrs Davis will not mind me being here unannounced, nor of Agatha leaving with me. Now, please, if you could.”

The woman sighed; whether it was relief or resignation, it did not matter to Rio. She stepped aside and left Rio to her business. 

The Davis House was a smaller house than the Vidal’s, but a proper house all the same. It was kept tidy and neat with a theme of cream and marble and white trim that seemed to smile like teeth along the walls. She followed it along a corridor until it was swallowed by an ostentatious staircase that ascended a gaudy mural of cherubs.   

Agatha once told Rio she started climbing out of windows just to avoid their chubby faces.

Rio pulled up her skirts and moved quickly past them, taking a right along the second story and following a darkened hall until she hit Agatha’s doorway. She took a breath in and twisted the handle. 

The room itself was longer than it was wide, with a brass twin bed tucked against the farthest corner beside a parapet window and the air stale with the smell of dried lavender and spruce that was bound with a piece of tattered twine and hung from a hook above a stool that held a chipped porcelain wash basin and pitcher. Rio could see the outline of Agatha covered beneath a patchy white quilt. It was held at a silly tension by her arms, attempting to shield her face from the light, the shape of it not quite long enough to cover the ends of her bare feet, which now poked out and seemed to twitch like a dog caught in a dream. 

Despite all of Rio’s impatient action, she found herself pausing at the foot of the bed and staring down at the ridiculous sight of her. 

It had been twelve days since that same weight had snuck into her bed. 

With a punishing impulse, Rio bent down and pinched one of Agatha’s toes. 

“Agh!” Agatha gawked, springing up in reflex and fighting to free herself from the tangle of her bedsheet. It pulled over her hair in a mess of static as she slammed the sheet into her lap, leaving her bare chest exposed to the air in a prickling of pale, rounded flesh. 

Rio’s jaw clenched at the sight of her, her fists clammy and brutal within the detail of her gloves. 

Agatha’s eyes narrowed as she pushed the wild hair back from her face to try and process the figure in front of her. When she spoke, her throat hitched along the sleep still caught within her throat. 

“My God, Rio, how long have you been there?!”

“You have some nerve,” Rio started with a laugh. 

Agatha clenched the sheet back up to her chest like a shield, shifting to her knees in the process, and stretching her free arm out as though she were trying to calm a wild stallion.

“Rio, Rio look- I can explain,” she chanted. 

“You know,” Rio continued, stalking up to her bed, her voice low against Agatha’s pathetic display of remorse. “I thought it best after you left that night to see to it that I would never speak to you again.” Rio lunged to slam her hands on Agatha’s mattress, the woman slinking back against her rising volume. “But I did not spend nearly eight years tolerating your incessant invasions of my life to have you disappear now. If I have to suffer this season, you will suffer it with me.” 

Rio punctuated the message by ripping Agatha’s pillow out from behind her and slamming it at her head with her next command. “Now get up. We are going to promenade.”

Agatha’s eyes strained in confusion, and she pulled her pillow back in a slow, disarming fashion that Rio thought absolutely ridiculous. 

“We are going to what?” Agatha said in disbelief.

“Promenade,” Rio restated in annoyance. “Along the park.” 

Agatha’s face twisted into a slow, burning wave of recognition across her eyebrows, her breath catching in a tsk. 

“Oh, I see,” Agatha laughed to herself in a deep, sarcastic understanding. “God, Rio. I would’ve thought you a little harder to catch. One night and you’re already stooping so low as to promenade. Must have been some gentleman.”

“And what do you know of gentlemen?” Rio snapped, her arms crossing in defence. 

“I know…” Agatha appeared to think, “...my share.”

Rio scoffed at this. “You are awful, Agatha.”

“I cannot be so awful if you are seeking my assistance.”

“This is merely a reflection of my own horrible desperation. I would no sooner seek your help with men than I would claim you to be one.”

Agatha didn’t reply and Rio refused to look again in her direction. 

In fact, she was trying to look at anything else. Unable to stand the sight of her smugness or the sound of the click of her tongue, while Rio’s blood continued to boil within the mere suggestion that Agatha had entertained men. Of course, she knew this. She must have known this. Or else why would she have come here. 

It surely wasn’t because she missed her company. 

“Can you please just get dressed?” Rio groaned.

“Fine,” Agatha replied, finally climbing from her bed, “but I hope you know I am billing you for my time.”

 

The Vahnhallen Gardens were the pinnacle of afternoon leisure within the city. Their grounds wove between duck ponds and fountains and displays of botanicals imported from all over the world, and its vast stretches of plush clover cradled the blankets and tents of patrons that dotted the greenery in broad patches of color like the butterflies that lounged along their flowers on a warm afternoon. 

Agatha and Rio had tucked themselves away behind a large rhododendron. Rio in green and Agatha in blue. Of course, Rio’s dress was tailored and well-fitting, and a shade that was enviable even to the trees, while Agatha’s was pale in age and held to her body with her usual half corset that was laced about as well as her hair was tied. 

Rio had to stop herself from staring as the strands continued to slip, perpetually spilling down the back of Agatha’s neck and in messy strands in front of her eyes.

Always a distraction.

“Why are you so sure you will recognize this man out of costume?” Agatha asked, slouching back on one of her shoulders and tossing an apple absent-mindedly with her free hand. 

Rio sat nearly shoulder to shoulder with her along a quilt, and from their position, they watched as strings of people strolled beneath the afternoon sun in and out of the main gates. The sound of children and laughter and bird song and the occasional yipping of dogs or the recital of a lyre filled the breeze between them and lifted the tails of kites across their periphery. 

“I told you, Agatha,” Rio replied. “He was different. I am sure I would spot him in any crowd just by the cut of his jacket or the way that he stood. There would surely be no way to miss him.”

“Yes, yes, you keep saying ‘different’, but different how? How different could any man be from any other in society?”

“He had this look,” Rio went on with a flourish of her hands. “Even my mother said it. There was a prettiness about him. Not just in his clothes, but in the way he carried himself. He was mesmerizing. Beautiful even.”

“Hmph,” Agatha huffed with a roll of her eyes. “Surely, no man is beautiful.”

“He was. I am telling you he was. I cannot explain it to you, because you were not there.”

“And what did he do? This pretty gentleman?” Agatha repeated the word back like an insult, taking a bite of her apple and mumbling back with full, annoyed cheeks. “You cannot tell me you are this worked up, merely over some sort of odd attraction.”

Rio spared an annoyed glance in the direction of her chewing, “You could stand to be more polite, Agatha. We are in public.” 

Agatha didn’t acknowledge it; instead, she proceeded to take another bite and spat out a seed onto the ground in front of them. 

Rio could’ve strangled her if it wouldn’t have attracted so much attention. 

“He was polite, proper,” Rio found herself saying, “and good humored. I watched him, from a distance at first. Everyone he approached, it was as though he held the room in his palm. He smiled at me, you know. From across the room. I thought I would try to introduce myself, but he kept slipping away into the crowd.”

“Perhaps he was avoiding you,” Agatha muttered, throwing the apple core into a bush and wiping her hands across her dress. 

“You would surely know, wouldn’t you?” Rio accused. “But that is unimportant, because he found me drinking on the terrace. I truly cannot explain it. Being alone with him there, if only for a few words. He felt so familiar and at the same time like no one I had ever encountered, and yet he refused to reveal himself. Claimed it would be dishonorable to my mother’s wishes. Can you imagine? I need to find out who he is, Agatha, or I fear the mystery of it will drive me to foolishness. It practically already has.”

“And what if he is no one?” Agatha asked. “What if he is just as ordinary as anyone?”

“And what if he is exceptional?” Rio responded, “What if he is the answer to all of this trouble?”

“You have said it yourself, you have barely spoken to the man. And now he is what? Your future husband?”

“Perhaps he is,” Rio exhaled, bringing her fingers to pick at her eyebrow. “I can think of worse fates.”

Agatha seemed to think about it. Her lips pulled tightly into her teeth as she cocked her head and looked. No, she didn't look; she searched. Her eyes flicked between Rio’s face and some far-off thought. 

Rio hated when she looked at her like that. It always seemed to erase her of her own logic. 

“We are wasting our time,” Agatha said dismissively. “As far as I am concerned, the only place you are going to find him is back within the guise of the masquerade.”

“And what do you suppose I am to do until then?” 

“Call on a dance tutor,” Agatha dismissed, “I’m sure you could use it.”

Chapter 6: Paper Faces on Parade

A fortnight had passed.

Agatha was no true help. Not in the matters of identification nor pursuit, and it was merely a few days before the topic had left them all together. Perhaps it was a mercy, Rio thought, that she might leave it and attempt to return to herself before the inevitable reunion with the stranger. That they might return to themselves. 

Though in all that time, Agatha never once snuck back into Rio’s bed, nor did they ride together to her father’s cottage. Rather, their afternoons seemed caught with small, passing engagements: a hand of cards, a trip to the market, a spotty conversation within the stable yards. It was unusually calm, for them, and devoid of shameless tricks or petty theft or drunken stupors. The unspoken difference thickened with a numbing cruelty as the time grew closer to the second night with Rio no nearer to understanding the truth behind her mysterious gentleman. As the night descended, Rio locked herself in the library, surrounded by the dim light of candles scattered across the dark wooden tables while she searched among her father's possessions for any documentation, letter, or directory that may have still held information of the Harkness Estate. Hoping, perhaps, that she could find him. 

But after hours of effort, she came up with nothing. Not a single association. 

There was no sense in continuing, but Rio’s restlessness had made her desperate, and the thought of sleep felt like a submission she was unwilling to offer. 

So while the Vidal House fell deeper into its slumber, Rio gathered her riding cloak and boots and set herself to the only place that may offer her solace.

Chapter 15: But Who Can Name the Face?

The moon was high, the brightness of its waxing scattered behind the ominous borders of grey that climbed against the wind. She pushed forward beneath its shadow and rode in careful steps along the main road until it turned to silt and mud and the oil flickered and fought in the distant lamp light of the city walls. 

It came on slowly, the rain, in small, starless sprinkles that blew into Rio’s face and caught in the grey mane of her companion while the distant crack of thunder rolled beyond the trees. Rio reached to stroke the drops across the hair.

“Just another mile,” she said.

With a tap of her heels, Rio pressed her horse to race the building sky. The rain fell harder with each passing minute. It trailed down Rio’s coat and skirts and seeped in webbed darkened patterns between the growing flecks of mud kicked from the road. Her thoughts were silent; her heart pounding in search of the road that turned to her father's familiar gates until they were forced to slow again against the inevitable downpour. Rio dismounted and led them blindly through its curtains, her eyes seeking iron in flashes of violent light. 

The sky split again, and their destination fell into view. She worked with haste, pushing against groaning metal and guiding them across the final stretch of road. 

In her exhaustion, Rio did not hear the slight, unsettled steps that echoed with the rain across the long row of stable doors. Instead, she worked diligently, half awake in her relief, to see to her horse’s comfort before braving the storm again in the final stretch to the main house. 

She took a deep breath and wrapped herself in the soaked fabrics of her coat as the heavy door swung open in a fit against the storm. Her boots slid against weakened, wet ground and the rain dragged her loosened hair across her vision in terrible effort. It was a wonder she could latch the door at all. 

She marched onward. Her gait faltered between each step, tripping in the heavy layers of her skirts.  Each act was harder than the last as her frustration culminated in the proximity. 

The silhouette of the cottage seemed to rock within the dark, the stone harsh and rigid in flashes of lightning. 

And then a door swung across the siding, a warm glow emanating behind the outline of a woman in Rio’s strained vision.

“Are you mad?!” Agatha’s voice sounded. 

Rio froze in her tracks, her figure shrunken in the continued downpour. 

“No. NO!” She cried, her foot stomping into the dirt like that of a petulant child, “You're not supposed to be here!” 

“And neither are you!” Agatha cried, “Get inside!” 

“I do not wish to see you!” Rio yelled back. 

Agatha groaned from beyond the porch, before setting a path towards Rio, “Close your eyes then for all I care, but you're not staying out here!” 

Before Rio could grasp the speed with which Agatha moved, she was knocked off balance and thrown over her shoulder.  

“Agatha! AGATHA! Put me down!” She cried and kicked against her.

On command, Agatha dropped Rio across the threshold. Her body slapped onto the wood with the effect of a wet rag. 

“There,” Agatha said, wringing her now soaked hair casually within the entry way , “Are you happy?”

“No,” Rio spat, “I am absolutely not happy. What on earth are you doing here?” 

“I could ask you the same question.”

“This is my father’s property, Agatha.” 

Agatha dismissed the comment and stepped over Rio’s body with an absentminded groan. 

Rio collected herself from the ground in an agitated huff, dripping along the floor as she followed Agatha further into the cottage. The main room was dark except for a few candles lit along a low table that held a small, precarious stack of books overturned along their spines in various states of completion. 

Rio glanced between them and back to Agatha in curiosity. 

“So you do read,” she commented. 

“I guess I do.”

“You could have just said that.”

“Wouldn’t want to ruin my reputation, now would I?”

Rio smirked, “I hardly doubt literacy would make a dent in your reputation.”

“Maybe not.” 

Lightning sparked beyond the windows, casting the sky in purple with a thunder that no longer came while the cottage fell back into the loud silence of the storm as it whipped against the house in blankets of heavy rain. Rio kept her distance, observing the woman in front of her as she moved to light a fire within the hearth. 

The small flame gathered in warm pools along her skin as it grew, reflecting along the moisture like the sun catches within a dew drop. She could not help but follow the trailing streams, the way they danced along her neck and fell beneath her collar.

Rio took a deep breath. 

“Why are you here, Agatha?”

Agatha sighed, “I needed somewhere to be alone. This place felt as good a place as any. I promise, if I would have thought you would come, I would have avoided it entirely.”

“The feeling is mutual, I assure you.”

“Yes, you have made that quite clear.”

Agatha’s tone seemed almost humorous, lacking in the sharpness Rio would have expected. And within the space that had been their escape for so many years, the very hint of the jest was completely disarming, endearing even.

Rio cursed herself for it, but she couldn’t bring herself to move. 

Agatha’s eyes remained fixed on the fire as she swung her dampened hair across her shoulder and began to unlace the strings of her corset. Her dress squelched onto the floor as it fell free from her waist. Rio’s breath hitched in the sight of her, unable to look away from the way her chemise clung to her bare skin, the cotton nearly translucent with rain. 

It was hypnotizing. 

It was agonizing. 

And as the woman in front of her seemed poised to remove the very last shred of fabric between them, Rio could no longer remember why she should cast her eyes away.

Rio cleared her throat of it, “Well, if you’ll excuse me, I have business to attend to.”

Agatha rolled her eyes, “Business? It is the middle of the night and you’re drenched.”

“I hardly see why that is relevant.”

“Just sit down, Rio.” Agatha patted the ground beside her. “Unless, you don’t think you can control yourself.”

Rio crossed her arms, staring down at the boldness of Agatha’s challenge as her gaze held her in complete anticipation. 

She would not give Agatha the satisfaction of her retreat.

She shrugged the wet coat from shoulders in a decisive play, letting it meet the ground without care as it folded under the weight of its own puddle. 

Agatha’s jaw tightened, her smirk faltering ever so slightly as her eyes followed the action. 

Rio pulled the soaked remains of her dress over her head, before her hands tore through the laces of her corset and petticoats in unceremonious display until she was left in nothing but the single layer of thin, plain cotton.

The lightning flashed again across the windows, the sudden brightness illuminating Agatha’s blue eyes with a wild glare that seemed to strip Rio further of her pride. 

Rio flicked the wet strands of hair awkwardly from her face and neck and sat herself down beside her. 

For a moment, the pair sat in the sound of rain and the soft crackling of the fire. Rio stretched her body toward the familiar warmth, her joints sore and aching as they sunk into the feeling.  

Despite her unwillingness to address it, she could not deny the way she softened in the ritual. 

It was a habit that could not be broken. 

“Why are you here tonight, Rio?” Agatha asked.

“I couldn't sleep.” 

“Mmm,” Agatha hummed, as if she knew it were only a partial truth. 

“Mrs Davis is worried about you.”

“She will be alright.”

“Will you?”

“I’ve managed just fine before.” 

Before, Rio thought. 

There had never been any space for before between them. 

But had she ever cared? Had she ever spared a thought?

Agatha had never mentioned her past, and Rio thought that she had been respectful in her persistent avoidance of it. But sitting there, she could not escape the fact that perhaps, in her own prejudice, Rio had never thought there was anything of merit behind it. 

But Agatha had always been more, whether Rio faced it or not, Agatha possessed more than she had ever known in another person. 

Before she had met the suitor. 

But she could no longer lie to herself, that the suitor, for all his qualities, had been merely a mirror to what she could not hold in her vision of Agatha.

It had always been Agatha. 

And now, a piece of her wished that it had been.

That the person she was so desperate to find was…

“Agatha?” She found herself asking.

“Yes?”

“What was your life before?”

“Rio…” 

“I know. I know you do not talk of it. But I feel… god, Agatha, it's been nearly a decade and sometimes I feel as though I barely know you, that you are this abstraction that I- that perhaps I have- I have not been the kindest in my assumptions of you.”

“I certainly haven't stuck around you all these years because of your kindness. In fact, part of the thrill is that I always thought you were going to kill me one day.”

Rio could not help but laugh, “Well, do not speak too soon.”

Agatha smiled briefly over her shoulder before returning to the fire. Rio said nothing else, waiting for Agatha to decide how the conversation continued. 

“When I was a little girl,” Agatha began, “I would sneak out of my room at night, and sleep in the stable with the hounds and the horses so I did not have to listen to my mother’s screaming. She would have these fits, these nights where she would tear through the home and break glasses and plates and old vases against the walls screaming profanities I did not understand. I did everything I could to make it stop. I thought if I took my education seriously, if I was the perfect daughter, if I was good…” Agatha shook her head. “But it was no use. In her eyes, I had ruined her life. Everything I did was just a reminder of that. So one night, I left. I grabbed whatever I could fit in my pockets and I followed the road as far as my legs would carry me. Mrs Davis found me out there. She offered me a home, employment, a new life… She never asked me who I was and she never gave me the option of going anywhere else.” 

Rio shifted slightly at Agatha’s story as she listened, the guilt of her ignorance sinking within her stomach. 

“It is good that you left,” she said, unable to voice anything else. 

Agatha bit her lip in thought, sighing deeply, caught within something Rio could not recognize. 

“I told myself I would never be that girl again. I would never go back to a world that doesn't want me. Not fully. But now… Now I am not sure how to reconcile what I- what I thought I had to do.”

It was then that Agatha turned to look back at Rio. Her eyes were glassy in the fight against her tears. It struck Rio, how when she stared back into them, that it was not sorrow that they held, but regret. 

Rio felt the emotion of it like a woman pleading in silence, atoning for some egregious sin. 

How could Agatha look at her like that, she thought, when it was her who had committed the worse offense? How callous had she been? How shallow? How dedicated to her own privileged suffering that she would be so blind to what had been offered to her in her life? 

“Perhaps,” Rio said, “perhaps, there is a way to start over, for you to make something for yourself that is only yours. You deserve that much, Agatha.”

Agatha muttered to herself in a half-hearted laugh, “Well, in a way, I already have. I am sure Evanora Harkness would be rolling in her grave if she knew what became of her daughter.”

Evanora. 

Rio sat frozen in the passing comment, her heart lurching in her chest, her throat collapsing in on itself as she tried to wrap her mind around what she had heard. 

Evanora Harkness. 

She was suddenly overcome with visions of her suitor, of his smile, of the way his hands fit within hers, the way his locket dipped along his chest…

“It is getting late,” Agatha said, gathering herself from the floor. 

It was my mother’s.

Rio stared at Agatha as the recognition flooded her. Her skin went hot, her cheeks flushing.

“I- yes-” she stammered. 

“Are you alright?”

“Of course,” Rio replied, “Goodnight, Agatha.”

Chapter 16: Please Pretend, You Will Understand in Time

She had been a fool. 

A complete, miserable fool. 

Agatha left her unknowingly to replay every detail, every moment, every glimpse of what she had been too proud to see. 

She should have been angry. Furious.

Agatha deceived her.

She lied to her.

But she could not bring herself to feel it. 

Not for Agatha. 

But for herself, she felt the boiling irritation of her ignorance, hot and scarring within her. 

Agatha had been so emboldened to seek her proposal, to pursue what neither of them would have dared and Rio had not accepted it. 

She would not have accepted it. 

She realized now that there was no possibility she would have entertained anything between them having known the woman for as long as she had. No, Rio would not have let herself. It was a fact as plain and as shameful as she could stand to rationalize. Agatha’s was always the friendship that had been forced upon her, the nightmare that had ravaged her of any sense of dignity. She was a subjection, a nuisance…

But then she wasn’t. 

Rio could no longer lie to herself. 

In Agatha, there was no more brilliant or beautiful a companion. She provided in her a life outside of any convention or status that she may have found elsewhere. She embraced Rio, in all her pompousness and severity. And it was her that she would seek in any crowd, in any lifetime, in any person that may so much as hold a candle of resemblance or reminder of her character. 

In the dove, she found Agatha. 

Again and again and again. 

She had been transfixed, beholden to the ruse, and even more enraptured by the feeling. 

She had given all of herself just to feel it. 

And Rio could not find any fault in it. No superfluousness nor maliciousness. If Agatha had been designed to vex her, then her intentions were just as irrelevant to the reality they were both now beholden to. 

The masquerades were but a perfect opportunity, and that certainly enraged her, for her mother had orchestrated it. The anger she felt at her mother’s secret satisfaction at her success was perhaps the strongest of her immediate reactions. 

Rio stared at the fire, watching as the flames ate the wood, and she fell deeper into her resentment. 

She was a fool. 

Agatha had deceived the entire city, hidden in plain sight within the eyes of a society that had scorned her very presence for years. From the moment she arrived. And even they saw what Rio could not. 

Agatha was unconventionally charming and witty and sharp. 

And Rio loved her. 

She loved her.

Rio was sorry for it, sorry that her rejections of Agatha were so entitled, so cruel as to split her from the perceived status of her suitor. 

Of what she had thought was expected. 

She was overwhelmed by the disgust she felt in that truth. 

How could Agatha ever forgive her for it? 

How could she see her in any light other than that of the woman who rejected her for a mask? 

She had no answers, no tangible pathway for which she could repair what she had broken; all that remained now was her exhaustion and the all-consuming feeling of being close to her. 

Rio did not stop herself, or think of its potential consequences. Instead, she followed the memory, the act as plain as any other, and followed the steps to the guest bedroom. 

The rain had weakened within the windowpanes, their trails stilled in the soft light of the moon as it broke through the dissolving clouds and cast the room in a hue of blue. Agatha’s hair was splayed out along the pillows. Her pale limbs were twisted across the mattress, seemingly clutching around an invisible presence among tangled sheets. 

Rio climbed into bed next to her, making sure not to wake her. She watched her, for a while, sleep evading her through the overwhelming emotion of the evening. She noticed the subtle way her lips twitched within her slumber, how her voice hummed along sweet, broken phrases. 

A waltz.

She swept the hair that fell across her face, brushing it away with an affection she hoped was not her last. 

"Ask me again," Rio whispered, tears pooling in her eyes. "And know that I would run to any desolate corner of this world with you. Please, ask again. I would spend every moment of my life regretting what I did not see before. What I could not tell you before. That I love you, Agatha. I know now I have always loved you, and only you. If there were to be any hope for forgiveness in this world, I wish it were now, so that you could hear me when I say that you must know I am yours. That you are mine. Please, Agatha. Please, my love."

 

The next morning, Rio awoke to find Agatha had already gone. 

The absence did not surprise her, nor did the certainty with which she fell back into her depression. Except now, there was no distraction, no stranger with which she could project any happiness. 

No Agatha to save her from herself. 

It was too late. It was far too late. 

Rio remained at her father’s cottage for the next several days, unwilling to face her mother in the impending conclusion that awaited her in the last of her masquerades. 

In time, she would bear it. She would embrace the punishment of it. 

Let her stand there alone, she thought, let everyone watch how she waits for no one. Let them all see that he will not come. 

It was what she deserved. 

It was what her mother deserved most of all. 

The feeling was even more justified in the way in which her mother greeted her when she did finally return home within hours of the final judgment. 

“Victoria! What is the meaning of all of this?! Over a week, and no word. Not a single message of you or of Agatha. I truly hope for both your sakes that you have managed a solution between the two of you. The city is ravenous for any news of your engagement, and the guests will start arriving within the hour...”

“Oh, spare me, mother.” Rio responded, her tone cutting, “You believe yourself to be so clever in your design, to have only my happiness at heart, when it is clear all you think about is the opinion of society. Ah yes, let them think your daughter has been betrothed to some mysterious gentleman! How clever, truly! Perhaps next time you will be so kind as to inform me of it before I make a complete and utter fool of myself and chase away the only person I have ever held dear!”

Rio did not linger to hear Lady Vidal’s defense. She knew perfectly well that her mother’s arrangement was sound and just within the desire to prevent Rio’s ostracization or the degradation of her family’s name. Merely weeks ago she had thought the very thing, even if she did not know the full story. 

But she no longer cared. 

She would sacrifice it all if it meant even a proximal chance that Agatha would return to her. 

Rio spent the remaining hours isolated and soaking in a bath of salts that Dotty had insisted upon. It filled the air with steam and hints of rose, the pleasantness of it nauseating in her own impatience as she thought of drowning herself within the metal basin. 

She decided against it, if only for a single, childish hope that Agatha might still arrive. 

Rio dressed herself in similar fashion, her emotions oscillating between reprehensible self pity and wishful assurances that Agatha would surely not abandon her completely. 

After all, Rio thought, was there not some blame to share? Would Agatha be so incapable of the decency to reveal herself after all they had faced? 

She stirred longer around the sentiment, finding solace in the argument of Agatha’s cowardice. 

But the relief did not last long, for when she tied the velvet behind her head for the last time and looked into the mirror at the face she had hid behind all summer, she felt nothing but dread and regret. 

Below her, the Vidal House was already alight in its finale. The halls and the ballroom sang of cheerfulness and the bright echoes of laughter as the guests toed the lines of identities soon to be revealed. 

Lady Vidal had already taken to the floor, Mrs Davis in toe, positively drunk and spinning within the grand circle dance of a La Boulangère. Her mother’s preoccupation was but a small mercy as Rio slipped between the crowds with the intention of disappearing beyond the bouquets. 

She tried to ignore the reaches of droning conversations and the prying whispers that passed in the wake of her suitor’s absence on her arm. Rio grimaced at the way their fans fluttered in front of their faces as though to politely hide their vapidness. But there was no escaping the smirks or the half formed rumors. 

Yet another punishment to pile on, she thought. 

“It seems as though our dove has flown away.”

“It was quite ambitious, was it not? To agree to such a betrothal on the merit of character. I dare say, I wonder if they discovered our dove was nothing but a well coached pauper.”

“Surely not!” another woman added, “Why should the fault lie in him? We all know this orchestration was Lady Vidal’s master plan because it is Victoria who holds the more scandalous reputation. Perhaps, someone simply told him about her mistress.”

“I heard Mrs Davis’s stable girl was positively distraught by the news. Mr Grady himself said he saw her ride out of town weeks ago.”

“Oh, that is a scandal! But to deny the fortune that is inevitably set within her dowry, I do think a man would be foolish to deny it. A womanly affair is no threat to a gentleman. No, I think the wedding has already occurred and he is simply avoiding the shame of the unmasking. ” 

It was all Rio could do not to become violent, though the image of it was nothing short of vivid in her mind and she smiled to herself with a morbid satisfaction at the image of ripping the curls straight from their beaded heads. 

If this was to be her future, she thought, she would be better to claim insanity now and live among her mother’s attic as a hysteric. At least then she would be able to wail freely from the windows of her disdain with Agatha’s name cursed upon her lips. 

Rio committed herself to the fantasy of it, to the picture of her pacing the halls and the gardens in nothing but a cotton underdress and her hair grown past her hips in tangled evidence of her tragic neglect. Years from now, Agatha would return, only to find her hollow and broken and pleading with lifeless eyes for her forgiveness. 

And it would be too late then. 

She would haunt the rest of Agatha’s days in what she had become. 

If there was to be no happiness between them, then let there be only suffering. 

Rio would set herself to rot in her own skin if only to make Agatha sorry for it. 

She continued her hiding and pacing, her eyes drifting up in random intervals as though she may suddenly find her there, only to fall deeper into the melodrama of her waiting. 

At a certain point, she gave up altogether, finding herself sorely draped along the front steps of the terrace. The night drifted by her in warm currents and the shadows of splendor as they radiated from beyond the windows of the ballroom. She stared up at the sky, watching the fullness of the moon dancing in and out of shapeless clouds and yet, every time the moon shone in between those dark moments she foolishly, girlishly hoped Agatha would come to her as she had their first evening as herself and her suitor. 

It was not her voice that broke her from her dissociation, but the shrilling cheers that erupted from the Vidal House. 

The merriment seemed to rupture along the stone like a wave crashing over her, pulling her deeper to fight within its current, as the sky watched from above with a deafening indifference. She gasped for air, closing her eyes in the wreckage, and swallowing the way her tears clung to the paper of her mask in salty streams. 

She tore it from her face, crushing the pearlescent facade within her grip and throwing it onto the cobbles. The act sliced a fresh cut of red that stung along her cheek and pulled her hair from its ties in broken strands. 

She wiped the blood from her face and stared at the crimson as it caught in the metal of her rings. The joyousness continued behind her, her mother’s voice projecting across amused parties as they proclaimed their attachments and revelled in the success of their guises. 

The strings crescendoed from behind the chatter and set the crowd through a series of final dances. Their melodies bounced across the air in endless loops and high pitched phrases that seemed to cut Rio deeper with each refrain.

The crowd dispersed slowly, the guests ushered out with trivial endearments as their seemingly endless laughter disappeared within the hushed whir of carriages and the clopping of hooves along the streets. 

Rio could not bring herself to move. 

As the silence renewed, her wallowing gave way to memories that plagued her with the emotions that had been lost to her own insufferable arrogance. Her head collapsed into her hands, her eyes reddened and swollen as she tried to stop herself from crying, exhausted and nauseated by the feeling of it, when a hand reached shakily from behind her. 

She barely recognized the motion and turned apprehensively towards the sudden presence.

The rings caught her eye first, the familiar mix of metal and stone stealing her breath. 

The suitor’s jacket seemed to fall off of his shoulders; the silver and gold threads frayed along the cuffs; his shirt’s collar wide, slipping within his vest, revealing the faintest hint of what seemed to be bandages wrapped along his chest.

Rio found herself tripping over the details, her heart pounding in disbelief of the vision so weathered and shrunken as his hand stayed reaching for hers. 

She turned her head upwards, and was met with a small, apologetic smile, a strong nose, and glassy blue eyes beneath what was merely a plain, white mask, tied together with ordinary string behind a tied mess of brown hair. 

“I could not find the mask,” the suitor said, as though it were a perfectly fine explanation. 

Rio did not accept the hand. 

“I- You-” Rio attempted, gathering herself from the ground in a clumsy collection of limbs and skirts. “The festivities are over. Everyone has gone home. There is no one here but you and I. Would you really insult me to show up now and still cover your face?”

“I did not know if you would wish to see me.”

“And yet you are here.”

“Would you prefer me not be?”

“You are impossible!” Rio groaned, her anger catching up to her, “There is nothing else in this entire world that I want than for you to be here! In fact, I am convinced that there is no more sinister a torture than to be faced with your absence. And of this, I am certain, you are plenty aware, and yet, you stand here now in your disguise and you look at me… you…”

“Dearest, you are tired, let us get you inside…”

“No! No! Not until you are honest with me!” Rio challenged. 

“Alright!” The suitor shouted, pulling his hands up defensively. 

Rio stepped back in observation. Her gaze was fixed on the suitor as he took a deep breath and unrolled his shoulders. When he spoke, his voice softened into something more familiar. 

Into the person that Rio had been waiting for. 

“Alright,” he sighed, “You must believe me that it was never my intention to deceive you. In truth, the first night I had thought you had seen right through me. I thought… I had always hoped… that you may feel the same. And I… I understood that I… I was never what you might have pictured for yourself. But I have now spent sleepless nights caught between the person you would wish for and the person that I am. I betrayed myself and I betrayed you. And still, I cannot shake the feeling that if it were not for him that…”

His voice trailed off again. 

Rio could not bear it any longer; every sentence, every stammer, was just another moment that she was without her. 

“Agatha…” Rio whispered. 

The suitor’s gaze flashed between them, blue eyes searching through tears, as Rio reached gently for his face, her fingers gentle and unwinding along the strings of his mask while she spoke.  

“I cannot lie and say that there is no truth in your anxieties. I cannot pretend that I would not have gone on in denial of the very feelings that all of this has brought to light, but I care not. I can no longer care about any of it. You are the one that I want. You in all of your complete insufferabilities.”

With a blushing pause, Rio pulled the mask away and looked into the face of the woman she loved. Her hand swept to hold her cheek, and Agatha’s came to join her, pulling her deeper into an embrace. 

She closed her eyes and leaned further into the sensation.

“I love you, Agatha Harkness.”

Agatha inhaled sharply, her hands clutching along the back of Rio’s dress in clumsy holds, before muttering softly under her breath. 

“I much preferred your confession when I was asleep.”

Rio groaned, pulling the infuriating woman away from her, only to answer the sentiment by clashing her lips into hers. 

Between brushes of their lips, their tongues, Agatha hummed. 

“I love you…”

“...I love you, Rio Vidal.”

Chapter 17: Queen and Priest

Lord Henry Vidal returned to the city on the final day of August, whereupon his arrival, the final details of his daughter’s engagement were completed. 

It was a peculiar match: Lady Victoria Vidal and the lost heir to the Harkness Estate, Sir Alexander Harkness. The city was delighted with the scandal, chewing on the remnants of the Harkness family’s past while altogether embracing the return through such romantic means as the masquerades. No sooner would the gentleman have had his chance to return, and no finer a match was there for the curious Vidal daughter. 

Any doubts regarding his absence at the last ball were cast out by Lord Vidal’s own overjoyed proclamations that Alexander himself had traveled to Germany to seek permission for his daughter’s hand. Every detail was fed and filtered through to the King and Queen themselves, and the aristocracy followed suit in the splendor of such a story. 

Lady Vidal later confessed to Rio that it was just as much a surprise to both of them as it was to her that Agatha had come from such a fortuitous background. And she insisted, continuously, that Agatha had not been informed of her or her father’s plotting, that it was merely a hope that either of them would so choose to take such advantage of her arrangement. 

Rio, of course, remained outwardly infuriated by her mother’s manipulations for quite some time, even if at heart she was grateful for them. 

They were married in late October, in an intimate ceremony by a half-blind priest who was none the wiser to the fact that Alexander Harkness stood at the altar wearing a woman’s corset beneath his jacket or that the lace of the bride’s train was detailed with hidden skulls. 

At his instruction, Agatha and Rio exchanged rings and signed their names in the church’s sacramental register.
