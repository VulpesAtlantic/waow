Agatha vs. The System

by LabNotesAndFiction

Summary

Moving day arrives, and Agatha thinks she’s prepared. She is not. Her chaotic boxes collide violently with Rio’s pre-labeled closet system, leading to bickering, laughter, and the kind of terrifying tenderness that comes from realizing you’ve actually merged your life with someone else’s. There are zones. And there’s Agatha, spiraling her way toward the truth: she’s absolutely doomed.

Notes

Unpacking should be simple. But with Agatha’s chaos and Rio’s pre-labeled closet system, it turns into an all-out domestic war.

Agatha liked to believe she was good at packing. She had, after all, traveled a fair bit in her life, moving for work, chasing stories, bouncing between short-term rentals when her career took her places stability couldn’t follow. Packing was supposed to be her territory. She knew how to shove clothes into a suitcase until the zipper strained, how to nest fragile items inside sweaters, how to angle shoes so they’d take up half the space they should. In her mind, this counted as experience.

So when the day arrived to finally haul her life across town into Rio’s apartment, the place that had, for all intents and purposes, already functioned as her home for weeks, Agatha felt smug. Smug enough, at least, to show up with an armful of boxes and declare herself prepared.

She had not, in fact, prepared.

The first clue should have been the boxes themselves: a mismatched collection scavenged from liquor stores, corner markets, and the back of her own closet. Half of them sagged when lifted, their cardboard already soft from years of use. One bore a faded logo for rum, another had a greasy stain from takeout containers. She stacked them in Rio’s hallway like trophies, dust trailing behind her as if to mark the path of her chaos.

“See?” she said, brushing her hands together with a flourish. “Practically professional.”

Rio, who had been waiting by the door with the kind of calm patience that only made Agatha more nervous, tilted her head. Her gaze swept over the boxes. Slowly. Carefully.

Not once did she say good job.

The silence stretched long enough for Agatha’s smile to falter. She shifted, suddenly aware of how precariously one of the smaller boxes was leaning. “Don’t look at me like that,” she snapped, defensive too quickly. “They’re fine. They’re structurally sound.”

“They are not,” Rio said flatly.

Agatha rolled her eyes. “Okay, maybe not sound, sound. But they’ll hold. Mostly.”

As if on cue, one of the boxes let out a groan—an actual cardboard death rattle—and slumped sideways against the wall. Agatha lunged to steady it, muttering something obscene under her breath.

Rio didn’t move to help. She just watched, arms crossed, expression carved in stone.

Agatha felt heat rise in her cheeks. “This is a system, alright? Clothes are in this one—” she thumped the sagging box she had just rescued, praying it wouldn’t collapse under the weight of her knuckles—“books are here, and, uh…” Her hand hovered over a third box, one that jingled ominously whenever it shifted. “Miscellaneous.”

“Miscellaneous,” Rio repeated. Her voice was utterly neutral, but Agatha swore she heard judgment lurking underneath.

“Yes,” Agatha snapped, bristling. “Everyone has miscellaneous.”

Rio didn’t respond. She just reached out, flipped the lid on the “miscellaneous” box, and peered inside.

Agatha’s stomach dropped.

Inside was a chaotic avalanche: tangled jewelry, a half-burned candle, a bundle of pens, a deck of cards missing at least three suits, and—because fate had a cruel sense of humor—a lacy bra she’d forgotten she owned perched right on top like a crown jewel.

Rio blinked once. Slowly.

Agatha lunged forward and slammed the lid shut, nearly taking off her own fingers in the process. “Okay, first of all, no one asked you to audit my packing. And second, that’s private inventory. Sensitive material.”

“I see.” Rio’s tone hadn’t changed, but something in her eyes glinted, betraying amusement she wouldn’t put into words.

Agatha wanted to melt into the floor.

This wasn’t how it was supposed to go. In her head, she had pictured herself breezing into the apartment, dropping her boxes with casual grace, maybe making some joke about lesbians U- Hauling. Rio would roll her eyes, they’d unpack while stealing kisses between stacks of sweaters, and the whole thing would end with them collapsing onto the couch in laughter, triumphant in their merged domesticity.

Instead, it felt like she’d brought a hurricane into a library.

Her belongings looked wrong against Rio’s tidy walls. Dust smudged the floor where she’d dragged boxes inside. A sweater sleeve stuck out of one, caught in the lid, waving like a distress flag. And Rio was staring at the mess like she’d just discovered an invasive species spreading through her habitat.

Agatha straightened her coat, trying to summon dignity. “It’s fine. This is just phase one. You’ll see. I’ll unpack, everything will find its place, and it’ll be perfect.”

Rio’s eyebrow arched. Just a fraction.

Agatha’s bravado cracked. “Okay, not perfect, but functional.”

“Functional,” Rio echoed, and for the first time that day, her mouth curved. The smallest, most infuriating hint of a smile.

Agatha’s heart skipped. Because of course Rio would find her incompetence amusing. And of course that smile, small as it was, would undo every ounce of confidence Agatha had left.

She turned away too quickly, fumbling for another box, muttering as she dragged it toward the bedroom. “Don’t just stand there judging me, help me carry this before it explodes.”

Behind her, Rio said nothing. But Agatha could feel her gaze, steady, unshakable, a weight between her shoulder blades, like Rio already knew that this wasn’t just about boxes. It was about the terrifying permanence of watching her life spill into someone else’s space.

And Agatha, of course, was already spiraling.

Agatha had barely managed to drag the second box toward the bedroom before she realized something was wrong.

Not wrong in the sense of collapsing cardboard or misplaced underwear, those were her usual disasters, easily dismissed with a mutter and a smirk. This was different. This was the sense of walking into a room that had been prepared in advance, like stumbling into a stage set for a play she hadn’t auditioned for.

She froze in the doorway, box still clutched awkwardly against her chest.

The closet was open. Wide open. And inside, she saw order.

Not just order—strategy.

Every hanger was lined with precision, evenly spaced like soldiers at inspection. One section of the rail had been left bare, conspicuously empty, with brand-new hangers waiting in a neat row. The shelves above held folded stacks of sweaters, all aligned at exact angles, but a whole shelf had been cleared, scrubbed, and labeled with a discreet strip of masking tape: A.H.

Agatha blinked.

Her gaze dropped to the floor, where a row of shoe racks gleamed in quiet efficiency. Three of the racks were occupied with Rio’s clean, neatly lined shoes. The fourth, glaringly empty, had a Post-it stuck to it in Rio’s small, square handwriting: for boots.

Agatha nearly dropped the box.

It wasn’t just the closet. On the far side of the room, the dresser drawers were labeled too, subtle but unmistakable. Socks. Shirts. And then, in neat black ink: Agatha’s drawer.

Her chest constricted.

Rio had a system. Of course she did. She hadn’t just let Agatha move in. She had prepared for it, mapped it out with the same ruthless efficiency she applied to Tetris boards and puzzle grids.

Agatha’s mouth went dry. She set the box down on the floor with exaggerated care, stalling for time. “You… labeled things.” Her voice cracked halfway through the sentence.

Behind her, Rio appeared in the doorway, calm as ever. “Yes.”

“Yes,” Agatha repeated, staring at the closet like it might lunge at her. “As in—you taped my initials on a shelf. Like I’m a department in your filing system.”

Rio didn’t blink. “It prevents confusion.”

Agatha barked out a laugh, sharp and incredulous. “Prevents confusion? Sweetheart, this is a closet, not a logistics hub.”

“It is both now,” Rio said evenly, stepping past her to adjust one of the empty hangers so it aligned perfectly with the others.

Agatha swayed. She wanted to make fun of her, to mock the absurdity of alphabetized drawers and boot-designated racks. But her throat was thick with something she couldn’t name, and her eyes burned in a way she refused to acknowledge.

Because the truth was, this wasn’t absurd. It was terrifyingly deliberate.

She knelt, fumbled with the flaps of the nearest box, desperate for distraction. Clothes spilled out in an avalanche of tangled sweaters and mismatched socks. She shoved them toward the empty shelf, half hoping to disrupt Rio’s pristine system, half testing whether Rio would stop her.

Rio stopped her.

A hand—steady, warm—closed gently around her wrist. Not harsh, not reprimanding, but firm. “Not there,” she said, guiding Agatha’s arm a few inches to the left. “This shelf is better for knitwear. Less sunlight.”

Agatha stared at her like she’d grown another head. “You… planned for knitwear.”

Rio nodded, already moving to refold the sweaters with frightening speed. “Yes.”

Agatha let out a noise somewhere between a laugh and a groan, dragging a hand down her face. “You’re unbelievable.”

Rio glanced at her, eyes dark, calm. “No. Just prepared.”

And there it was again, that word. Prepared. Efficient. System.

Agatha’s chest ached. She wanted to roll her eyes, to make some crack about Rio treating her like a data set. But the longer she stood there, the harder it became to ignore what was really happening: Rio had carved out space for her. Real space. Labeled, measured, waiting.

This wasn’t tolerance. This wasn’t reluctantly shifting a few things to make room. This was an invitation.

Her throat closed.

She covered it, as always, with sharpness. “You do realize normal couples don’t map out closet space like a battle plan, right?”

Rio didn’t look up from her folding. “Normal couples are often inefficient.”

Agatha choked on a laugh. “God, you’re insufferable.”

But her voice cracked, just slightly, and she hated how obvious it sounded in the quiet.

She turned away, busying herself with another box, throwing up walls of sarcasm to keep from admitting the truth. Because the truth was unbearable: she wanted to cry, standing there in front of a shelf labeled with her initials, because no one had ever done this for her before. No one had ever made space for her life with such deliberate certainty.

And that more than the boxes, more than the chaos, was what terrified her.

It should have been simple. Clothes into closet, books onto shelves, shoes in rows. A process, nothing more.

Except nothing with them was ever just a process.

Agatha had barely survived the shock of seeing her initials taped neatly to a shelf. Her throat was still thick with unspoken things when she shoved another sweater toward the empty space, determined to reclaim some semblance of control. If she could just bury Rio’s tidy system under a wave of fabric and leather, maybe she could breathe again.

But Rio was faster. Always faster.

“Not there,” she murmured, sliding the pile out of Agatha’s arms and refolding it with surgical precision.

Agatha bristled. “What do you mean, not there?”

Rio nodded at the closet, calm as ever. “That’s the wrong side.”

“The wrong side?” Agatha repeated, voice pitching up. “It’s a shelf. Shelves don’t have sides.”

“This one does,” Rio said simply, tucking the sweaters onto the lower left section with movements so neat they looked choreographed.

Agatha gawked. “You’ve—what, divided the closet into territories? My sweaters are only allowed to live on the left?”

“Yes.” Rio didn’t look up.

Agatha sputtered, half laughter, half outrage. “Do I need a passport to visit the right side?”

Rio finally lifted her gaze, and the calm steadiness in her eyes nearly undid her. “The system works best if we respect boundaries.”

Agatha threw her hands up, pacing a few short steps across the bedroom. “Boundaries? Sweetheart, this isn’t an international border, it’s a closet! We’re not negotiating peace treaties between cardigans and coats!”

Rio, infuriatingly unbothered, went back to folding.

Agatha swore under her breath, dragging a hand through her curls. She yanked open another box, pulling out a stack of dresses she hadn’t worn in years, and shoved them onto the rail on the right side of the closet, just to make a point.

Rio was beside her in an instant. She didn’t raise her voice. She didn’t even frown. She just plucked the dresses off the hanger and moved them silently to the left.

Agatha’s jaw dropped. “Did you just—did you just correct my side of the closet?”

Rio’s voice was calm, matter-of-fact. “You put them in the formalwear section. That’s inaccurate.”

Agatha’s brain short-circuited. “Formalwear section? You’re telling me my closet has districts now? Like we’re in The Hunger Games?”

“Not districts,” Rio said. “Zones.”

Agatha nearly screamed.

She wanted to argue—oh, god, she wanted to argue—but her chest was too tight with laughter and panic all tangled together. Because this was absurd, yes, but it was also terrifying. It wasn’t just about dresses or sweaters. It was about territory, about merging, about the way her chaotic life was crashing up against Rio’s meticulous order.

She caught her reflection in the mirror, standing there, hands on hips, surrounded by boxes and half- unpacked chaos and for a split second the enormity of it hit her. Her things. In Rio’s space. Their things, soon.

Her throat clenched. She covered it with sharpness, as always.

“You know,” she said, plucking a stray hanger from Rio’s hand, “I could just throw everything into a pile on the bed and call it done. Very efficient.”

Rio blinked. “That would not be efficient.”

Agatha grinned, wicked. “Exactly.”

For the first time that day, Rio’s composure cracked. The faintest twitch of her mouth betrayed a smile she couldn’t suppress.

Agatha’s chest lurched.

She had the sudden, horrifying urge to laugh and cry and kiss her all at once. Because under all the bickering, under the absurdity of arguing over closet zones, she could feel it: this was real. This was them, colliding and clashing and making space where there hadn’t been space before.

And the truth, the unbearable truth, was that she wanted it. Even if it scared her to death.

It started with a sock.

Not just any sock, though in fairness, Agatha wasn’t sure what “just any sock” looked like in this apartment anymore, but a sock that had, apparently, escaped her packing box and was lurking under the dresser like a fugitive.

She found it mid-rant, crouched on the floor with her hand jammed behind the furniture in search of a missing shoe. Her fingers closed on something soft and stretchy, and when she tugged it free, she held up a single sock patterned with lurid cartoon martinis.

Rio blinked. Slowly. “That is not yours.”

Agatha snorted. “Excuse me? Of course it’s mine.”

Rio tilted her head, her version of disbelief. “I have never seen that before.”

“Then you clearly haven’t been paying attention, sweetheart,” Agatha said, waving the sock like evidence in a courtroom. “This has been a staple of my wardrobe for years. Very chic.”

“Chic is not the word I would use,” Rio murmured.

“Blasphemy,” Agatha declared, tossing the sock onto the bed with exaggerated flair.

And somehow, maybe it was the absurdity of defending cartoon socks like they were priceless relics, maybe it was the way Rio’s mouth twitched like she was trying not to laugh, maybe it was the pressure of the entire day threatening to crack, something inside Agatha snapped. She started laughing.

Not the sharp, biting laugh she used to mask nerves. Not the dry, sarcastic chuckle she deployed to cover spirals. Real laughter, bubbling up from her chest so fast she had to brace herself against the dresser to stay upright.

Rio watched her for a long beat, expression unreadable. Then, with the smallest, most devastating exhale, she let out a laugh of her own. Quiet at first, soft, almost like she didn’t quite trust it.

Agatha’s heart flipped.

And that was all it took. The fragile balance tipped, and suddenly they were both laughing, the sound ricocheting off the walls, filling the cramped bedroom until it felt too big to hold them.

Agatha doubled over, clutching her stomach, gasping between fits of hysterics. “God—look at us. Closet wars and contraband socks. This is what domestic bliss looks like, apparently.”

Rio shook her head, still laughing, though hers was quieter, steadier. “This is chaos.”

“Chaos,” Agatha echoed, breathless, “is half the fun.”

She collapsed backward onto the bed, arms spread wide, hair fanned out against the rumpled sheets. The sock rolled off the edge, forgotten. Her chest heaved as she tried to calm down, but every time she caught sight of Rio, standing there with one hand braced on the closet door, shoulders shaking with suppressed laughter, it started all over again.

And then, just as abruptly as it had started, the laughter softened. Faded into a quieter rhythm, the kind that leaves echoes in its wake. Agatha found herself staring up at the ceiling, smiling in spite of herself, her chest aching in a way that had nothing to do with humor.

She turned her head. Rio was watching her, eyes dark, expression open in a way that made Agatha’s throat tighten.

The silence stretched, but it wasn’t uncomfortable. It was full. Heavy with everything neither of them had said yet.

Agatha swallowed hard, suddenly aware of the mess surrounding them, half-unpacked boxes, sweaters strewn across the floor, closet zones still contested territory. It should have felt overwhelming. Disastrous. Instead, it felt… alive. Like the start of something, not the ruin of it.

Her voice came out softer than she meant it to. “You know, if this is what moving in with you looks like—arguing over socks and shelf space—I think I can live with it.”

Rio’s gaze didn’t waver. “I expected worse.”

Agatha barked out a laugh, rolling her eyes.

Rio’s mouth curved, just slightly, just enough to make Agatha’s chest squeeze.

And there it was again: the unbearable softness beneath the bickering, the tenderness that kept bleeding through no matter how much Agatha tried to armor herself with sarcasm. The realization that beneath all the systems and the spirals, this was theirs. Messy, precise, chaotic, ordered. All tangled together in a room that smelled faintly of dust and fresh laundry, laughter still hanging in the air.

The laughter didn’t last forever. It never did.

Eventually it thinned out, tapering into the kind of silence that still felt charged, like static buzzing after lightning. The sock lay abandoned on the floor, the boxes hunched in corners like sulking children, the closet stood divided but not conquered. And the two of them — sprawled on the bed in the wreckage of half-unpacked lives — were quiet.

Agatha stared at the ceiling, her chest still rising too fast, the ghost of her grin tugging at the corner of her mouth. She could hear Rio moving beside her, the soft shift of fabric as she sat on the edge of the bed, still folded into that infuriating posture of calm.

It should have felt ridiculous. The whole day had been ridiculous: cardboard collapsing, closet zones defended like military borders, a sock on trial. And yet Agatha’s throat was tight, her chest too full, because beneath the absurdity was the part she couldn’t shake, the fact that she was here. Not crashing, not visiting, not overstaying. Here.

Her things — messy, mismatched, embarrassing — were seeping into Rio’s order, and Rio hadn’t just tolerated it. She’d made space for it. Labeled it. Expected it.

Agatha’s hand curled in the sheets.

She thought about every place she’d lived before, every apartment filled with half-packed boxes, every drawer that never really belonged to her, every toothbrush perched precariously on someone else’s sink like a visitor’s badge. Always temporary. Always with one foot already out the door.

This was different. Terrifyingly different.

Because Rio wanted her here.

Not in the half-hearted way, not in the casual you can crash whenever way. In the deliberate way. In the way that cleared shelves and labeled drawers and made room for boots. In the way that fought her over closet zones but still refolded her sweaters with care.

Her throat burned. She turned her head toward Rio, who was fussing silently with the edge of a half-folded box, smoothing the cardboard flap like it mattered. Agatha almost laughed again at the absurdity, at the sweetness, at the sheer enormity of the moment, but the sound caught in her chest and turned into something else.

She was doomed. Absolutely, irrevocably doomed.

Because she wanted this. She wanted the boxes, the zones, the socks, the laughter. She wanted the fights and the messes and the quiet mornings after. She wanted all of it, more than she had ever let herself want anything.

And that was the problem. Wanting meant risk. Wanting meant the ground could collapse beneath her at any second. Wanting meant she’d handed Rio the power to break her, simply by giving her a shelf with her initials on it.

She squeezed her eyes shut, pressing her palm hard against her forehead. Christ, Agatha, pull yourself together.

But when she opened them again, Rio was watching her. Calm, steady, like she always was.

“What,” Agatha muttered, too sharp, because softness scared her.

Rio tilted her head. “You’re very quiet.”

Agatha barked out a laugh, brittle at the edges. “Don’t sound so shocked. I can shut up sometimes.”

Rio didn’t answer. Just kept looking at her with those dark, steady eyes, like she could see every spiral ricocheting inside her skull.

Agatha groaned, dragging a pillow over her face. “You’re going to drive me insane.”

The bed dipped as Rio laid down beside her, steady as always. “That’s unlikely. You already are.”

Agatha barked a laugh, sharp and unsteady, because god help her, she couldn’t even argue.

The words hung in the air, too bare, too close to truth. She yanked the pillow down, glaring at the ceiling like it had betrayed her. But Rio didn’t flinch. She just stayed there, calm, unbothered, infuriatingly steady.

Agatha’s chest ached. Because somehow, between the sock, the boxes, the laughter, this had turned into a confession. Not out loud, not exactly. But she felt it in her bones. She was in too deep. She was already gone.

Her eyes burned again, and she swallowed hard. You’re doomed, Harkness, she thought, staring at the ceiling until her vision blurred. Absolutely fucking doomed.

And yet, when Rio’s hand brushed against hers, casual, steady, grounding, she didn’t pull away. She curled her fingers into the touch, let out a shaky breath, and admitted — at least to herself — that doom had never felt so much like home.
