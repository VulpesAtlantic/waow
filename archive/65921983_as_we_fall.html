As We Fall

by EveUnleashed

Summary

She hit the ground hard.

Not the kind of hard that makes good footage.

No sparks. No crash. Just a thud.

Ugly. Muffled. Final.

Like something important had cracked.

OR

Rio is stubborn and insists on still doing her own stunts, until one day, it goes wrong.

Notes

This takes places months after "The Tabloid Trap". It's a bit shorter because I originally had planned to skip this day until I got the idea a few days back. (Special thanks to Ang for actually helping me figure it out!)

Here we go!

Enjoy!

The desert wind kicked up loose dirt, coating everything in a fine layer of grit and tension. Cameras were set. Sunlight filtered harsh through a scaffolded rig. A crane operator adjusted his angle. Somebody yelled for a water bottle. The kind of chaos that only pretended to be under control.

Rio stood at the edge of the drop, arms loose at her sides, eyes locked on the canyon below like it owed her something. Her harness was already on, taut and unforgiving beneath the open black jacket. The stunt was ridiculous: drop twenty feet, twist midair, catch the wire, hit the mark, then pretend it didn’t hurt. CGI would do half the work. But she insisted.

Of course she did.

“Let the double take this one,” someone murmured behind her, too far away to be brave about it.

“Not happening,” Rio called over her shoulder, voice even. “If I’m dying on screen, I’m doing it myself.”

Her coordinator jogged up beside her, headset slightly askew. He was sweating through his t-shirt. “Rio, c’mon. You’ve already done the wire pulls, the glass shot, the rooftop jump. This one’s—”

“Clean,” she said, checking the tension in the line herself. “And rehearsed. I’ve done worse one’s.”

He scrubbed a hand over his face. “That’s not comforting.”

She gave him a grin that didn’t quite reach her eyes.

“Places!” someone called. The cameras began to reset. Rio took her mark. The world slowed down.

The moment before the stunt always felt like a held breath. She could hear the wind shift. The hush of the crew. Even the birds were quiet. Time stretched. The world narrowed.

Rio stood still at the edge of the drop, pulse a slow drumbeat in her ears. Below, the canyon open like a dare. Behind her, the crew fell silent, the kind of hush that came not from respect, but fear.

She could feel it graze the back of her neck, teasing under her collar, tugging at the straps of the harness like it had questions. Somewhere behind her, a headset buzzed. A clipboard shifted.

No one moved.

Even the birds stood still.

She flexed her fingers once. Twice. Rolled her shoulders like she was walking into a fight. Then without warning, without fanfare, without a countdown.

She jumped.

The air caught her with open teeth. The harness pulled late, just a blink, just a breath, just long enough for the angle to shift wrong, momentum gone wild.

A gasp caught in someone’s throat.

The catch that was supposed to be smooth turned violent. A twist instead of a roll. A slip instead of a landing.

She hit the ground hard.

Not the kind of hard that makes good footage.

No sparks. No crash. Just a thud .

Ugly. Muffled. Final.

Like something important had cracked.

The silence afterward was worse than the sound. It didn’t feel like a set anymore, it felt like a funeral. A full second passed. Maybe more.

Then—

“Medic!”

“Get the harness off her—”

“She’s not… Oh god—don’t move her!”

Then chaos, the spell shattered. People surged forward. Calls erupted into headsets. A camera operator dropped the rig. The stunt coordinator was already kneeling beside her, shaking hands hovering over her frame, afraid to touch. Radios sparked. Someone swore. The director shouted something no one heard.

Rio didn’t move.

Not even a twitch.

Everything stopped.

Her eyes were open, barely. Her mouth parted like she wanted to say something, but the breath never made it.

The lighting was warm. Strategic. Agatha sat in a velvet chair that looked expensive but wasn’t, angled towards the host, one leg crossed neatly over the other, a glass of water untouched at her side. She wore a navy suit with silk lapels, sharp as a blade, hair swept back to give her profile that deliberate, effortless precision.

She looked calm. Poised. Like the kind of woman who couldn’t be rattled.

“And what was it like,” the interviewer was saying, voice smooth and reverent, “stepping into a role that demanded so much emotionally while still maintaining that signature restraint you’re known for?”

Agatha smiled, the polite kind, the one she’d trained herself into. “Well, that’s the trick, isn’t it? Bleed on camera, but make it look elegant.”

The crew laughed. Lightly. Admiringly.

She shifted slightly, just enough to glance toward the edge of the set.

And froze.

Lilia was standing off to the side, half-shielded by a lighting rig, phone pressed to her ear. Nothing about her posture was calm. Her shoulders were tight. Her free hand was fisted against her ribs. She wasn’t speaking, but her silence was loud.

That wasn’t normal.

Lilia didn’t get nervous. Lilia didn’t pace. Lilia didn’t fidget.

Agatha blinked, the tail end of the interviewer’s next question landing somewhere just beyond her focus.

She glanced further around the room.

The energy had shifted. Slight. Subtle. But it was there.

One of the producers was whispering something into a headset. A PA was staring at a phone, lips pressed into a line. Someone else knocked over a coffee cup and didn’t even apologize. The tension spread like smoke, curling in at the corners, silent but unmistakable.

Agatha straightened a little. Her spine stiffened. Her hands stayed in her lap, but her fingers curled inwards.

Something was wrong.

She looked back at Lilia.

And Lilia was already looking at her.

No smile. No cover.

Just a look.

That was the first drop of Agatha’s stomach.

She didn’t hear the next question. Didn’t need to.

Because the other interviewer, one of the younger ones, too eager, too plugged in, was already glancing down at his phone. Then again. Then up at her.

His eyes widened just slightly. He turned toward her, slow, like he knew he was about to shatter something.

“I—uh—sorry,” he said, cutting into the flow like a knife. “This is... just now, but—”

He looked at the host. Then back to Agatha. Hesitated.

“Are you aware that Rio Vidal’s been in an accident?”

Agatha didn’t react right away.

Her brain took the words, turned them over, and refused to recognize them. As if the sentence were in another language. As if it belonged to someone else entirely.

An accident.

Rio .

No.

That wasn’t right. That wasn’t possible.

Rio didn’t get hurt. She got bruised, sure. Scraped, maybe. She joked about pain the way other people talked about traffic. She wasn’t the one who got hurt . She was the one who stood back up, bleeding, laughing, flipping off the medic.

Agatha’s breath caught in her throat, shallow and useless.

The set around her blurred, the studio lights too warm, too close. The polite smiles of the interviewers suddenly grotesque. The room had weight now. Too much of it. Pressing in around her ribs. She couldn’t hear the chatter anymore. Couldn’t feel the chair beneath her. Only that sentence, echoing.

Like it had been carved into the air.

Like it wouldn’t leave her alone.

An accident.

Rio’s been in an accident.

The words repeated, looping. Each time a little louder. A little worse. Like her mind was trying to find a version where it made sense.

There wasn’t one.

Something inside her dropped again. Not just her stomach this time, something deeper. Heavier.

And then she was standing.

Not gracefully. Not politely. Just up.

She turned toward Lilia.

Their eyes locked.

And in that second, Lilia’s expression was enough.

Not panic. Not grief. But something worse, uncertainty.

She was already moving before anyone said her name. The host stammered something, a nervous laugh fluttered somewhere behind her, but she didn’t hear it. Her heels clicked like gunfire against the studio floor.

Lilia was already walking toward her. Calm on the outside. Fast under the surface.

“What happened?” Agatha demanded, voice low, sharp, already fraying. “Where is she?”

“We’re still getting—”

“Don’t,” Agatha snapped. “Don’t filter this. What do you know?”

Lilia hesitated, just for half a breath. “She was filming. A stunt. Something went wrong. They airlifted her out fifteen minutes ago.”

Fifteen minutes.

Fifteen minutes and no one had told her.

Agatha blinked, like that might make the world settle. It didn’t. Her jaw tightened.

“I want to go to her.”

Lilia’s mouth opened to argue, but one look from Agatha silenced it.

“Now,” Agatha said.

The car was too quiet.

Outside, the city blurred, sunlight shining off glass and chrome, people living their lives, oblivious. Inside, Agatha sat stiff and silent in the backseat, hands white-knuckled around her phone even though she wasn’t using it.

Lilia sat beside her, her own phone pressed to her ear, murmuring updates to someone on the other end. Her voice was low, efficient. Emotionless.

Agatha hated her for that, just for a second.

She pressed the back of her hand against her mouth, trying to breathe through the rising panic crawling up her throat. Her mind kept replaying the last time she saw Rio, laughing over coffee, boots on her table, some smug remark on her lips. So alive. So loud.

Now all she had were headlines. Text messages with no replies. A three-second news clip that cut out before the medics even reached her.

“She’s going to be fine,” Lilia said suddenly, without looking up.

Agatha turned to her, slowly. “Don’t,” she said. Her voice cracked on the edge of it. “Don’t say that like it’s a fact. You don’t know that.”

Lilia’s expression didn’t falter. “No. But I have to say it like I do. Because if I don’t, you’ll fall apart.”

Agatha didn’t answer. She looked back out the window, lips tight, eyes burning.

They didn’t speak again.

Not until they pulled into the hospital driveway.

The emergency entrance was swarming, security, press, production runners who’d clearly come straight from set. Someone recognized Agatha immediately and moved to intercept, but Lilia stepped in first, flashing credentials and slicing through the chaos with sharp, precise words.

Agatha barely heard any of it.

She pushed through the doors like they owed her something. The fluorescent lights hit her like a slap. The lobby was all antiseptic white and false calm, the kind of place that asked you to sit quietly while your world unraveled.

A nurse behind the desk looked up. “Are you family?”

“Yes,” Agatha said without hesitation. “I’m her partner. Please, I need to know if she’s—”

“She’s in surgery,” the nurse replied, too gently. “I’m sorry. We don’t have an update yet.”

“What happened to her?” Agatha demanded. “Was it the harness? Was it the landing? Did she hit her—”

“I don’t have that information.”

“But someone does,” Agatha snapped, voice breaking through the sterile quiet like thunder. “Someone saw it. Someone was there .”

Lilia stepped in again, hand brushing Agatha’s arm, grounding. “Let them do their job.”

Agatha jerked away. “No. I want answers. I want someone to tell me what happened to her.”

But no one did.

Not then. Not yet.

All they were given was a white waiting room with cold chairs and bad coffee. No names. No charts. No time frame. Just silence and the soft, maddening tick of the clock on the wall.

Agatha sat down because standing felt impossible. Her knees were shaking.

Across from her, Lilia crossed her legs, phone still in hand, eyes scanning the screen like it could offer something better than helplessness.

Agatha folded forward, elbows on her thighs, hands clasped so tight her knuckles ached.

The room was too quiet. Every second stretched for an eternity. The hum of fluorescent lights grated at the edge of her focus. Somewhere behind her, a machine beeped at regular intervals, each one louder than the last.

She didn’t look up.

Because if she looked up, she might have to see the way Lilia kept checking her phone instead of her. Or the way the nurse behind the desk wouldn’t meet her eyes anymore.

She focused on her breath. In. Out. In. Out.

It didn’t help.

Her thoughts moved too fast, jagged, brutal, merciless. Replaying every moment she’d brushed off Rio’s recklessness with a smirk. Every time she said “don’t be dramatic” when Rio came back limping or bruised and proud of it. Every time she pretended not to care how close the danger really was.

She should’ve been there. Should’ve seen it happen. Should’ve stopped her.

Why the fuck didn’t I stop her?

The thought hit like a blow to the chest. She sat with it, let it dig in.

Would she have told her not to jump?

No. Not honestly.

Because Rio was built for the fall. And Agatha. Agatha had always been the one afraid of the landing.

She squeezed her eyes shut, trying not to imagine the worst, but her mind betrayed her anyway, flashes of blood, silence, the way the medic’s voice cracked when someone said she wasn’t responding.

She didn’t know how to do this.

Not the waiting. Not the not-knowing. Not when it was Rio on the other side of the door.

The person she fought with. Woke up next to. Argued about coffee with. Held hands with under tables when no one was supposed to be looking. The only person who ever managed to make her feel seen without demanding anything in return.

And now she was on the other side of a wall Agatha couldn’t break.

She swallowed hard, jaw clenched so tight it hurt. Her throat burned. Her chest felt hollow. Like something had been scooped out of it.

She didn’t cry.

Not yet.

But she was close.

God, she was close.

She just needed someone to open the door.

To tell her anything .

But the only thing that came was silence.

The door opened with the sound of soft soles and bad news.

A doctor stepped in, young, polite, carefully composed. He didn’t sit, didn’t smile. Just stood there with his hands clasped in front of him like he’d rehearsed this a hundred times.

Agatha stood the second she saw him.

“She’s alive,” he said. “But she’s still unconscious. There was some head trauma during the impact. She’s stable, but in a coma. The surgery went as well as we could’ve hoped. She’s resting now.”

Agatha’s knees nearly gave out. She nodded, once, too fast. Her hands shook.

“I want to see her,” she said immediately.

The doctor’s face didn’t change. “She’s in recovery. Only surgical staff for now. We’ll let you know when visitation is possible.”

“She’s alone in there?”

“She’s being monitored closely.”

“That’s not what I asked,” Agatha snapped, the words punching through her throat before she could dull them. “She hates hospitals. She hates being alone in hospitals.”

The doctor didn’t flinch. “I’m sorry. We’ll inform you as soon as you can visit her.”

Agatha opened her mouth to argue, to demand, but Lilia was already beside her, placing a hand on her arm, grounding her again.

“Let them do their job,” she said quietly. “You need rest.”

“I don’t want rest. I want Rio.”

The doctor gave her the kind of apologetic nod that meant absolutely nothing, then disappeared through the same door he came from, leaving nothing but the smell of antiseptic and the sound of her blood pounding in her ears.

They sent her home.

Lilia tried to make it sound reasonable. "You'll be the first call the second she's moved into a room." But the car ride back was a blur of red lights and brake lights and headlights that made her want to scream. No one spoke. There was nothing to say.

By the time they reached her house, the sun was nearly gone, and Agatha felt like she hadn’t breathed properly in hours.

The second the door closed behind her, the quiet hit her like a slap.

No monitors. No doctors. No hum of fluorescent lighting. Just the soft hush of her own too-large house and the muted sounds of the city outside.

She stood in the doorway for a moment, coat still on, shoes still wet from the hospital floor. Her reflection stared back at her from the darkened windows. Hair messier than she remembered. Lipstick long gone.

She looked older somehow. Smaller.

She finally moved.

The couch was too soft. The cushions gave under her like they didn’t want her to stay upright. Her body agreed. She sank into them like something crumbling. Not collapsing, exactly. Just… eroding.

She didn’t cry. Not really.

She didn’t have the energy.

Instead, she picked up the remote with numb fingers and turned on the TV.

Big mistake.

Every channel had it.

First, the polished entertainment coverage.

“—in critical condition after a high-impact stunt gone wrong on the set of Blood Oath, insiders say Rio Vidal was airlifted to Lethe Center Hospital after falling over twenty feet—”

Agatha flinched. Changed the channel.

“—no word yet from her partner, actress Agatha Harkness, who reportedly left a live interview when the news broke—”

Click.

“—rumors swirl over whether the stunt was properly rehearsed or if safety measures were overlooked—”

Click.

“—some fans are speculating that the fall may have been intentional, citing cryptic social media posts—”

What the fuck.

Agatha threw the remote. It hit the floor with a dull thunk and skidded under the coffee table. She didn’t bother retrieving it. The screen kept talking without her.

She stared ahead, eyes glassy. Not watching. Not blinking.

Eventually, she reached for her phone.

She shouldn’t have.

The notifications were a minefield. Hundreds of mentions. Her name trending, Rio’s name trending higher. Hashtags like #PrayForRio and #AgatharioForever and, horrifyingly, #DidAgathaKnow.

Some people were sympathetic. Fans sharing clips of their favorite interviews. Grainy photos of the two of them holding hands at wrap parties. Quotes they’d forgotten they’d said. Someone had posted a slowed-down video of Rio brushing Agatha’s hair out of her face during a tour, captioned “she looked at her like she hung the stars.”

Agatha’s throat tightened.

She scrolled.

The worse were the vultures.

Tabloids speculating whether the relationship was real. Columnists dissecting the look on Agatha’s face as she stormed out of the interview. A few sites insinuating the whole thing was a publicity stunt gone wrong.

“Power couple no more? ” one headline asked.

She wanted to throw the phone. Wanted to break it. Burn it. Set the entire narrative on fire.

Instead, she dropped it face down on the couch and pressed her hands against her face.

She’d never felt this helpless.

Not when the fake dating thing started. Not during the press tours. Not even when the line between performance and reality blurred so much she couldn’t see the edge anymore.

At least then, Rio was beside her.

Now… she was alone.

And Rio was in a hospital bed somewhere. Quiet. Unmoving. Tubes in her arm, monitors tracking every heartbeat Agatha couldn’t hear.

The space beside her had never felt so empty.

She got up. Poured herself a glass of something amber from the cabinet. Didn’t taste it. Sat down again and turned the TV off completely. Let the silence come back.

She stared at the dark screen and tried not to drown in all the things she had said, but too late, or too carefully, or too jokingly.

It wasn’t that she hadn’t told Rio she loved her. She had. In hotel rooms at midnight, in quiet, half- lit kitchens while coffee brewed. In touches. In glances. In a thousand soft, unspoken ways.

She thought about the times Rio touched her, soft, unthinking, instinctive. A hand at her waist. Fingers brushing hers under the table. A thumb grazing the inside of her wrist when she thought no one was looking. Little things Agatha never asked for but always noticed. Always remembered.

She never really said how much they meant.

Now, every I love you felt too small. Every kiss too short. Every moment they’d wasted being proud or stubborn or tired felt like a wound she didn’t know how to close.

They had a life together.

A rhythm.

Mornings that started with stolen shirts and uneven coffee, nights that ended tangled in warmth and sleep and the safety of Rio’s arm thrown lazily across her waist. That stupid little humming sound Rio made while brushing her teeth. The way she never closed cabinet doors. Her laugh, always a little too loud when Agatha was trying to be serious. All of it was carved into the shape of their days.

And now.

Now it all felt distant. Fragile. Like it had never been real enough to hold onto.

She’d thought they had time.

Time to fix the parts they hadn’t figured out yet. Time to argue over holidays and plants and whose turn it was to take the laundry out. Time to let the fear melt. To stop bracing for loss. To believe that maybe, just maybe, this love was safe.

But time wasn’t promised. Not even to people who loved each other as fiercely as they did.

And now Rio was unconscious in a hospital bed she couldn’t get to.

And Agatha… Agatha was stuck in a house that still smelled like her. Still had her jacket thrown over a kitchen chair. Still had her boots by the door. Like she’d be back any second, calling out something inappropriate just to make her laugh.

It hurt.

It hurt in the deep, painful way only love could hurt.

Because she hadn’t just lost her person. She’d lost the chance to keep building the life they were still learning how to hold together.

And if Rio didn’t wake up; if this was it…

There wouldn’t be a single I love you in the world big enough to cover the silence she’d leave behind.

The thought hit her like cold water.

Her breath caught. Her chest ached. Not in that poetic way people talked about heartbreak, this wasn’t soft. It was surgical. Like something being carved out of her ribcage in slow, silent movements.

She would give anything to go back, not to some dramatic turning point, just five stupid minutes before the fall.

To pull Rio aside, cup her face, kiss her like it meant something urgent. Not soft. Not casual. But stop-everything real. And say it, not as a joke, not as a whisper half-buried under the weight of routine, but fully, honestly.

“I’m scared of losing you. Because I already love you too much to know how to survive it.”

But time didn’t work like that.

So she stared.

She stared at the dark screen, its blank reflection staring right back at her. A mirror she didn’t ask for. A verdict she couldn’t appeal.

Now all she had were the empty rooms, the silence, one that would echo, and a thousand versions of what she could have said.

It was just past 8 a.m. when the call came.

Agatha hadn’t slept. Not really. She’d been lying on top of the sheets in yesterday’s clothes, phone in hand, screen dark. Waiting. Always waiting.

When it finally rang, she didn’t even look at the number.

“Hello?” Her voice cracked, rough with exhaustion.

“Miss Harkness?” The nurse on the other end sounded cautious, careful. “We’ve moved Rio Vidal into a more appropriate room. She’s stable. You can visit now.”

Agatha didn’t respond right away.

Her breath caught in her throat. For a second, she forgot how to move.

Then the phone was on the bed, and she was already standing. Jacket. Shoes. No makeup, no bag. She didn’t care. The sun wasn’t even up, but she didn’t stop to wait for it. She was out the door in under three minutes.

The streets were starting to awaken. Every red light felt like a personal offense. By the time she reached the hospital, her hands were shaking again, her chest tight with something she couldn’t name, relief, maybe. Or something too scared to be that yet.

She didn’t speak to anyone at the front desk. Just nodded once, sharp and silent, and they led her down the now-familiar hallways without a word.

And when they opened the door, everything else stopped.

The room was quiet.

Not the kind of silence that felt peaceful. The kind that rang in the ears. Sterile and steady and endless.

Monitors blinked softly in the dark. A low, mechanical beeping tracked every beat of Rio’s heart, steady, but too slow for Agatha’s comfort. Tubes trailed from her arm like threads, her skin was pale under the harsh hospital light, lips cracked, hair mussed where the pillow had flattened it.

She didn’t look like Rio.

Not exactly.

Not the woman who picked fights for fun, who smirked in press tours, who danced barefoot in Agatha’s kitchen at 2AM to bad music just to make her laugh. This was someone still. Someone far away.

Agatha stood at the edge of the bed, frozen.

She’d imagined this moment. She thought she’d be ready. That when the door finally opened and they let her in, she’d rush to Rio’s side, grab her hand, say something right.

But all she could do was stare.

Until her legs gave out, and she sank into the chair beside the bed.

Very carefully, like she was afraid she might break something, she reached out. Brushed a lock of hair away from Rio’s forehead. Her fingers trembled.

“I’m here,” she said, barely above a whisper. “You can stop scaring the shit out of me now.”

No response. Just the steady rhythm of the machines.

Agatha let her hand drift lower, along Rio’s temple, to her jaw, to her hand resting against the blanket. She laced their fingers together slowly, gently, like she was asking permission.

And then she leaned in, pressing a soft kiss to her forehead.

Not rushed. Not for comfort. Just quiet. Present.

She stayed like that until the nurse asked her to leave.

She came back the next morning.

And the morning after that.

And the one that came after.

And again.

Each day she arrived earlier. Stayed longer. Ignored the messages piling up on her phone. She missed meetings. Walked out of a scheduled shoot without a word. Her publicist left nine voicemails. She didn’t listen to any of them.

Lilia tried once to pull her aside. “People are asking questions.”

Agatha didn’t even look at her. “Let them.”

She stopped wearing makeup. Her hair lived in a loose knot. Half the time she forgot to eat until a nurse gently placed something in front of her.

Most of the time she didn’t finish it.

Her world narrowed to four walls and the sound of Rio’s breath through the monitor.

She talked sometimes, when it was just them. Things that didn’t matter. Things that did. A running monologue of headlines and gossip and the absurdity of hospital coffee.

One afternoon, she brought a paperback they never finished reading together. She sat by the window and read aloud until her voice cracked.

At night, she would fall asleep in the chair, chin against her chest, their hands still clasped. Nurses let her. Doctors didn’t ask questions anymore. Her name was on the chart now, emergency contact, partner, next of kin.

She never said the word aloud. Not here.

But it was written everywhere now.

She watched Rio breathe. Watched her chest rise and fall. Counted the beats. Traced the lines of her face like it would summon her back.

Come back, she thought. Over and over, like a prayer.

Because without her, the house was quiet. The bed was too cold. The world too sharp.

She would stay here. However long it took.

She wasn’t going anywhere.

The room was dim, washed in early morning rays. The blinds were cracked just enough for soft light to spill across the edge of the bed. Machines hummed. Steady. Familiar now.

Agatha sat in the chair beside Rio, curled into herself. Elbow on the armrest, forehead resting against her knuckles. Her eyes were open, barely, but unfocused, somewhere between watching and waiting and praying.

She hadn’t left the hospital in days. Not really.

A nurse came in hours ago, adjusted the IV, checked vitals. Left without a word. They’d stopped trying to make her leave. She was as much a fixture in the room as the monitors now.

She barely noticed the shift at first.

A sound, quiet, shallow, like something catching on itself.

Then again.

A cough.

Agatha’s head snapped up.

“Rio?” Her voice was hoarse, unused. She leaned forward so fast the chair creaked beneath her. Rio’s face twitched. Her brow furrowed, slight, but there. Her lips parted again, dry, rough, another cough, then a weak breath.

Agatha’s chest seized. She reached out instinctively, hand finding Rio’s, fingers curling in tight.

“Hey—hey, I’m here. You’re okay. You’re okay—” Her voice cracked and shattered all in the same breath.

Tears welled up before she even realized it. Hot, stinging, immediate.

Rio’s eyes blinked open.

Slow. Dazed.

She stared at the ceiling for a second, then turned her head, just barely, to look at Agatha.

Her gaze was cloudy. Pupils slow to focus. Her mouth moved before sound followed.

And then…

"...Who are you?"

Agatha froze.

The world didn’t fall apart all at once.

It fell in layers.

First her breath. Then her chest. Then her heart, splitting open with something deep and primal. Like grief that hadn’t had time to settle before it was twisted again.

She stared at Rio, stunned. Mouth slightly open. Words caught in her throat, tangled.

Her fingers were still holding Rio’s, but now they felt fragile. Like if she squeezed too hard, she’d break something.

Silence filled the space between them.

Loud. Crushing.

Agatha’s entire world stopped on the edge of that moment.

And just like that, the ground gave out beneath her, without sound, without warning, without mercy.

It hit her like a second fall, silent, sharp, and nowhere to land.

She couldn’t move. Couldn’t speak. Just sat there, fingers still clutching Rio’s hand, feeling everything inside her fracture at once.

Then, Rio blinked.

Her mouth twitched.

And a laugh escaped her. Raspy. Broken. But real.

“You should see your face,” she wheezed.

Agatha didn’t react at first. She just stared. The laugh didn’t register, not for a second. It was like her brain had to catch up to the sound, drag itself through the fog.

And then it clicked.

“You absolute bitch ,” Agatha gasped.

Relief slammed into her like a wave. Not gentle, violent. She exhaled a laugh that sounded far too close to a sob, then smacked Rio’s arm, not hard but with conviction.

Rio flinched, letting out another hoarse cough. “Okay, ow! Injured, remember?”

Agatha laughed again, and this time it broke fully out of her, shaky and wet and full of days and days of tension finally cracking. She wiped at her face with the back of her wrist, then leaned over, pressing her forehead against the edge of the bed, against Rio’s arm, needing the contact to feel real.

“You’re the worst,” she muttered into the sheets. “I thought—Jesus, I thought—”

Rio turned herself just slightly, watching her through heavy-lidded eyes, voice still raw. “Hey… I’m here.”

Agatha looked up at her again. “Barely.”

Rio gave her a tired, crooked smile. “Yeah, but I’m winning.”

Agatha let out a huff that was part laugh, part sob, part exhaustion.

For a long moment, neither of them spoke. The tension between them softened, not gone, but bearable now. Something human again.

Then Rio’s eyes flicked toward the window, like she was checking the time, as if it still made sense.

“How long have I been off?” she asked, voice quiet.

Agatha didn’t answer right away. Her lips twitched.

She looked Rio dead in the eye. “It’s been three years.”

Rio stared at her. Just stared.

Her entire face dropped.

“You’re kidding.”

Agatha held the silence just a second longer.

And then she burst out laughing.

Rio groaned, letting her head fall back against the pillow. “You’re a menace.”

“That’s rich coming from you.”

Rio turned her head back to face her, exhaling slowly, like the laughter had taken everything she had. “Seriously though…?”

Agatha’s smile faded into something softer. She reached out again, this time brushing her fingers along the side of Rio’s face.

“It’s been a week,” she said. “One week.”

Rio blinked. Let that land. Then gave the smallest nod.

A beat passed.

And then, instinctively, they both reached for each other’s hand again. No jokes. No tricks.

Just fingers threading together in silence.

The weight of everything they’d been through sat between them, heavy and real, but for the first time in days, it didn’t feel unbearable.

They were still here.

It had been three weeks since Rio came home.

The bruises were still yellowing along her ribs. The scar on her temple had thinned out to a pale, angry line. She couldn’t sit too long without her back seizing up, and she still winced when she sneezed.

But she was alive.

And that fact was written in the rhythm of the house now, in the way Agatha’s breathing slowed when Rio entered the room, in the second coffee cup placed beside hers in the morning, in the quiet music that played in the background most afternoons, like the silence was finally safe to fill.

The couch had become Rio’s throne-slash-prison.

She hated it.

She lay sprawled across it now, tucked under a grey blanket, left leg propped on two pillows, TV remote in one hand, chewing the end of a pen. She hadn’t written anything with it in over an hour. The window was open just enough to let in the afternoon breeze, which carried the smells of city traffic and late-blooming jasmine. Her eyes kept flicking toward the open balcony door like she could will it closer.

Agatha’s voice called from the kitchen, clipped and knowing.

“Stop looking at the window like you’re planning a prison break.”

Rio groaned from the couch. “I just want to go outside. Feel the sun. Taste danger.”

“You tasted concrete. Isn’t that enough?”

Rio rolled her eyes. “I’m just saying. I think I could make it to the railing and back if I had a head start.”

“You still need help getting off the couch.”

“I’m emotionally resilient.”

“You’re physically held together with duct tape and caffeine.”

A beat.

“Okay, that’s fair.”

Agatha entered the living room carrying a tray: a steaming mug, a small bowl of something green, and exactly one piece of toast sliced into two annoyingly precise triangles. She set it on the coffee table with an air of finality and straightened her back like she’d just delivered a verdict.

Rio sat up slightly and squinted at the bowl. “What... is that.”

“Nutrition,” Agatha said, deadpan.

“That’s not an answer. That’s a threat .”

“It’s a smoothie bowl. It has spinach, banana, protein powder, and healing intentions.”

“Did you hex it?”

“Wouldn’t you like to know.”

Rio gave her a look that could only be described as theatrical betrayal. “You used to be cool.”

“You used to have an intact ribcage.”

Agatha crouched beside the couch and braced her hands on either side of Rio, hovering just close enough to steal the air out of her lungs. “So,” she whispered, eyes narrowing, “eat the bowl.”

Rio stared her down for exactly three seconds, then tilted her chin up like a brat. “Make me.”

Agatha didn’t flinch.

She leaned in, slow, dangerously calm, until her nose nearly brushed Rio’s. Her lips twitched into a smirk. “I’m not above force-feeding you with a spoon like a Victorian orphan.”

“You’re turned on right now.”

“Only a little.”

They both broke at the same time, Agatha with a short exhale that might have been a laugh, Rio with a huff that ended in a cough. She winced immediately and brought a hand to her ribs.

Agatha’s smirk vanished.

She reached out, gentle now, and steadied Rio’s shoulder with a feather-light touch. “Hey. You okay?”

Rio nodded, breath shallow. “Yeah. Just forgot I’m not invincible anymore.”

“You were never invincible.”

“Don’t ruin my brand.”

Agatha sat back onto the rug and crossed her legs beneath her, still watching her closely. “You’re allowed to be bored, you know. And cranky. But you have to eat. And rest. And not sneak off to the balcony when I’m in the shower.”

Rio blinked, then grinned. “I did that once.”

“And nearly passed out.”

“I looked majestic, though. Like a wounded crow in the wind.”

“You looked like a Victorian ghost with a limp.”

Rio laughed, gently this time, and finally picked up a spoon. She poked the green mush with exaggerated caution. “This better not taste like regret.”

“It tastes like me loving you. Suffer through it.”

She took a bite.

Her face scrunched immediately. “You’re lucky I’m in love with you.”

“I am lucky,” Agatha said softly, more to herself than to Rio.

Rio paused at that, spoon halfway to her mouth, suddenly still. The room went quiet again. Outside, a horn blared in the street, followed by birdsong, like the city couldn’t decide if it wanted to scream or sing.

Agatha didn’t break eye contact.

“I know I say it a lot,” she added, her voice steady now. “But I don’t think I said it enough. Before.”

Rio’s gaze softened.

“You say it enough,” she said. “And even when you don’t, I know.”

“I just—” Agatha exhaled, shaking her head like she could physically toss the memory off. “You were lying there. And I couldn’t reach you. Not really. And I kept thinking, what if I hadn’t said it enough times for you to carry it with you.”

Rio placed the spoon back in the bowl, leaned forward despite the ache in her back, and cupped Agatha’s face with her hand. “I carried it,” she whispered. “Even when it hurt. Even when everything else stopped. That never did.”

Agatha blinked, quickly, furiously, then tilted into the touch and kissed her palm.

A long moment passed, warm and still.

Then Rio smirked. “Also. Your face when I fake forgot you? Priceless.”

Agatha groaned, yanking a throw pillow and swatting her. “Unbelievable.”

“I regret nothing.”

“You’re lucky you’re cute.”

“I’m devastating.”

“You’re currently in a blanket burrito eating spinach goo and smelling like menthol patches.”

“Sexy.”

Agatha rolled her eyes and stood up, snatching the now mostly-eaten bowl. “You get five minutes on the balcony. That’s it.”

“With a blanket cape?”

“Yes.”

“And a juice box?”

“You’re pushing it.”

“I’m wounded!”

“Exactly.”

They moved together toward the open balcony door, slowly, carefully, hand-in-hand. Rio shuffled with the grace of someone held together by painkillers and determination. Agatha stood just behind her, ready to catch her at the first sign of unsteadiness, but didn’t say anything. She let her do it.

Let her reclaim the movement, the space, the breath.

They stepped into the golden light of late afternoon. The city spread below them, loud and messy and alive.

Rio turned her face to the sun and closed her eyes, a slow smile unfurling across her lips.

“I missed this,” she said quietly.

Agatha looked at her, not the healing bruises or the scars, but the her underneath it. The one she almost lost.

“I missed you,” she replied.

Rio’s eyes opened.

“I was always coming back.”

Agatha swallowed hard.

“I know,” she said.

And this time, she believed it.
