Summary

Everything about Agatha is fascinating—and it really shouldn’t be, considering the fact that Death has borne witness to countless lives across time from beginning to end. But there’s something rich and raw in the way that Agatha moves through the world, a heady, almost carnal enjoyment filling every indulgent aspect of life that she chooses to engage in. Even in the most mundane moments, Agatha’s enthralling.

Death can’t look away from her.

Agatha sits at a tavern and slyly flirts with the innkeeper’s daughter over a tankard of ale and a plate of carrot and potato stew—Death is a literal fly on the fucking wall.

Agatha lures a witch—or two, or three—off into the woods and goads them into attacking her? Death arrives early to watch the show.

Agatha goes to bathe in a nearby river, dark hair loosed from its typical braid, body glistening wetly, showcasing every minute movement of muscle beneath her soft, supple skin—well.

All of a sudden, Death is fixated on the day-to-day details of mortal existence.

Agatha, Agatha, Agatha.

If Death had a heart, it would probably beat itself in the triplicate rhythm of her name.

Death is the first to come into being amongst their siblings, for there can be no true beginning made without considering, already, its end.

 

They are given no face, no form—merely a duty, and the will to uphold it. (This is a fact that holds true for eons. This is a fact that begins to form devastating, world-bending, universe-ending cracks somewhere in Salem, Massachusetts. The object of this inevitable future destruction will be born to unloving hands in a time that mortals on Earth will refer to as the year 1674.)

 

At the start of their existence, the lives they collect are small. Simple. Death shuffles through the universe and sweeps single-celled organisms from one plane to the next with ease. (There is no such thing as begging yet, and Death has no such heart to be swayed. Yet.)

 

Things change, as they must. It is the nature of living things to be in a state of constant flux. Death does not begrudge them this. Death, after all, is merely a function. A universal Roomba, if you will. (This will be a joke that Death will conceptualize in early 2003 after spotting one zig-zagging around a body for the first time. The clumsy little machine is trying its best to clean up a mess it didn’t cause. Death will recognize the curious kinship existing between them and laugh before turning their focus back to the departed. They will tuck this tiny bit of humor away in the back of their mind for when the actual inventor of the machine finally comes to meet them.) 

 

Emotion is a distant, mostly foreign concept. Irritation? Unfathomable. (Until Agatha Harkness, that is.)

 

Time is… more of a suggestion, than anything else. For a decent chunk of eternity, Death drifts around without really putting any thought towards it. What does time matter to them? Things die constantly; they are never out of work and never will be until the entirety of existence finally shuts its doors. Death is the universe’s original—and best—employee. Tireless, unyielding. Possessing no concept of needing sick days and no desire to put in vacation requests. 

 

The job gets more interesting when living things start developing the ability to actually care for one another, when the relationship between parent and offspring becomes more than just evolutionarily demanded care. 

 

That’s when creatures begin to fear Death. 

 

That’s when they start to resist. 

 

In a far off corner of the galaxy, a child dies before several hours before its parent is destined to follow. Death, usually, would not wait. There are other places to be and other souls to reap; Death returns as needed to the creatures that call for them. 

 

But the child looks at Death—currently a soft, nebulous blob attempting to project a sense of peace—and gives a plaintive squeak of dismay. It shies away from them, quaking; the shining, soft soul of a thread cut close to the quick. It moves to nuzzle not at its own body—no longer of any use, but also no longer a source of pain—but its mother’s trembling flesh instead. Death realizes then that the tiny spirit in their charge is scared to leave that which is most familiar to them. 

 

The mother is dying anyways. 

 

Death can feel the pull of its spirit, knows that they would soon make the journey back to this forlorn place anyways to gather what is owed them. 

 

So Death, for the first time, waits. 

 

The parent passes. 

 

Her soul slips free from her body, presses itself close to her child. They follow Death to the bridge in unison. And then, as much as either of them can, they nudge the edges of Death’s foggy, abstract shape and make matching lowing sounds that Death perceives to be surprisingly pleasant before walking off into the unknown.

 

It leaves a strange sensation somewhere in the theoretical void of Death’s being. 

 

Oddly rattled, they go off in search of one of their siblings. 

 

Eternity is Death’s favorite—or would be, if they felt strongly enough to have favorites. (Someday, they will. Someday, they will feel things so strongly that they will almost forget the hollow taste of emptiness entirely. Unlike Death, Eternity enjoys having shape—and even some kind of definition, which is why Eternity is a he right now while Death remains more of a… thing.

He rouses from his slumber as Death assumes the guise of a tiny asteroid and settles somewhere beside him. Death does not always take on a tangible form when they visit, often preferring the vague ephemerality of being nothing more than a constant, unchanging concept. Agatha, Agatha, Agatha.

 

Death closes up her body’s chest and calmly surveys her work with critical not-eyes, still existing as a swirling mass of shadow. Finding no flaws except the ones they’d intended upon, Death allows herself to relax.

 

Then, with the careful, shockingly arduous task completed at last, Death falls void-first into her own creation and finally—finally—breathes.

 

 

There’s a steep learning curve that comes with having an actual body and submitting to its various needs and desires. All of which remain optional for Death, but for the sake of gaining new, lived experiences, Death allows herself to go along with what the body suggests.

Death finds a secluded place to curl up, deep in the bounds of a forest that man has yet to fully traverse. A soft bed of fluffy moss rises to meet her body as she settles down on the ground and the surrounding trees shake a covering of leaves over her form.
