EARTH LOW ORBIT

THREE DAYS AFTER THE BATTLE AGAINST THANOS

It’s a long shot, really.

Peter did the math plenty of times as a kid, through the better part of his teenage years, and well into his twenties. He was eight back when Yondu picked him up, and that’s going on thirty years ago now. His Gramps had probably been, what, fifty-something at the time? Maybe sixty? Sixty and thirty— he’d be ninety years old by now, if he’s even—

He shakes his head, tries to stop thinking about it, forcibly shoves down that little voice in his head that keeps saying, it’s gonna be too late by now, Pete, you should have come home sooner.

In the end, Rocket’s the one that finds him.

“Humies on this planet are crazy, I’m telling ya,” he says, pulling up a file on the ship’s main holoscreen. “Keep all their info where anybody can find it. Took me like two seconds.”

Peter doesn’t say anything. He’s too busy reading over all the data listed on the screen. Everything’s on there. His grandpa’s name, his age, two phone numbers, an address, a social media account that hasn’t been updated since the day it was made. There’s a picture, too, and Peter thinks that maybe his brain is playing tricks on him, because Gramps looks just about exactly the same as Peter remembers, maybe only a little bit older.

His voice doesn’t work at first. When it does, he can’t even finish the question.

“Is—? Is he…?”

“Alive, yeah,” Rocket nods, “and kickin’, apparently.”

He pulls up a video. It’s from someone else’s profile with his grandpa’s name mentioned in the caption. A shaky camera job, for sure, someone who barely knew what they were doing when they took it, but there he is — his hair a little grayer, his face a little older. But he has a big old smile on his face, walking around and tapping a soccer ball with his foot to pass it between him and a little giggling girl with blonde pigtails.

Who’s the little girl? His cousin, maybe?

“Not bad, for an old guy,” Rocket comments. “So? We going, or what?”

“Maybe we shouldn’t go.”

“Peter.”

“I’m just saying, I mean, it’s been thirty years. It’s been a really, really long time,” Peter says. He’s sitting up in bed, a holoscreen open in his lap as he scrolls through pages upon pages of his old family’s life. The little girl was his cousin, apparently. His Aunt Marianne’s kid— and God, he’d forgotten all about her, hadn’t he? The memory came back to him as soon as he found her picture, his Aunt Marianne, laughing and talking with his mom in the kitchen, sneaking him extra cookies behind her back while Mom wasn’t looking. She’s only ten years older than him, apparently, which doesn’t fit too much with what he remembers. She’d always seemed like such a grown-up back then, but she must’ve been only a teenager at the time.

And now she’s got a kid. Two of them, apparently. Fourteen-year-old Robert and little six- year-old Ava. His little cousins. His grandpa’s other grandkids.

And then there’s his uncle, and he still only half-remembers his uncle, his mom’s older brother. George, who’s pushing sixty now with a wife named Lin and three kids all in their twenties. Justin, Emily, and a girl named Danielle who goes by Trixie, though Peter has no friggin’ clue where that comes from.

But… this is his grandpa’s family.

Two kids, even after Peter’s mom died. Five grandkids, even after Peter literally vanished off the face of the Earth.

The mattress dips as Gamora sits down beside him, leaning gently into his side. She looks down at the photo he’s on now, his cousin Justin’s senior high school pictures.

“He looks a bit like you,” she says. Her hair falls over his shoulder as she leans past him to zoom in on the image, just slightly. “I’d know those eyes anywhere.”

And at least that’s nice to know, that Peter must have gotten his eyes from his mom. Peter nods, clearing his throat to dislodge the lump that’s been sitting there for the last twenty minutes— a pointless move, really, considering that what he’s about to pull up on the screen is just gonna bring it right back. Still. He figures Gamora deserves to see it, too. He goes back to Aunt Marianne’s page, scrolls all the way down to the date that he’s had memorized his whole life, and taps on a post from that day last year.

Can’t believe you’ve been gone for almost thirty years now, she wrote. You’d be 56 today. I know you’ve been looking down on us all this time from that better place you found, so from all of us: Happy Birthday, Mere, love you and miss you more every day ♥

“Is that—?”

“That’s Mom, yeah,” Peter answers.

The picture is a good one, from well before his mom ever got sick, with her hair falling in tousled waves way past her shoulders. The picture was taken without her knowing, and she’s in the middle of laughing at some unknown joke, beaming at something off camera.

“She’s beautiful.”

Peter just nods, and he wipes his cheeks with the back of his hand. Gamora leans her head onto his shoulder, her hand gently running up and down his arm.

“I, uh, I just don’t know,” he says when he can speak again. He clears his throat, tries again. “I don’t know if we should go.”

“Peter—”

“Hear me out, though, okay?” he says. “It’s been thirty years at this point, you know? Gramps is almost ninety. They miss my mom, yeah, and they missed me, sure, maybe, but… Gamora, they moved on. They got on with their lives. Gramps has a whole bunch of other grandkids. They’re happy. I mean, what if me showing up again just throws a huge wrench in all that? Hell, they don’t know what happened to me, they probably think I died or something. And then, what, that missing kid shows up, not even close to a kid anymore, and he’s got a bunch of aliens with him, too? What are they supposed to do with that? What if they don’t believe it’s me? What if they don’t…?”

What if they don’t care that it’s me?

He doesn’t say that part out loud. He can’t.

Gamora probably knows where he was going with it anyway. She takes the holoscreen away from him and sets it aside. Then she shifts around, moving so that she’s straddling his lap, and she places both hands on either side of his face.

“Peter, you’re missing a crucial detail here,” she says, sweeping a thumb across his cheekbone.

“Yeah?”

“You moved on, too,” she tells him. “You miss your mother dearly, I know, but you found a new family, just as your grandfather and your aunt and your uncle have done. And you desperately want to see them again, so what makes you think they won’t feel the same way?”

He chews on the inside of his lip. It’s a very… well, a very Gamora way of looking at it, and pretty much everything she says always makes way more sense than it has any right to.

But still.

“And if you think they’ll have a difficult time adjusting to us… not being human,” Gamora adds, way too casual with it, “you could always wait to introduce us. I won’t be offended at all if—”

“No,” Peter cuts her off, shaking his head. “No, that’d probably be the smarter way to do it, but… Nah. No way am I doing this without you.”

The smile tugging at her lips is gentle and hesitant and a little sad, but it still lights up her whole face the way her smiles always do. She leans in to press a kiss to his forehead, and then to his nose, and then to his lips. She lets that last one linger, and then Peter wraps his arms around her waist and buries his face in the dip between her shoulder and her neck.

“Okay,” he mumbles into her skin. “Yeah. We’ll go.”

The farm’s exactly how Peter remembers it.

Sort of. He definitely never saw it from this high up, but so far, it fits. A big old rickety house in the middle of a wide swath of open fields. There’s the one field over toward the back of the house that Peter thinks used to be where his grandpa grew a corn maze every year — he has a vague memory of running through stalks three times as tall as he was, wearing a headlamp and pretending he was on some kind of search-and-rescue mission, though that field’s totally empty now. Wrong season, maybe? Hell if he knows. On either side of the house, there’s rows and rows and rows of some bright green crop that Peter couldn’t name if you paid him. There’s his grandpa’s old shed and a picnic table and what’s gotta be a chicken coop, too, in the backyard.

There’s also a huge gravel driveway out front that leads down to a long, winding country road, and there’s two cars parked right there in front of the house.

“Hey, you know what,” Peter says, trying not to sound like his heart’s about to pound out of his chest. “Looks like he’s got company over already. Now’s probably not a good time—”

“Nah, c’mon,” Kraglin says without missing a beat. “More the merrier.”

Then he pilots the Benatar down to the empty field across from the house and completely ignores the noise of protest Peter makes. Because he’s a dickhead.

Rocket opens up the ramp, and suddenly the whole place smells like — wow, Peter did not realize he’d remember exactly how the place smelled after thirty years. Grass and hay and dirt. Mantis heads down the ramp first, always excited to see a new place, and Nebula’s close behind her, quickly followed by Drax and Groot and Rocket.

Kraglin pauses by the ramp on his way out and asks, “You good, Pete?”

Peter shrugs one shoulder, gives a quick so-so wave.

“You’re not gonna yak, are ya?”

“That is… a really good question, dude,” Peter says, shaking himself. He’s got this. He’s got this. “I got this. It’s cool, I’m good. Let’s go.”

Most of the others hang back a bit as he approaches the house, but he makes sure to keep a hold of Gamora’s hand so she knows he wants her right there next to him. Hell, he’s not even sure he’d be standing if she wasn’t right here next to him. He feels like he’s about to go shaking out of his damn skin, his heart still skipping a rabbit-thump through his temples, like maybe his bones are just gonna turn to jelly and fall right out of him.

And then they’re standing at the front door.

“Holy shit, I remember this,” Peter murmurs, staring wide-eyed at the little black horse-and- buggy decal on the screen door. It’s lower than he remembers, but he figures that makes sense. He’s higher up than he used to be. “Rest of the porch is a little different, though. I don’t remember the rocking chair. I think the shutters are different, too? I dunno, maybe they, like… painted them or something.”

Gamora gives his hand a reassuring squeeze, and he stops rambling.

Peter sucks in a deep breath, blows it out all at once, and knocks on the door before he can chicken out.

He still tries chickening out anyway.

“Shit, this was a bad idea. This was dumb. We should—”

But then, from inside, a voice cuts him off.

“Now, now, I’m comin’,” the voice says, and Peter recognizes it instantly. There’s a low boof that must be some kind of dog, and then: “I’m comin’. Stay there, Daisy.”

Peter can’t move. He’s still shaking, but he can’t move. There’s no rattle of a lock or anything — apparently his grandpa keeps the place open — so there’s nothing to delay it. No warning before the door opens with a faint creak, and then he’s right there on the other side of a flimsy little storm door. His grandpa, thirty years older and yet, somehow, still looking almost exactly as Peter remembers him. He’s tall, right around Peter’s height. He’s still got those tiny little reading glasses on his nose. He’s still got a ring of greyish hair around his head and a much thinner layer up top.

Naturally, his eyes go to Gamora first, and then to the others all gathered around behind them, and then to the massive spaceship on the other side of the street, before he finally lands on Peter.

“Uh,” Peter says at first, then clears his throat. “Hi. You, um, you probably don’t recognize me, but—”

“Pete,” his grandpa says, sounding like he’s just had the wind knocked out of him. His eyes are wide and shining and, Peter realizes with a jolt, exactly the same color as the ones he sees in the mirror every day. He opens up the screen door, clutching it tight in one hand like it’s the only thing keeping him from passing out right here.

And now there’s literally nothing separating them except a couple feet of empty air.

“Holy hell, that’s really you, ain’t it?”

Peter gulps. He nods, because he’s not totally sure he can trust his voice.

His grandpa reaches out for a second, an aborted reach like he’s afraid to actually touch Peter. Then he seems to gather up the nerve, laying a hand on the side of his face. His hand is rough and calloused and warm.

Then, before Peter even knows what’s happening, he’s being pulled into a hug that is way too damn strong for a guy as old as his grandpa is.

“Holy hell,” his grandpa says again, this time against his shoulder. “Holy hell, Petey.”

Peter can’t help it. He laughs. And if it comes out sounding a little manic, well, whatever.

“Yeah,” he says, hugging his grandpa back just as tight, hardly believing it. A few days ago he thought half the universe was gonna get snapped out of existence, and now he’s here, he’s home, he’s in Missouri again after three goddamn decades, and he’s getting the life hugged out of him by his grandpa. He feels a weird superposition of eight years old and thirty-eight all at once, too tall and bulky to be the kid he was back then, but somehow made smaller by the way his grandpa’s patting the back of his head. Just like old times. “Yeah, it’s, uh… It’s me.”

“Jesus, kiddo,” his grandpa murmurs.

Kiddo.

Huh. So that’s where Peter got it from.

“It’s been…”

“Uh, thirty years, about,” Peter finishes for him, swallowing down the lump in his throat. “Sorry I took so long.”

“Hey, no, c’mon, you stop that,” his grandpa says, breaking off the hug but leaving a firm hand on Peter’s shoulder. He’s doing a little better than Peter in the not-crying-like-a-baby department, but not by much. He swipes his free hand over his face and sniffs as he looks Peter over, from head to toe and back up again. “You’re here now, ‘s what matters, ain’t it? Jesus, look at you.” He sniffs again. “Still got that bright-eyed puppy look you got from your mom, huh?”

“Yeah?”

“Recognized it in a heartbeat,” his grandpa nods.

Now, finally, he looks around at the others again, at Gamora and at everyone else all standing just off the porch and at the massive spaceship across the street. His eyes are still wide, and behind them Peter feels like he can see the gears turning, like he can physically see his grandpa trying to realign himself in a reality where his long-lost grandkid is alive and there also happens to be a ton of aliens standing around his front lawn.

“Guessin’ it’s, uh… kind of a dumb question askin’ where you been all this time, huh?” he asks, his voice getting a little smaller, and he gestures with an awkward wave at the Benatar. “Outer space.”

“Yeah, um… yeah,” Peter stammers, shaking himself again. It’s like all that nervous energy from before built up and built up and built up until it popped, and now all that’s left is the other kind of nerves, the hyped up talking-too-fast kind. “Yeah, space. Sorry. Right. This is Gamora, she’s— well, she’s, uh—”

“I’m his girlfriend,” Gamora says so easily that it sends Peter’s whole face burning, and she holds out a hand for his grandpa to shake. “It’s wonderful to meet you, Mr. Quill.”

His grandpa clears his throat and then, sounding a little starstruck, takes her hand and says, “No, no, pleasure’s all mine. And George, please. None of that Mr. Quill nonsense.”

“George,” Gamora repeats with an acquiescent nod.

“Yeah, and uh, you gotta meet the rest of the family,” Peter adds, turning to address all the others, but then—

“Hey, Pop?”

Peter automatically goes tense at the sound of another voice coming from the house, his thoughts going from oh shit right he has somebody over to who the hell is this gonna be to wait she called him Pop so that means it’s gotta be—

“Uh, Marianne,” his grandpa calls back into the house, eyes still on Peter like he can’t quite look away. “Might wanna brace yourself there, hun.”

“What? Why?” comes the voice again, closer this time. “Who is it?”

And then, one of the tiniest women Peter has ever seen steps into the doorway. She pushes open the screen door to step out onto the porch. She can’t be more than five foot, probably less, which feels super disconnected from the big grown up Peter remembers flitting around his old kitchen back home. But she’s also got his mom’s blonde hair with only a few little flecks of gray in it, and she’s got his mom’s big green eyes behind a pair of glasses, and she’s got the same little crease between her brows that his mom always used to get when Peter did something stupid and he wasn’t totally sure if he was in trouble yet.

The screen door clacks against the frame. Her mouth falls open, wide eyes blinking at the scene in front of her.

“Pop,” she says. “There’s, uh. There’s aliens on your front lawn.”

His grandpa laughs a genuine, hearty laugh. “Sure are! Come on, you remember your nephew, don’t ya?”

“My…?”

The crease between her brows deepens, and her mouth is still hanging open as she looks from the spaceship to Gamora and then to Peter. It takes a few seconds for it to click, but it’s plain as day when it does. She brings both hands up to cover her mouth, and unlike Gramps, Aunt Marianne immediately starts crying.

“Oh, my God,” she whispers. “What?”

Peter’s crying again, too, because he’s already a little raw by now and he’s a sympathy crier, so sue him. But he still manages to offer a little wave, and he says, “Hey, Aunt Marianne.”

“Oh, my God.”

“Yeah.”

“Peter?”

“Yeah,” Peter laughs.

“Oh, my God.”

“Yeah!”

“Peter, holy shit,” she cries, wiping her eyes over and over again like it’s gonna make any difference. “That’s really— you’re— holy shit.”

She tries to fan her face to slow the onslaught of tears, fails at that, and then just shakes her head and all but rams her whole body into him with the force of a pint-sized bulldozer. Her arms wind around his middle and her face ends up planted in the center of his chest, and Peter half-laughs half-cries as he returns the hug, dropping his cheek on top of her head.

Her voice is barely audible when she croaks, “You got so big.”

“Yeah,” Peter says, swiping his forearm across his cheeks so he doesn’t get tears and snot in her hair. “You didn’t.”

She sniffles, loudly, and pulls away from him, hands still on his upper arms with a surprisingly strong grip. Her eyes are red-rimmed and shining, like she might start crying all over again if given even the slightest opportunity. “God, look at you. That’s really you. Same eyes and everything, huh? Jesus. Where the hell—? Did you really get abducted by aliens, then? Pop always said, but I never… Holy shit.”

“So, uh, Peter,” his Grandpa speaks up, gently rubbing Marianne’s back. “You said something about introducing us to the rest of the family?”

“Yeah! Right, uh, yeah,” Peter says, turning and looking around at the others. “Well, this is… Oh, boy. This is gonna take a minute.”

EARTH’S SOLAR SYSTEM

OUTER RIM FOUR MONTHS LATER

“It’s not that I don’t want to go—”

“You know, you say that, but it kinda sounds like you don’t wanna go, man,” Peter says, slinging a towel around his shoulders as he steps out of the Quadrant’s workout room. He’s plenty out of breath from his and Gamora’s sparring session, but behind him she’s still pumping away at one of the bots; he can hear the crunch and pop of metal giving way under her fists, and knowing her usual routine, she’s gonna be occupied for the better part of the next hour.

Loki rubs at his temple, still leaning against the wall outside the sparring room. “It’s not that I don’t want to go,” he oh-so-patiently repeats. “It’s that I shouldn’t.”

“Oh, yeah?” Peter asks. “Why’s that?”

Loki drops his hand, and the look he gives Peter for that says something along the lines of come on, dude, you know why, or whatever the hoity-toity Shakespearean equivalent of that would be.

“Humor me.”

Loki rolls his eyes. “Earth is… an admittedly tricky situation for me,” he explains, carefully. “You know that. The moment I step outside the boundaries of New Asgard, I’m essentially considered a war criminal.”

“Sure, to the Uniformed Nations, maybe.”

“Oh, my God, the United Nations—”

“Whatever, man,” Peter shrugs. “Who cares? Hell, we’re all legally banned from the state of Missouri—”

“And several others, to hear Mantis tell it—”

“— but we’re all still going anyway, aren’t we?”

He turns, gesturing with a tilt of his head for Loki to follow him down the hall, because if they’re gonna have this conversation, they’re gonna have it while Peter gets a little bit closer to being able to take a nice hot shower.

Jesus, he’s exhausted. Gamora never goes easy on him. Not that he’s really complaining.

“Y’know, you are invited, if that’s what you’re worried about—”

“I’m not worried about—”

“And like, specifically, you’re invited,” Peter cuts him off. “Gramps said he wants, quote, the ‘whole family’ to show up, including the ones he hasn’t met yet. That means you. He’s already heard you on the background of like, a million and a half phone calls anyway. And yes,” he raises his voice a tiny bit because Loki was totally about to interrupt, “he knows you’re the same guy that did the whole, uh… the whole ‘taking over New York’ thing, so. You know. No reason to worry about a cat that’s already outta the bag, I guess.”

“What a remarkably flippant allusion to the fact that I was directly responsible for the deaths of several hundred humans six years ago.”

“Hey,” Peter says, stopping and facing Loki. “Look at me, dude. Seriously.”

Loki does, even though it’s with his arms crossed and with a look on his face like he’s only barely willing to hear Peter out for more than a second.

“You feel bad about it?”

Very quickly, that barely-listening look drops away. Loki’s eyes widen just a bit. His grip on his own arms tightens. He opens his mouth to answer, freezes, and then huffs a sort of half- laugh that’s not really a laugh at all, like some answer had been cooked up in the back of his brain and then fizzled out before it made it even halfway up his throat.

It’s alright. It was a rhetorical question anyway.

“Look,” Peter says, softer now. “Everyone on this ship’s done stuff they’re not so proud of. A lot of people being dead ‘cause of you isn’t good, obviously, but seriously, it’s barely a blip compared to some of the stuff Nebula got into when she was still working under Thanos. Same with Gamora. Hell, even I got my ghosts, you know?”

Loki recovers, or appears to recover anyway, enough that he can scoff and ask, “Do you.”

“Sure do! But we’re not talking about me, we’re talking about you,” Peter says, poking him in the chest. “And I know if you… y’know, if you tried to take over a city or whatever, and you killed a bunch of people — or you let a bunch of people get killed, same difference — I know that wasn’t really you, man. Or maybe it was, but it’s not the guy you are now. It’s not the guy that saved a bunch of people in that subway station—”

“— still cannot believe Drax told you about—”

“— and it’s not the guy that almost died trying to help us save half the universe, and it’s not the guy that I’ve been living with for the last few months, alright?” Peter asks. “My grandpa knows you’re the guy from that invasion, yeah, but he also knows that if it weren’t for you I would’ve bled out on a forest floor on Uӓdar a couple weeks ago. So I’d say you’re doing alright in his books so far.”

“You told him about that, too?”

“Course I did! I talked you up, man. Sue me.”

Loki groans, dragging a hand over his face. “Do you not think that it might still be easier—”

“Nope—”

“I’m not finished. Do you not think it might be easier if I were to simply spend this time in Norway? I have to go there anyway to see my brother, so—”

“So, you and Thor can both head over there as soon as the barbecue’s done. Valkyrie, too.”

Loki stares at him. The beginnings of irritation are showing in the way he narrows his eyes, and in the impressively monotonous way he says, “What?”

“Well, obviously I invited Thor, too,” Peter says, making no effort whatsoever to suppress a wide shit-eating grin on his face. Got him. “C’mon, he’s your brother! Course I invited him. Told him to bring a plus one, too, and I’m guessing the terrifying but strangely cool warrior girl he’s obviously crushing on is gonna be the person he brings along, so.”

He shrugs, then leans in and whispers:

“You’re coming. No excuses, dude.”

And then he thumps Loki on the arm, spins around, and makes his way down the hallway toward his own quarters and a nice, hot shower.

There are times Peter can’t believe he was ever nervous about the fact that his grandpa had a whole big family aside from him and his mom. Seriously, now that he met them all during that emotional rollercoaster of a week a few months ago, he knows he never would’ve wanted it any other way. He can’t imagine his grandpa being all alone for those thirty-some- odd years without Aunt Marianne and her two kids, without Uncle George and his wife and three other grandkids to keep him busy.

Plus, it makes things a little more interesting when Peter shows up to the big family barbecue and nearly doubles the party size with one M-ship full of people.

The newly repaired entrance ramp thumps down on the dirt in his grandpa’s side yard, and the whole ship is instantly flooded with the familiar smell of grass and hay and, this time, burning charcoal and a whole lot of smokey food. Mantis and Groot go racing down the ramp first, and Peter follows quickly behind—

— only to be immediately rammed by what feels like a very furry miniature truck.

“Woah, hey!” he yells, laughing as he backs up a step.

This dog is not his grandpa’s dog, or at least not the one his grandpa had the last time they were here, calm old lady Daisy and her giant jowls and her speckled gray-brown fur and her paws the size of dinner plates. That dog, Peter realizes with a quick glance, is lounging in the grass way over by the house. This dog is a lot closer to what you picture when you think of a dog on Terra, smaller but by no means small, all golden-yellow and floppy ears and a crazy amount of energy. Enough energy, in fact, to follow Peter’s hasty retreat and jump up to put a pair of muddy paws on his chest.

“Gramps, did you get a new dog?” Peter shouts in the direction of the picnic tables a few dozen yards away.

“No, Pete, I didn’t— Roofus, down, boy,” his grandpa says, suddenly right next to him and smacking the dog on the butt until he hops back down to all fours. “That’s your Aunt Marianne’s dog. You didn’t get a chance to meet him last time.”

“Roofus, huh?”

“Roofus the doofus, yeah,” Gramps sighs, hands in his pockets, and he flashes a tired smile at everybody else.

“We’re trying to teach him not to jump on people,” his Aunt Marianne speaks up from where she just apparently breathlessly chased the dog right up to the Benatar’s entrance ramp. “Do me a favor and yell at him if he does it again, will ya? Hi, Peter.”

She tugs him down to her height and hugs him quickly but firmly around the neck, ruffling his hair as he pulls away from the hug.

“I gotta—” she gestures vaguely back at the house, still out of breath. “Rolls are in the oven, but I will properly say hi to everyone in a sec. Promise.”

“Ooh, I’m coming with ya,” Kraglin says, hurrying after her.

“He literally has not stopped talking about food since we got into the solar system,” Peter tells his grandpa, watching Kraglin disappear into the house with his aunt. “Let my brother get a few more drinks in him. Drax will prevail in time,” Loki says. Then, as an afterthought: “You’ll want to step in before one of them starts sparring with the Valkyrie, though, lest they accidentally deal some damage to the property.”

“Eh,” George shrugs. “I dunno, I think I’ll let Gamora handle that one.”

“I’ll let Gamora handle it,” Loki repeats, quietly, and George gets the impression he’s mocking him. And he’s proven right when Loki adds, “You and Quill really are related, aren’t you?”

George laughs again, louder this time.

He sorta thinks he can see it, maybe, how this weirdo thousand-some-odd-year-old God and his grandson get along so well, why he likes to spend so much time around such a rowdy bunch that spends half their time yelling at or making fun of each other. The Quill family’s always been something like that. This little offshoot of the Quill family, apparently, ain’t so different.

They fall into a silence again after that, but one that’s a little more comfortable than it was before. Just a couple of old guys sitting out here enjoying the quiet.

Until, of course, there’s a clap of thunder from back by the house.

“Well,” Loki says, standing up, “if you won’t step in to save your own property, I suppose I’ll have to.”

The party continues well into the night because, as it turns out, Midgardians have much the same penchant for these sorts of festivities as Asgardians do. It’s all very similar to Asgard, actually, all of it almost bizarrely familiar. (Well. If you threw in a few more clashing swords and about an ocean’s worth of ale and gilded all the walls in gold, at least.)

Not that Loki’s homesick. Not really.

It’s just… interesting, the similarities.

He’s not sure what time it is, but the sun has long since set over the horizon and the windows have gone fully dark by the time Quill’s aunts begin to ferry everyone back outside for what is evidently going to be a fireworks show that will, quote, knock all your gosh-darn socks off. Quill’s uncle and one of his cousins have apparently been preparing it for the better part of an hour.

Loki’s nearly at the door when Mantis sprints past him and barrels through the door herself, cackling wildly, quickly followed by Drax — Loki actually has to jump out of the way to avoid being knocked over — and then, of course, the youngest of Quill’s cousins runs shrieking at their heels.

“Oh, boy,” Quill’s grandfather sighs, watching as the screen door clacks shut. He’s in the process of digging a few woven blankets and folding chairs out of the closet next to the kitchen. “That girl’s really gotta lay off the sugar.”

Quill’s leaning into the fridge, getting himself yet another drink when he says, “I think Aunt Marianne cut her off a few hours ago, Gramps.”

“Oh, well, that’s good, at least.”

Loki takes another swig from his drink. Probably best not to mention that he’s been vanishing sweets out of the kitchen and conjuring them up in the girl’s vicinity every chance he’s gotten since her mother cut her off. He did tell the girl to take the secret to her grave, after all, and so far he has no reason to suspect that she won’t hold to their deal.

“Hey, let us get that,” Gamora’s voice comes from the kitchen entrance, and she effortlessly takes the two-foot-high pile of blankets that Quill’s grandfather has just stacked into his arms.

“Ah, c’mon, I may be old, but I can still lift a few blankets.”

“Gramps,” Quill speaks up, shutting the fridge door and handing him a beer. “You got a bunch of people here who can bench press whole ships. Trust me, you’re gonna wanna take advantage of that while you got it.”

Coming in from behind Gamora, Valkyrie weaves an arm through a row of metal folding chairs that were leaning against the wall and then hoists them all up one-handed — without spilling the drink in her other hand, of course.

“Honestly, we’re happy to help,” Gamora adds.

“Course we are,” Valkyrie agrees, and she slides a boot under the next pile of blankets, kicking them into the air at an angle so that they go careening across the kitchen and straight toward Loki. He manages to catch them with his one free hand, though just barely, and a bit messily, tossing the haphazard bunch of blankets over his shoulder to better hold them together.

Gamora and Valkyrie head out together, and once they’re outside, Quill’s grandfather asks, “Say, Pete, when are you planning to put a ring on that girl, anyway?”

Quill chokes on his beer.

“Oh, yes, Quill,” Loki says, grinning at his expense. “When are you planning on—?”

“Oh, my God, keep your voices down, will you?” he manages, still trying to cough the last bits of fluid from his throat. “Jesus. Obviously I’m gonna. I’m just, you know…”

His grandfather asks, “Waiting for the right time?”

Quill sighs and grabs the last pile of blankets from his grandfather’s closet, tossing it over his shoulder like Loki’s done with his own pile. “Waiting to figure out how the hell to do it, more like. ‘Cause look, it’s not like I’m not thinking about it, I mean, I’ve only been thinking about it since she finally agreed to go out with me,” Quill says, and he pushes the door open with his foot and gestures for his grandfather and Loki to go ahead of him. He drops his voice lower once they’re outside, lest he be overheard, though Loki imagines the music and the chaos of his relatives’ voices overlapping over each other is doing most of that job for him. “But I don’t wanna ask the human way, I wanna ask the Zehoberei way, you know? And I got no clue how that works. No one does. Aside from her, they’ve been basically extinct for about thirty-some-odd years, so it’s not like there’s anyone else I can ask.”

Loki pauses, turning to send him an incredulous look.

“What?”

“Quill,” Loki says. “I’m over a thousand years old. Did it not occur to you to ask if I knew?”

“Uh. Do you?”

“No,” Loki admits, turning forward again. “But I’m sure I could find someone who does.”

“Wait, seriously? Dude!”

“Shh. Later,” Loki shushes him, because there is absolutely no way they won’t be overheard by someone if they keep on about this, and also because he… doesn’t actually know who he’d ask anyway. He’s got some ideas where to start, though. Maybe. Hell, nearly every resident of New Asgard has been around since well before the near-extinction of Gamora’s species; surely one of them might have done some traveling in that time.

And if not, there’s always the Asgardian libraries, or what’s left of them.

They reach where everyone else has gathered for the fireworks show, a good fifty yards or so from the house itself, just past where the yard itself ends and the empty field begins. Quill tosses his pile of blankets toward the ground— but Loki rolls his eyes, and the blankets all pause in midair, hovering and glowing with magic, before he casts a spell that sends each blanket neatly drifting to the ground until they’re all laid flat. He does the same for his own pile, too, snapping his fingers as he does it.

“Show off,” Quill murmurs, but he’s smiling when he says it, and he weaves around the various members of each of his respective families until he finds Gamora, flopping down to settle in beside her and throwing an arm over her shoulders.

His grandfather has found a folding chair at the far end of the crowd, already sitting, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees and talking to his daughter and one of his other grandchildren.

Loki settles down right where he is, at the back of the group, but he’s only sitting on his own for about five seconds before Thor joins him, groaning theatrically with a hand on his stomach as he sits down.

“My God, I don’t think I’ve ever eaten that much in my life.”

“That is patently untrue,” Loki tells him, and Thor barks a laugh.

Valkyrie settles in on Thor’s other side, and soon the thump of Drax’s footsteps announces his approach from behind as well—just before he circles around Loki and falls back on his arse on one of the blankets with all the grace of a drunken Bilgesnipe. There’s a rumble as Groot joins them, too. Then Mantis, hand in hand with Quill’s six-year-old cousin and beaming with delight, her antennae glowing, comes and sits in front of Loki.

Over the general cacophony of everyone talking to each other at once, Loki says without looking away from the silhouettes of Quill’s uncle and cousin in the distance:

“Groot?”

“I am Groot.”

“Where is Rocket?”

“I am Groot.”

“Oh, that can’t be good,” Loki says, as all of them look straight toward the field where Quill’s uncle and cousin—and, now that he’s paying attention, a vaguely raccoon-shaped silhouette as well—are all gathered around the box of fireworks.

“Should we…?” Valkyrie starts to ask, but she’s interrupted by the first pop of a firework going off and soaring up with a whistle into the night sky.

Loki sighs, prepping the spell before it’s quite needed. The firework climbs up and up and up like a needle and then, with a reverberating boom that rattles all the way through the ground, it explodes directly above them in a fiery spectacle that blooms across nearly the whole of their field of view, dazzling red and white and sizzling yellow. A shield, barely perceptible in its semitransparence but just slightly greenish against the black of the sky, bubbles up above them and blocks the worst of the drifting sparks and debris. Just in case.

And it’s a good thing, too, since that initial explosion ends up being the first of very many. He loses count after about twenty, after which they begin to overlap, a display of controlled chaos that nearly hurts the eyes but is still somehow impossible to look away from.

Eventually, once he’s certain that the shield will stop Quill’s grandfather’s lawn or any of his relatives from literally catching on fire, Loki relaxes, accepts the (fifth? sixth?) drink that Valkyrie hands him, and cracks it open.

Might as well enjoy the show.
