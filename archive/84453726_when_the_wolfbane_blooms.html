When the Wolfbane Blooms

Lucille roamed the valley in the center of her cattle herd. The cows trusted her and kept up with her pace. She swung a large walking stick as she kept moving forward with the cows. Then, the cows gave pause as each of their ears twitched and Lucille paused with them to look around at why the herd had gone still on her. She could not see anything in her view as the valley remained nearly empty for all except them but then something out of the corner of her eye moved in the grass. She snapped her head in the direction and squinted against the mid-day sun but saw nothing but the bull next to her shifted on its hooves; it saw it too.

“Easy,” she muttered as she reached over and patted its head. 

Her heartbeat quickened with the thought of something being wrong if the bull too was spooked. Again, something out of the corner of her eye moved in the grass toward her. For a moment she swore it was just a shadow being cast wrong by the sun or a cloud but then it moved toward her. She could make out a shape that was long and dark brown, maybe black and low to the ground as it pulled itself forward. The thing in the grass halted in its movements and gradually rose onto its hind legs. This was all wrong to Lucille, she had never seen something like this before. The cows all began to shift around her in fear while the two bulls in the herd stomped into the ground doubling down on their position in front of Lucille. Lucille froze in utter fear when she saw the beast turn and look directly at her. It charged forward as it dropped down onto all four legs with teeth snarling. Lucille let out a sharp scream and began to run and the cows scattered in fear. Lucille turned her head around to look behind her and saw without a doubt in her mind, a wolf, gaining on her. Just as it got into a position of a leap, the two bulls charged at it from the sides as they aggressively jabbed at it with their horns.

She lost her footing and fell hard onto the ground and fell hard enough to rip her stocking at the left knee leaving a bloody cut. She got back onto her panicked feet and continued to run. She again turned back to see where the beast was and when it tried to leap at her again, the bulls held it at bay. Lucille, seeing that the bulls are keeping her safe, halted in her run and took one last look at the beast. She saw it looked like a wolf but its limbs were too short, the curve of its spine when it was on all fours was warped, its mouth was too wide and too long from the few wolves she had seen in her 12 years.

One of the bulls finally slammed its horns into the beast. The contact gave a loud crack and the impact caused the beast to lose its footing, twist sideways, and fell hard enough to cause an indent into the earth. When it got back up, its limbs folded in strange directions before they snapped back into place. The bulls didn’t give it time to try to attack again and charged the beast with such force that they kicked up dirt into the air. The beast was pushed back toward the forest tree line as it snarled at the bulls. The snarl did not sound like a wolf to Lucille; it was lower and too deep. Lucille covered her ears from the noise. 

With one last swing from the bulls’ horns, the beast ran back into the forest having given up on attacking her. The bulls stood guard for a moment before they wandered back to Lucille. Once they came to her she broke down into tears and pulled both in for a hug as she cried as she looked behind her and saw the rest of the herd across the valley waiting for her. Lucille took one last look at the forest tree line and began her walk toward the rest of the herd as the bulls stayed behind her as she walked forward. Even with the bulls standing guard behind her, she still found herself glancing back but the beast never came back out of the forest.

Once she got home and got the cows back onto their pasture, she ran to her father to recount what had happened. He was confused as there were no wolves in the countryside. That was the whole point in them moving here. Lucille let out a miffed groan. He wasn’t fully grasping the situation of it, almost getting her. Just as she was about to clap back with a response, far off into the distance a howl was heard. Both Lucille and her father stopped in their steps and looked toward the forest.

“That wasn’t a wolf,” he muttered.

He said nothing more and turned to look at his daughter in worry. Finally to Lucille, he understood what had happened. By morning, everyone in the village knew what had happened to Lucille. On her walk to school, she knew people were whispering about her. She felt everyone’s eyes on her; some were curious on what truly happened in the valley while others remained skeptical over the events.

“Girl’s clearly shaken up,” one voice said.

“She saw a shadow, end of story,” another said.

“Bulls don’t charge at shadows,” a reply was heard.

Lucille knew better and kept her head down as she walked. She held her schoolbooks against her chest tightly. Every time she closed her eyes, she could still picture it in all its detail: the way it stood on its hind legs and the way it looked at her. Lucille crashed into a woman on the street when she was too in her head to notice. She was knocked down and was helped back onto her feet by the woman. Once Lucille got back up, she looked at the woman.

“You’re the farm girl,” she commented.

“Yes,” Lucille said simply.

The woman held Lucille’s books out to her. Lucille grabbed them and held them back against her chest. “How close did it get? If you don’t mind me asking.”

As Lucille went to answer the question, a sharp screech of a scream cut through the village square. Everyone turned their heads all at once toward the sound as it came again and everyone began to move toward it, including Lucille. As the screaming continued, Lucille moved down a narrow side road that curved toward a group of cottages that sat along the forest tree line. By the time she reached the place where the screaming came from, a crowd already formed outside one of the cottages. There were too many people pressed together trying to see what was going on without taking a step forward. Lucille moved forward and pushed her way through. Someone grabbed her arm to stop her but she broke free and continued to walk forward.

The door to the cottage hung barely on its hinges. The wood cracked inward as if something forced its way in. Inside there was blood, too much of it as it painted the floor in dark wavy streaks that just began to soak into the wood flooring. More was splattered across the walls in arches. Lucille found the pattern strange and turned her head and saw a girl lay near the doorway. Her breath caught in her throat as she saw that the girl couldn’t be much older than her. The girl’s body was twisted and mangled with one arm bent beneath her still gripping the floor with her fingernails, as if she tried to crawl away. Her dress was practically missing as it was too shredded. Lucille’s eyes drifted to the girl’s head—the wounds weren’t like that of simple blade cuts, they were torn at and her head barely hung onto the body with just a thin layer of skin being what kept it attached. Something had bitten and shook this girl, tearing her apart. Lucille was whisked away from the body and led out of the cottage and led her back to the village square.

“Who did this?” someone in the crowd asked another.

“Not a who, but a what,” someone answered back.

Her stomach churned at the thought that it could’ve been the same beast. The village deacon arrived and as he crossed the threshold, his robes immediately became stained with blood. He observed the scene before he made his way to the girl’s body and crouched next to it. His jaw tightened.

“Animal,” someone called out. “It has to be.”

He rose to his feet and looked at the crowd. “No,” he simply said. “This is no ordinary beast.”

Lucille tore away from her guide and ran up to the deacon. “I saw it!” she yelled at him. “In the valley yesterday.”

Everyone went quiet and all eyes were on her once more. The deacon motioned for her to continue.

“It stood up on its hind legs,” she continued. “It rose up and looked at me.” A few members of the crowd scoffed.

The deacon’s gaze stayed on her longer than the others. “And you are certain of what you saw?” he asked.

“It tried to attack me. It would have succeeded if I didn’t have my family’s cattle with me.”

The deacon looked at her once more, absorbing what she said. Someone ran to the crowd and to the deacon. He was struggling for breath and grasped at his knees bending down as he steadied his breathing. “They found another!” he announced.

“Where?” he demanded.

“Just up the south road,” the man replied.

The deacon moved to follow the man and everyone got back to the village square. Villagers brought back the second victim and Lucille saw them pass her by with a stretcher that had someone laying on it fully covered. The sheet that wrapped the body was completely red with blood. Lucille tried to block out the sight and continue her walk to school, she didn’t need to look at this one. She already knew what was going on, as did everyone else in the village.

“Two in one day,” one said.

“It’s hunting,” another said.

The deacon stood at the steps of his church observing the villagers. His expression was unreadable as the body was taken inside. “This is a trial!” he announced. “A test of our faith!”

A few villagers nodded at his words while others looked unconvinced.

“We will hold a vigil for the two girls tonight,” he continued. “No one is to walk alone after sunset, doors are to be locked and windows barred or shuttered.”

“What about that thing in the forest?” someone demanded. “What are we supposed to do about that?”

The deacon’s gaze flickered to the tree line in the distance. For a moment, something uncertain crossed his face before disappearing. “We pray,” he stated.

That night, the village did not sleep. Candles were burnt in every window, homes were locked up, and voices kept low. Lucille sat on the bed and stared at the thin beam of light coming from under the door. Her father was heard wandering the house checking all the windows and doors as he paced in worry. That’s when Lucille heard it again, the howl from the forest. By morning, no one spoke of doubts anymore, everyone heard them that night.
