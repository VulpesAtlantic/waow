and therefore is love said to be a child

Because Agatha Harkness’ fellowship still lived by the old ways, by their grandmother’s ways, Agatha was not to meet her betrothed until the day of their handfasting. Because Agatha Harkness’ mother was a bitch, there was no circumventing this rule.

Of course she’d tried bargaining (“If it’s a man, mother, if it’s a man, I’ll kill myself. And then who will bear your grandchildren?” “Oh, Agatha, I’ll find somebody.” “Somebody? Somebody??”), but her mother only pretended to divulge when she was trying to vex Agatha as punishment for half-assing her chores (“Just so you know, Agatha, it’s old man Liscovitz,” “No it isn’t, even you wouldn’t—” “Oh, but yes it is. Perhaps if you were better at sweeping the floor I would have picked Violet Shaw—Agatha, for God’s sakes, get away from the window.”)

Agatha never understood this peculiarity of their shared life. They did not teach these things in school because they expected them to be the purview of one’s parents. Agatha, however, had no father and but one mother who at least once a week got so deep in her cups that she would lock Agatha out of their hovel. By accident or not, who knew.

And so her first stop for information, at age 10, was Ms. Spalding, who owned the butcher’s shop above which their hovel sat. Ms. Spalding had an abundance of pity for Agatha and on the nights when she would catch her shouting up to her mother’s window from the muddy street below, begging her to let her in, she would bring her into the shop and make her a bed on the floor of the back room and feed her jerky scraps like a stray dog.

That was fine. Sometimes it was better to be a stray dog than it was to be Agatha.

“It’s about vanity,” she’d said, sitting on the floor beside Agatha’s nest and tearing a piece of jerky off with her teeth. Ms. Spalding was probably too young and pretty to be Ms anything, but Agatha didn’t want to take any chances with her manners and end up sleeping on the street. “You don’t choose your match, the fellowship chooses for you. Based on what’s best for the community.”

“Yuck,” said Agatha, “That just doesn’t sound very nice. Why can’t you know who it is until that day?”

“I think that part’s just theatrics,” admitted Ms. Spalding. Not helpful in the slightest. “Somebody did it that way once a very long time ago and it just stuck. Lots of things are like that.”

“What if my mother picks for me and he’s horrible to look at?” Agatha asked. She was burrowed in her blankets and peering up at Ms. Spalding very seriously. It was helpful that Ms. Spalding did not say your mother would never do that. It made Agatha trust her more.

“Then you’ll close your eyes,” said Ms. Spalding, also unhelpful, and when Agatha grimaced: “Baby sister, you are a child. You have years to worry about this.”

“Auntie Lilia says I’m old for my age.”

“Auntie Lilia might be right about that,” she paused, finished her jerky, and dusted her hands off on the front of her skirt, “Come to think of it, Agatha, I think I have something that might help.”

She stood and spent a moment rustling around the room, moving papers, opening drawers. From her place on the floor, Agatha watched her skirt move around her calves. When Ms. Spalding knelt again, she had a book that she passed to Agatha.

It was thick and leather and that leather was carved with all sorts of things. Flowers and bells and people dancing. The front of it just said Recipes.

Agatha frowned. “What is it?”

“It’s a book.”

“A book of what?”

“A book of everything,” Ms. Spalding sat back on her calves, “That’s how we keep knowledge in the fellowship. We write it down, it gets copied, the copies get passed around.”

Nobody would accuse Agatha of being a great reader, but Recipes had a few things going for it that other books did not. Firstly, it was illustrated; every page with a border of flowers, a picture of people dancing, a few penises. It wasn’t always pretty, but it kept things interesting.

Secondly, it went nowhere you expected it to. There was a recipe for buttermilk biscuits, a dress pattern, a medicinal brew for a colicky baby. When Agatha finally found what she needed, it was in between advice on when best to plant green beans (boring) and a fertility treatment for livestock (oddly titillating).

The section on women’s handfasting was pages long and written partially in verse, partially in an ancient, non-english language, and partially in short prose. There were drawings of birds and fruit trees and women in all stages of undress.

Near the very end, there were steps to a ritual. Illustrated above the ritual was a bird carrying a handkerchief with a fat, rosy-cheeked baby in its beak.

As an editorial note, it was true enough that some things in Recipes were real and some just gave people false hope (the blessed salve that Beatrice Fletcher used to try and clear the blemishes on her face, for instance). But this ritual was real. Agatha may not have fully believed it at the time, but some years after her conversation with Ms. Spalding, Liddell Newton would marry her wife, a woman with the most harrowing blue eyes Agatha had ever seen. Eyes like she was looking right out the back of your head. And she’d be damned if every single one of Liddell’s children didn’t have those eyes, poor bastards. The evidence was sparse but persuasive.

If Recipes were to be believed, women had been loving each other for centuries to great effect. One of the more bone-chilling stories had been about two of them marrying and having a dozen children. Harrowing as a concept, but in the illustration, all fourteen of them were smiling.

Agatha kept the book on her side of their apartment and read it at night by moonlight while her mother slept. She held the text very close to her face and put a finger to the words.

“To be born of two women on the first full moon night of a union is a blessing unto the child and unto the fellowship,” she murmured to herself, “And such a child is a bounty with no equal.”

Attached to this page was a long list of the perks of such babies that folded out like an accordion book. Apparently, locks of their hair were effective in love potions and you could till their nail clippings into soil for a most fertile harvest. There were also some more evocative pictures of a few of the ways you could go about making such a child.

She tore these out and stuffed them under her pillow. Ms. Spalding, she thought, would not miss them.

Next was Auntie Lilia, an easier mark by miles than Ms. Spalding. Lilia was clairvoyant—as another editorial note, this was also real magic, not fake. Agatha could not divine shit but she was a natural born grifter and Auntie Lilia thought her acting skills good enough that Agatha could take over the shop once she’d decided to retire. This was as good a life plan as Agatha was ever likely to muster for herself, and so she swept the floors and cleaned the windows and apprenticed there.

From the storefront Auntie Lilia read tarot and tea leaves and ran a minor curiosities business. Three eyed frogs in jars and the like. She also kept a racket of cats there, always rustling around in the background while they worked.

Back then, Lilia still had some brown hair mixed in with the grey. The top half of her left pinky was gone and had been replaced by a wooden prosthetic. She told a different story every time somebody asked her about it. Her pointer and middle fingers were permanently stained yellow with tobacco.

She did not say anything when Agatha skipped school to work at the shop and she told Agatha everything she asked. About the business of the babymaking ritual in the book, she said this:

“Well, there are many sociological reasons for the human urge to procreate—”

“Horseshit,” said Agatha, sitting at the tarot table and polishing the crystal ball. That was what she said whenever Lilia was horseshitting her. She was maybe fourteen.

Lilia lifted a brow, her expression somewhere between laughter and reproach. “Way back when, we used to be just like everybody else. Men and women, men and women,” She waved a hand, reclined in an armchair in the corner of the room with an orange cat purring contentedly atop her lap, “But then, oh, maybe five hundred years ago, there was a plague.”

“A plague?” Said Agatha, breathless.

“A plague.” Said Lilia. “Be a doll and roll me a cigarette.”

Agatha jutted out her bottom lip, “I want to hear about the plague.”

“You will when I’ve got my cigarette.”

Agatha rolled it on the tabletop with skill and ease. By then, she could have rolled a perfect smoke during an earthquake. She passed it over to Lilia who lit it, inhaled, and coughed.

“Where was I?” She croaked.

“The plague.”

“Yes, right. Well, it only killed men, believe it or not. Now, that ritual has been around since time began but nobody ever found much use for it because you only get a very small window to perform it,” she paused to pick a bit of tobacco from her tongue and flick it off into the room. “On your handfasting night, before midnight. What’s the use in all that trouble, if you can make a baby all on your own?”

“Not much.”

“No, little sister, not much. However, around the time the men were dying and our numbers were dwindling, some very industrious women started to wonder if they could use the ritual to their advantage. Two women get married, they do the ritual, and there you go. Any old full moon, you could get pregnant.”

Agatha wrinkled her nose, “Just from existing?”

“You need to have sex to get pregnant, Sugar.” Lilia said, and held the cig in her mouth to make the requisite hand gesture. “Maybe I ought to encourage your schooling after all.”

“I know that.” Hissed Agatha. “Even girls?”

“Even women, yes, and our foremothers apparently had quite the time for themselves way back then. Repopulated the whole fellowship and then some. And now, of course, we’ve made a big stink about it,” smoke billowed out of Lilia’s nostrils. She seemed lost in thought for a moment, “I appreciate the ceremony of it all, but it always seemed a bit much.”

“The book says babies with two mothers are lucky. You’re supposed to grow things out of their clipped toenails.”

“Horseshit,” Auntie Lilia said, and emitted a mighty cough. She’d smoked her cig down to a nubbin, “Some of the worst people I ever met in my life had two mothers. Not that I ever had to worry about it.”

There were other inquiries. Agatha had been told, once, that while the ritual had to be done before midnight on the handfasting night, it was most effective at midnight, but she considered that an old wives tale with no real merit. She had also been told that the sturgeon moon was the best for it and such was why all weddings happened in the dead heat of August. On that point, Agatha had no fucking idea, only disliked that it made her think of fish and sex in the breath.

At a certain point, maybe around age sixteen or seventeen, she warmed up to the idea. Agatha was ashamed to admit it, but it was true. Warmed up to it because she saw Liddell’s blue eyed children and suddenly the wisdom of her foremothers dawned on her clear as day. It would not be so bad to have babies that looked like her and the person she loved all smooshed together. Not at all.

Dark hair, dark eyes, perhaps teeth just a little crooked. A real family, not whatever mess Agatha had been born into. Nobody screaming or locking each other out of the house. That was an idea that a person could hang their hat on. An idea that could carry somebody through a day, a month, a year.

Her mistake had been mentioning the fantasy to Lilia. It was a Tuesday, and raining hard, and Agatha was ditching school because she didn’t much feel like going. Rio had turned up outside her bedroom window that morning, hair curling with rain, and thrown rocks at it. They’d talked for a little while, Agatha hanging out and gazing onto the street, Rio calling up to her. Rio had looked so beautiful trying to cajole her into going, like a prince in a story.

She told Lilia as she dusted the books that she had become quite at peace with the idea of getting married, as she figured if she skipped a few Yuletide gifts and was extra diligent with her chores her mother might work to match her with—

“Agatha,” Lilia cut her off. Her voice was serious, and sad, and it pierced through Agatha’s rambling like a needle popping a balloon. Her thoughts of Rio’s gently curling hair. Agatha turned and found her sitting at the tarot table, tapping her wooden pinky against the surface, “Come sit for a moment.”

Agatha didn’t want to, but she did. Lilia had been practicing a spread and the cards laid out in front of her, “What’s wrong?”

“Listen to me well,” Lilia said, her face drawn, “You are not going to marry that girl, no matter how badly you want it.”

Those words sat between them. Agatha never felt embarrassed, as a rule, but to her horror she felt a hot shame crawling through her stomach. How stupid the idea sounded when Lilia said it out loud. How girlish. “Then I won’t get married, like you.” Lilia’s face softened, which was unimaginably worse. Agatha looked away, down at the wood grain of the table, “I know what you’re going to say.”

“Ah, so you’ve accessed the gift.”

Agatha flicked her eyes up to her, irritated, “You’re going to say that you are from a good family and I am from a bad one and so you can do whatever you want but I can’t.”

“Agatha, child, nobody envies you,” said Lilia. Her demeanor softened and she looked on Agatha with an unwelcome fondness. “You do not have the luxury to be frivolous, or foolish, or to live in a fantasy. And that’s what this is. It’s a fantasy.”

Agatha had just assumed, at that time, that Lilia knew. Lilia was a knower by profession. She assumed that Lilia had peered into the wide open window of Agatha’s life and seen the road twist and turn and thus had also seen the end of it.

Some people were born for happiness, for families and babies, Agatha was not. Afterward, when she saw women cooing over fat infants she thought of them as silly. Touched. If asked, she would deny that she had ever seen such a future for herself.

She and Violet Shaw only spoke of it once. Through many sweaty encounters, they did not broach the subject of marrying one another until the summer of Agatha’s twenty-third year, laying out beside the old grist mill in the hot glow of the July sun.

Violet shoved her skirt down and Agatha wiped her hand against the grass beneath her. “You should consider mentioning it to your mother,” Violet said, sitting up and adjusting her braid. She had that hair that looked either tawny or ginger depending on the light or the day.

“Mentioning what?”

“Me. Marrying me.”

“Marrying you?” Agatha squinted over at her, “Why would we marry each other? I thought you liked Acer.”

“I do like him,” Violet turned to her, grinning. One of her right molars had been replaced with a silver tooth that glinted when she smiled. Agatha could still taste the metal under her tongue. “But I like you just about as much, and my mother thinks that women should marry other women if they can help it at all. She’s a bit old fashioned.”

“I guess it doesn’t matter, does it, what either of us wants.” Agatha said.

“Agatha,” Violet gave her a look like, are you serious? It ground Agatha’s post-sex high to pieces, “I mean, technically you don’t meet your spouse until the handfasting, but it’s not like it doesn’t matter at all what you want. Unless your parents are tyrants you’ll typically get to—” She cut herself off, perhaps finally understanding her words, or registering Agatha’s expression. Her smile softened, “—anyway, I don’t think your mother would object, would she?”

“My mother would marry me to a tree stump if it got me out of her house,” Agatha paused, “We’ll have to have children. They’ll be redheaded.”

“Maybe not.”

“My grandmother was a ginger.”

“Maybe so, then.” Violet lay down beside her, “I’m sorry in advance about our redheaded children.”

“Hm.” Grunted Agatha. She squinted up at the sky. It was almost too beautiful a day for them to be discussing such things. “Maybe I just won’t get married, like Auntie Lilia.”

“Auntie Lilia has three sisters, does she not? All married?” Violet said this with a sort of mock flippance, “And you have only your mother.” Agatha was quiet for a long time, scratching at the knuckle of her pointer finger. “I know that I wouldn’t be your first choice, but you should give it some thought. I think we’d have fun.”

“Fun? We’d be churning out babies from the first night. No more time for this sort of thing.”

Violet snorted. “Nobody does it on the night of anymore. You just fiddle around while everybody’s at the party if you really want to and then wait until the next morning. How embarrassing—to get pregnant first thing. Like you couldn’t have waited, or something.”

Agatha rolled onto her side facing her, “Violet, I really don’t want to talk about it anymore.”

“You’ll think about it?”

“I’ll think about it.” She agreed.

Agatha did mention a passing interest in Violet to her mother that night. From then on, she’d assumed that it would be Violet, who was an agreeable match in every way but one unspeakable one. She was good tempered to a fault. Quick to fun. A dog with an ever-wagging tail. So unlike Rio, who’s favor was hard won and who liked things in the exact opposite way you’d expect her to (starting with the things she liked the most, like spiders, frogs, etcetera, ending with the things she liked worst, like the color red and cakes with too much sugar).

Agatha was trying to compare Violet to Rio less, so she pushed those thoughts to the back of her mind when she had them.

Sometimes she wished that she could peer into Lilia’s crystal ball and see it all. Her redheaded children. Violet’s silver tooth glinting in her smile. Maybe it would hurt less for it to play out in real life if she could just prepare herself.

But then, of course, maybe it was for the best that she couldn’t. For a clairvoyant, Auntie Lilia hated ruining surprises for people, and what ended up happening at Agatha’s handfasting would come to be the subject of much awe and gossip, and, hundreds of years later, a PhD thesis on socioeconomic mobility in agrarian communities.

Because it was not Violet Shaw who knelt at the altar with Agatha on that day, although she would have made a sensible match. Nor was it old man Liscovitz, nor was it anybody else. It was the person who made the least sense. It was the person that Agatha had woken up that morning dreaming of. It was you are never, not ever, going to marry—

“Rio,” Agatha whispered, hands clasped in front of her while the Reverend Mother prayed over their bodies, “Is that really you?”

“Agatha, please.” Her mother hissed from behind her. Agatha craned her head and tried to deliver a stink eye, which was difficult, because she was kneeling in prayer to the holy mother and because she wore a ligneous crown of branches and flowers heavy on her head.

People had been whispering about her lately, more than usual, and now she understood why. She turned her head to try and steal a glance at Rio, to check again that it was really her, but Rio wasn’t looking at her.

Agatha pursed her lips. She hadn’t been able to get Rio alone for two weeks—and holy mother knew she’d tried. Stood outside her bedroom window. Searched for her in her favorite spots. Delivered gifts to her house that she thought Rio might like—a salamander’s tail, a pleasing stone from the riverbed. And now—well, they weren’t alone, per se, but—

“Rio, I want to say I’m sorry,” Agatha whispered. “About your garden.”

In front of them, the Reverend Mother cleared her throat. Agatha glared. Wretched old hag.

She’d known Rio for twenty-five years—which was a long time, but especially long if it was the entirety of one’s life, which it was for Agatha—twenty-five years in which Rio had grown handsome, and suitably tall, and rightly featured. She had carried her baby face with her on into adulthood. It suited her, though it would have made some other woman look dull, or stupid.

When they were children, looking at her used to madden Agatha to the point that she had to act out. Wiping charcoal covered fingers over the fabric of her dress. Pinching her when they stood next to one another to be counted before lessons started.

“Ow, Agatha,” Rio would say, rubbing her arm, dark eyes growing wet, “Why’d you do that?”

And Agatha would peer over at her with complete honesty and say, “I don’t know.”

As adolescents, Agatha was relieved to realize that this violent streak was only attraction. By then they were friends of a certain caliber. They spent all their time together despite their wildly different temperaments. When Agatha was seized by the urge to do something to Rio—to push her into the grass, to bite her, to pull her hair—she could wrap herself into her arms instead, and half resist the urge to grind against her like a rutting animal.

Rio was a soft touch. Her own doctor had once called her “sensitive” and “prone to bouts of melancholy”, accusations that Rio had denied to Agatha through tears. She needed a little persuading to come around to things—mischief, for instance, was never something she conjured on her own, but always something that she enjoyed once put to it by Agatha.

Not very much later, Agatha had come to terms with the fact that what she felt for Rio might be worse than attraction. It seemed to only grow larger in the face of its own impossibility. It did not matter that Agatha was never, not ever going to marry that girl. There were not five consecutive minutes in a day during which Agatha was not thinking of her.

Somebody more civilized than she may have left it as it was after being told by a clairvoyant that there was no hope of a happy ending. Agatha, however, had been expertly schooled in settling for what little she could have. Those who could not afford the meal became talented on filling up on scraps.

She’d talked Rio into practicing kissing with each other (fun, and then a disaster when—well, the outcome didn’t bear repeating). When they sat next to each other at the lunch table and Rio let her knee press into Agatha’s knee, Agatha would wrap her foot around her ankle. Once, in a fit of true desperation, she’d tucked a bundle of mugwort tied together with her hair under Rio’s pillow.

Over the years she had come to know Rio’s idiosyncrasies and even to love them. Rio had once gifted her an entire rabbit skeleton she’d found in the woods and shown her, on the floor of Agatha’s bedroom, how to assemble it back into the ghastly visage of the creature it once was.

(Sometimes, in the nights to come, when she could not sleep, Agatha would remove the bones from the box under her bed and place them together. Piece by piece, each one making her think of Rio, like a talisman, a fetish.)

The next day, Agatha had reciprocated by pinning Rio down in the woods and licking the side of her face, chin to hairline. Rio had squirmed and endured this. Then she’d said, “Do the other side, too.”

Which is to say, she never fully took the bait. But she did hold Agatha’s hand underneath the dinner table when Agatha ate with her family. Sometimes, when Agatha returned home afterward, she would remember the feeling and start to cry, like she was the sensitive one.

Rio did not always understand the breadth of her own capacity for cruelty, if she understood that she had it at all.

When they put their hands together to be bound by the bolts of fantastical silk ribbon, Rio’s palm was sweating. While there had been several moments throughout the surreal day in which Agatha thought somebody was going to announce the horrible mix up that had just occurred—no, Agatha, your handfasting is across the way—it was then that Agatha realized that it was real. The touch of her damp skin. Rio was just as nervous as she was.

That night, they lit up the stave church so bright that it could be seen across and through the deep, dark wood. Agatha had watched Liddell put candles in jam jars until her fingers bled, back when she had no idea who her betrothed would be, back before she had any reason to think it would be Rio Vidal of all people.

The celebration had obviously been subsidized by Rio’s family. There was food and wine flowing like they’d never known a shortage of it. There was dancing and revelry for all. There was Agatha and Rio, sat out amongst the birch trees at a table of woven branches receiving gifts from the fellowship. One by one they came in the hot midsummer night, bringing fruits, bringing meats. There was even a goat tethered to the table, by the end of it.

Rio held herself with the posture she used when she was answering a difficult question in class. Everybody at the celebration wore identical green, linen garb, distinguished only by their embroidery, done special for the event by mothers and grandmothers and sisters.

There was white thread all down Rio’s shapeless frock, adorning the buttons, the collar. Mostly runes, some shapes. The best of it had clearly been done by her mother, but Agatha could see the places where her brothers had tried to contribute. A lopsided star. A long-stitched mushroom. Her hair was up in a crown of such intricate braids that Agatha could scarcely see where it began and where it ended.

Agatha’s dress had no embroidery, and she wore her hair down loose around her shoulders. She touched the collar of her frock self-consciously, not that it mattered. Rio was paying attention to everything at the celebration but her.

Every time she snuck a glance, Agatha’s heart sped up. She was not the world’s best student nor had she been paying very much attention to the ceremony, but Agatha was pretty sure that she and Rio had just sworn at the altar to have sex and make a baby or several together, with the strong implication that they would begin that evening.

Ordinarily, this would be good fortune such that Agatha could hardly comprehend. Relationship legitimized in front of the fellowship and the church, she would no longer have to settle for simply trying to covertly despoil Rio’s virtue in the hope that it would take some of the razor edge off her desire. She could be despoiling it in a completely above board and church sanctioned manner in the three or so hours they had before they cruised into the accidental pregnancy window.

But, as she was wont to do, Agatha had ruined her own fun without even realizing she’d done it.

Oh, fuck it all to hell. If she hadn’t been so stupid she could be having increasingly risky sex as the night progressed. Heavy petting at bare minimum. Instead they were sitting at the table, feet apart, not looking at each other.

It wouldn’t have felt so dire, maybe, if it had ever happened before. The odds of marriage aside, Agatha could never figure exactly why she wouldn’t just take advantage of what was being offered to her. She would have let Rio, the bringer of rabbit skeletons, do disgusting, terrible, sacrilegious and extremely premarital things to her if she’d only had a mind to ask.

They’d come close, once. A Sunday afternoon in August, swimming at the lake. Rio brought them a basket of grapes and cheese and tried to get Agatha to read poetry and it was not until years and years later that Agatha came to wonder if the poems weren’t supposed to be for her specifically. They’d been eighteen, and only just, so she wasn’t thinking about it like that then. Rio had always outpaced her by miles in matters of the heart, like understanding poems, or music, or getting teary eyed when watching the sunset. Agatha only cried at sunsets when she thought about how Rio must feel to see them.

After they swam they went back to Rio’s house to nap before dinner. Rio said the same to her mother as they went up the stairs, hair wet, dresses clinging to them in places, “Agatha and I are going to nap in my bed.”

Rio’s mother, from her place talking to a maid in the kitchen, said, “Leave your door open.”

This put Rio into a minor tizzy. She kept saying, “Leave the door open—why should we leave the door open—” Like she was offended by the implication that she and Agatha could be going upstairs to have sex. Her neck was red. Agatha wasn’t offended, or surprised, because that’s pretty much what she’d been angling for. She said nothing on the subject.

They lay in their underdresses in Rio’s bed, door wide open, under the thin top sheet. The sounds of the kitchen filtered up. From the open window above the bed, the sounds of the outside filtered in. It got hot, Agatha got dozy. She lay on her back and Rio curled on her side around her, like an affectionate cat.

She was halfway to sleeping when she felt Rio’s hand on her stomach, over her dress. Her eyes fluttered open and she rolled her head on the pillow until her nose was brushing Rio’s nose. Rio’s eyes were open and she was watching her. “Can’t sleep?” Agatha murmured.

“No.” Whispered Rio. Then her fingers began to move, just so. Just gently. Agatha exhaled through her nose. For a moment, it almost lulled her back to sleep.

“That feels nice.”

“Does it?”

“Mhm.”

The motion continued. She was rubbing a circle around Agatha’s navel, like Agatha was a dog. It moved the fabric of her dress around; pushed it up, tugged it back down. Sleep disappeared from her mind.

“Is this helping?”

“What?”

“Is it helping you sleep?” Agatha nudged Rio’s nose with her nose and smiled. They were being very quiet.

“No. I don’t know. Is it helping you?”

“No. It’s not making me feel sleepy.” They locked eyes, Rio’s hand paused on her stomach. Agatha thought for sure that she’d ruined it from dancing a little too close to the line. But then Rio’s hand moved again, down to the hem of her dress, then underneath it. Back to her stomach, resting on naked skin this time.

Agatha’s eyes flashed to the open door. She watched for a moment, then tilted her head back to Rio’s. “You can move your hand,” she murmured, and hummed when Rio did. Rio’s fingers drifted over the peachfuzz around her bellybutton, soft at first, then with a little more intention. Agatha thought about all the ways she’d seen Rio touch things; gathering sticks for a fire, holding a chalk piece, patting the haunch of a horse.
