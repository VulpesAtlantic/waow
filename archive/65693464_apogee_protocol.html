Summary

NASA’s newest long-duration deep space mission requires a new kind of compatibility. Commander Agatha Harkness is perfect for the assignment in every measurable way. Except for the part where she needs a partner.

Enter Rio Vidal: decorated pilot, alien truther, and Agatha’s ex.

In space, no one can hear you pretend it’s not real.

Six Months Prior

Agatha supposed it was entirely possible that the site of the NASA complex at Cape Canaveral was chosen explicitly because anyone with the

misfortune of spending a summer in Florida would be all too eager to leave the planet entirely.

The beach was slightly redeeming. It was nice until the tourists arrived. The tourists were loud, sunburnt, and constantly surprised by the existence of alligators. About the time they cleared out—late August, usually—that’s when the hurricanes came to visit.

Agatha preferred the hurricanes.

She’d stock up on beer (buying water was for suckers), sit on her back patio, and watch the rain come in sideways sheets. The power would cut out eventually, and she’d light candles, crack open a bottle, and wait for the storm to speak.

As the eye passed overhead, she’d stand barefoot in the grass and stare up at the strange, yellow stillness of the sky. Looking up into the expanse, it felt like being watched by some eldritch entity too old to care what it was looking down upon. Then the next band would hit and leave no question as to what forces were in charge.

She’d always loved that—how small a storm could make her feel, how it shoved everything into perspective. You couldn’t argue with a hurricane. Couldn’t reason with it. You either held your ground or you didn’t.

On her second space flight, she’d watched one form from orbit spiraling over the Atlantic, a soft white pinwheel of annihilation. From that distance, it looked delicate and perfect.

She remembered pressing her forehead to the glass of the observation window, watching that hurricane. It looked so small from up there. She could blot out the whole Earth with her thumb if she wanted to. Just like that, humanity became background noise.

But the storm remained.

Even from orbit, the chaos didn't go away, even when she floated so high beyond the stratosphere she could pretend she was untouchable.

People liked to say space gave a person proper perspective. That floating above the planet made one feel small, humble, awed, and fragile. It reminded one how lucky they were to be alive, to be tethered to something warm and blue and breathing.

Agatha already knew how small she was. How easy it was to drift out of frame, and to stop being missed.

Unlike some of her cohort, she didn’t need low-Earth orbit to teach her humility.

She’d learned that a long time ago. In the things people didn't say and the way silence filled a room after a slammed door. In how long it took anyone to call her back when she left town. How quickly the calls stopped coming at all.

Agatha didn't need the enormity of space to humble her. The boundaries of her time spent on Earth already had.

All her trip beyond the marble did was give her the quiet to admit it.

If she had not been brought into existence as a human, perhaps the energy of her would have made a very fine storm.

The thing no one mentioned to her about hitting her forties was that it felt a lot like standing in the eye of a storm. She knew what was behind her. She could feel what was coming. But there was this quiet in between, this pause that could make someone think maybe they’d finally earned a little peace.

Agatha didn’t believe it. Not for a second.

Her life had always been measured in extremes. She wasn’t good with stillness. Itchy feet, her mother used to say, in the rare moments she bothered to try and sound maternal. Couldn’t stay put if someone nailed her down.

Agatha had left Florida behind like something was chasing her. A whole ecosystem of soggy front porches and gas station microwaves, of sawgrass that sliced her ankles open and mosquitoes that didn’t care who they feasted from. A landscape held together with mildew and misfortune, where every summer brought a new local headline that began with Florida Man and ended with something involving a machete and a Publix parking lot.

She couldn’t get out fast enough.

When she allowed herself to fantasize about what her future could look like, she dreamed of clean lines. Of glass and chrome and city grids that followed the rational laws of geometry. She dreamed of a skyline made of steel, not of humid haze and heat lightning. Buildings tall enough to block out the sun. Somewhere the cockroaches didn’t fly and the people knew how to shut the hell up in elevators.

College had been the first ticket out. She didn’t care where it was—so long as it was somewhere that had seasons apart from: hot, hotter, rainy, hot again. Her acceptance packet had come, and she didn’t even wait for graduation. Just started packing.

She arrived in Boston with a duffel bag, a laptop with a cracked screen, and a chip on her shoulder the size of the Everglades. No friends. No fallback plan. Just the cold, stubborn certainty that she could build something better than where she came from.

Agatha had been right.

But these things take time to develop.

Her undergraduate years were a blur of lab reports, skipped parties, and precisely two and a half catastrophic relationships. She hadn’t planned to study physics.

Initially, she’d enrolled in chemistry, mainly to be able to use her major as a pickup line. But then one night, drunk and angry, she’d gotten into a screaming match with a smug little bastard in the quad about whether nuclear decay was something that could be considered beautiful.

She'd switched her major out of spite, and at the end of the semester, she'd earned top marks in the program.

It suited her. It was precise, demanding, and mostly solitary. A field that required both discipline and the willingness to crack something open just to see what made it tick. There was no room for fluff. Just pressure, risk, and consequence, neatly calculated and occasionally explosive.

And it made people incredibly nervous, which was a massive bonus.

Something about a volatile person taking far too much of an interest in unstable compounds put people on edge.

Agatha enjoyed that immensely.

She liked the way lab partners flinched when she was assigned to their group. Loved the way certain advisors danced around the word “intimidating” and substituted “highly independent” instead. Agatha thrived on being the smartest person in the room and making sure everyone else knew it.

She didn’t mind being underestimated at first. It made the aftermath that much more satisfying.

Let the boys call her difficult. Let them joke that she was half a miscalculation away from detonating. Let them ask, behind her back, who let a hurricane into the lab.

Agatha would smile and remind them that hurricanes have names.

And the ones named after women? Statistically caused more damage.

Because they weren’t taken seriously.

People didn’t evacuate. They didn’t prepare. They thought, subconsciously, that a storm with a woman’s name sounded softer. Gentler. As though it might knock over a few lawn chairs, rattle the windows a bit, and then move along.

And so they underestimated it.

They underestimated her.

Agatha found that fascinating. And then she found it useful.

She leaned into it. Let them believe they had her figured out—too sharp, too cold, too much. Let them think they could wait her out, wear her down, outpace her.

But Agatha would never be a storm you waited out. She was the kind which reshaped the coastline.

She went to grad school partly for the prestige, partly for the access to research-grade reactors, and partly because it gave her another four years to avoid the crushing logistics of adulthood.

After graduation, she took a commission in the Navy—not because she felt especially patriotic or because it looked good on a résumé, but entirely because she liked the idea of being unreachable for months at a time.

They put her on a nuclear submarine for a nine-month deployment. It was silent service. There was no sunlight. There was no surface. Just pressure and darkness in every direction.

She’d never felt more at peace.

Agatha didn’t see the sun for 263 days and didn’t miss it once. The dark didn’t bother her. The pressure didn’t bother her. She’d been under worse kinds, inside lecture halls and faculty boardrooms and beds she didn’t want to leave in the morning. She’d survived her mother.

It was terra that unsettled her, not the expanse of great perpetual nothingness above or below.

Earth was loud. It pulsed and shouted and demanded attention. Earth was unpredictable in the worst ways, demanding small talk, eye contact, and the kind of emotional calibration she had never mastered and never cared to learn.

Everything on the sub had a purpose. Every alert and sound was necessary, and each hour, minute, and breath was accounted for.

Agatha understood that.

NASA had called not long after she resurfaced.

She liked to think it was because of her publications, her record, her absolute refusal to lose her cool during a fire drill in the reactor compartment.

But it was probably the same thing that always got her into rooms she wasn’t supposed to be in: Agatha Harkness didn’t fucking flinch.

Not when it counted.

Agatha had flown four missions in her career. Three that made headlines, one that didn’t. Each one had taken something from her—a fraction of bone density, a chunk of her left knee, a few more miles of emotional distance she didn’t bother reclaiming upon reentry.

Her last flight had been scheduled for launch two years ago. A new orbital research station had been commissioned and needed a pioneer crew to build it out. It was the kind of mission that would’ve punched her ticket for lead commander in whatever came next.

Her gear was packed, and her body was prepared. She’d done the physicals and sim drills and completed all necessary clearances in triplicate.

Then, without warning, the rug was pulled from under her. The mission was canceled, and the project went private.

Budget cuts, resource reallocation, something about the optics of sending a late-thirties astronaut who avoided press releases like the plague back into orbit when the public was clamoring for a new generation of space cowboys.

They didn’t say it that way, of course. They couched it in diplomacy and press statements about future priorities.

But Agatha had seen the writing on the hangar walls. Space tourism was the wave of the future. Big checks, flashy names, and marketable smiles.

She wasn’t marketable. She was just good at her job. And as it so often goes, far too often in this world, that wasn’t enough.

For quite a while after, she’d spiraled in a way she didn’t have language for.

Not a breakdown. Not exactly. She still got up in the mornings. She still answered emails. She still showed up to briefings in pressed uniforms.

But something essential had slipped loose inside her.

It was like a drift. A slow, grinding float. Like she’d been cut from the tether and was still waiting for the moment when someone realized she wasn’t anchored anymore. Waiting for the recall code that never came.

Agatha kept herself busy because busy was safer than stillness. She ventured into the speaker circuit. Did some private consultations. She lent her name to grants and early-stage propulsion studies and talked to undergrads who didn’t ask questions she hadn’t already answered a hundred times before.

She started listening to a lot of Bowie—Space Oddity on repeat, Blackstar when she was feeling maudlin, and Starman when she indulged in whimsy. Sometimes, she let the songs play on loop while she stood at her stove, letting the sound fill the silence like air hissing in a pressurized cabin.

There was a sharp, familiar comfort in it: the slow unfurling loneliness of floating too far from the ground, the ache of a voice calling out for connection across an empty black sky. She understood it in a way she never had when she was younger. She knew exactly what it meant to radio home and get only static back.

She wasn’t grounded.

She was flung out into orbit, with no planned descent.

But in the quiet hours—in between 3 a.m. insomnia stretches and muted footage of storms rolling across the Gulf—she’d started to wonder if that had been it. Her last launch. Her last time up.

Maybe this was how it ended for Agatha Harkness. Not in a crash, not in a fiery explosion... just sent spiraling adrift. A long, slow arc into obscurity. One last loop around the third rock from the sun while the next generation came up smiling and camera-ready, with straighter teeth and fewer scars.

She’d almost made peace with it. Nearly convinced herself that she could learn to live in the quiet, that her last contribution would be reviewing other people’s flight logs and signing off on simulations that she used to lead.

Almost.

Then came the whispers. A new program, a deep space mission with a record-breaking duration, psychological endurance under extreme conditions... Not the kind of mission that could be spun into an eight-minute puff piece.

It was a journey that would take years, not weeks. Historic. Guaranteed legacy.

Agatha knew she was perfect for it.

Nearly.

She met every qualification. Top of her field. Elite flight record. Zero mission failures. She’d already updated her will. Not because she was pessimistic. Just practical.

The only thing she didn’t have, the only line left unchecked on the endless goddamn form, was the name of her partner.

Apparently, being the best wasn’t enough anymore. It wasn’t sufficient to navigate a multimillion-dollar vessel through, calculate orbital mechanics in her sleep, or spend months in silence without cracking.

Now she had to prove she was emotionally resilient, too. As quantified through the narrow, saccharine lens of a society that viewed romantic relationships as the ultimate marker of wholeness.

Her worth in her profession was now tied to whether she had someone waiting for her at home. Whether she could love and be loved in return. As if compatibility could be measured in percentages. As if intimacy had ever been something she could put on a spreadsheet.

NASA wanted a partner. A documented, cohabitating, psychologically bonded other half.

A test of humanity, disguised as protocol.

Agatha had always scored highest when the tests were mechanical. Cold. Predictable.

This was some bureaucratic litmus test for softness. A glorified personality quiz wrapped in the language of “stability,” “predictive cohesion,” and “interpersonal durability.”

They didn’t care if she was brilliant. They wanted to know if she was palatable.

NASA’s newest obsession was wrapped up in the name Apogee Protocol— originally a working title, but the PR team had sunk their claws into it, and now it was on mugs, briefing folders, and recruitment videos set to swelling music.

Apogee —from the Greek apogeion , “away from Earth.” The point in orbit where a satellite object is farthest from the planet around which it revolves.

Apparently, now it also meant “let’s trap a handful of emotionally entangled astronauts in a glorified tin can and study what happens when they can't escape each other.”

They wanted to know what love looked like at apogee—when you were the farthest you could be from everything familiar, and still expected to hold each other together.

Agatha understood the scientific reasoning. She even admired it, in theory. Long-duration missions couldn’t survive on procedural checklists and technical aptitude alone. Humans needed friction and comfort. Reassurance that they weren’t just meat and brain matter flung across the void in matching jumpsuits.

She understood it. But that didn’t mean she had to accept it.

She’d stared at the requirement for an hour. She’d re-read it like it might change. It didn’t.

Agatha Harkness. Professionally ideal. Personally disqualified.

Unless…

She took another sip of her beer and let the carbonation burn on her tongue.

There were worse ideas. Terrible ones, actually. But one kept circling back, no matter how many times she batted it away. Like orbital debris that refused to burn up and became space trash instead.

Rio.

It was a solution. It was also a problem.

They had history, tension, and chemistry—all the messy little building blocks of a believable story. Their names had appeared together in reports, simulations, and mission logs. There were timestamps, photos, and evidence of time spent trying to make something stick between them.

Agatha had been too sharp-edged, too quick to retreat before she could be found out as someone who carried a gentle heart under her bristles and barbs. Rio had been too calm in the way she never chased what slipped away.

But they’d tried. More than once.

So how hard would it be, really, to give it a go one more time? For science.

This mission needed a partner who met the paper criteria for the powers that be. Good compliant subjects for their latest, buzzworthy social experiment.

Beyond that, she didn't underestimate the intensity of the planned mission. Agatha needed someone she could trust in deep space—someone whose brain moved fast under pressure, someone who could match her in silence and was unafraid to fire back over the din, someone who wouldn’t crack if she didn’t sleep for three days, if the reentry plan got scrambled, or if she lost her temper and said something she couldn’t take back.

And Rio?

She was the type of person who never flinched, either.

Agatha told herself it was purely practical and strategic for both of them. They'd always had that in common, their hunger to wrap their hands around what else might exist beyond what was.

But some part of her knew the truth.

Even in this scheme, in this deception, there was a truth. She hadn’t thought of anyone else.

Just Rio.

The cursor blinked at her in slow and steady judgment. Agatha glared back at the screen, and slammed the backspace key again.

She’d written five versions of the message already. None of them were usable. One had veered into legal language. One had sounded like a breakup text. The most recent had included an itemized list of pros and cons, which was so detailed that even she had to admit it made her look unhinged.

She drummed her fingers against the edge of her desk.

What could she even say to someone like Rio, who’d always been too bright, too sharp, too much for a medium like email? An exceptional pilot, rumored to be a die-hard friend, and personally known by Agatha to be extremely good with her hands.

She opened a new draft.

Subject: Apogee Protocol Opportunity

Too formal. She deleted the subject line and tried again.

Subject: Hypothetical Scenario

That was better. Neutral. Vague. The kind of subject line that wouldn't raise eyebrows if someone happened to glance over Rio’s shoulder. Not that Agatha cared who Rio was spending her time with these days. Not that she’d thought about it.

Agatha stared at the empty body of the message. Started typing.

Hey. I’m aware this is out of the blue, but—

No. That sounded like she was apologizing. She wasn’t.

So, I’ve been monitoring the updates on the new initiative and—

Delete.

Rio, I have a proposal.

She paused and considered it. Technically true.

You meet the flight criteria. I meet the mission criteria. We both know I’m not the sort of person who asks for help unless it’s the only option left.

Which was... too honest in a way that made her chest feel uncomfortably tight.

She deleted the entire thing again.

Agatha knew she should just ask her in person. But walking into the simulator bay and starting a conversation with “Remember when we last slept together in that hotel outside Huntsville a couple years ago? I have a professional favor to ask,” felt a little too on-the-nose.

Agatha pushed back from the desk and paced the length of the room, bare feet whispering across the tile. She could still feel the faint pulse of panic under her collar—the same slow thrum that had settled there the moment she saw the eligibility requirements.

She wanted this mission. Needed it. Not for the prestige, not even to prove a point anymore.

If a star expands enough, it can punch through the boundary of gravitational influence known as the Roche lobe.

A teardrop shaped boundary of containment. Defining the space in which one body can hold its own, separate from the pull of another. But if a star swells—grows too hot, too vast, too much—it can spill over. Gas and plasma slip past the edge and begin to transfer to the other body. A one-way overflow. A quiet, inevitable collapse into shared orbit.

That was the thing about Rio.

She’d never quite broken Agatha’s gravity. She’d just grown luminous and unpredictable until there wasn’t a boundary left between them that held.

And Agatha, for all her systems and safeguards, hadn’t stopped her.

She didn’t know if she could sell the lie, or if Rio even wanted to be part of it.

But she did know one thing to be absolutely true.

If she was going to be trapped in a capsule for two years—spinning farther from Earth than anyone had ever gone—she didn’t want someone boring beside her.

She wanted someone who made her expand.

If there was anyone who would say yes to a plan this reckless, impossible, and almost plausible, it would be Rio. Not because she necessarily believed in the scheme.

But because they were both cats who hadn't yet lost all their lives to curiosity. Agatha knew that Rio would want to see what would happen if they tried.

Agatha returned to her desk, opened a new message draft, and typed one sentence:

I’ll be making rounds tomorrow. We should talk.

She stared at it for a long moment.

Then she hit send before she could think better of it.

The next morning, the Sim Bay doors slid open with a hydraulic hiss, and Agatha stepped through.

It was too cold in the bay.

The climate was kept frigid to mimic the interior conditions of a shuttle: cooler heads were said to make better decisions. Agatha privately suspected it was just one more test of endurance. Everything at NASA was.

She spotted Rio immediately.

She was draped across the pilot’s seat of the Type-X simulator like it was a recliner at a dive bar—boots propped on the console, headset cocked slightly askew, head tilted back in the direction of the overhead light. Her hair had only gotten wilder since she’d cut it short. It curled rebelliously at the nape of her neck. Her tie was loose and askew, and her sleeves were shoved up to her elbows. Rio also had the audacity to continue whistling off-key rather than acknowledge Agatha’s entry.

Agatha cleared her throat.

Rio didn’t move, but a slow smile danced its way across her face. “Hey, early bird,” she drawled, still watching the ceiling like it had something more interesting to say. “I figured you’d need at least until lunch time to rehearse your pitch.”

Agatha rolled her shoulder and stood a little taller, which was impressive, given how rigid her spine already was. “I didn’t come here to pitch anything.”

“You say that,” Rio said, finally turning her head toward her, “but you’ve got an impressive record of only deigning to speak to me when you need something.”

“I speak to you when it’s appropriate Vidal,” Agatha replied icily.

“Mm. Sure.” Rio swung her boots down and sat up with that feline grace Agatha had never been able to manage herself.

“So what’s today’s occasion? Dumping an intern on me? Delivering a budget memo?” Rio gave a mock gasp and clapped a hand over her heart. “You’re finally here to admit I was right about that thing we saw in Albuquerque that was definitely not just a drone?”

Agatha narrowed her eyes. “If it’d been an actual ufo, I wouldn’t have passed up the opportunity to send you back to your home planet.”

“Yeah, well, I keep phoning home, but it seems like I keep dialing wrong numbers,” Rio laughed, leaning back in her chair. “You know, you could just say you missed me. It’d be faster than whatever this is.”

She absolutely would not be doing that.

Agatha exhaled through her nose and stepped forward, clipboard in hand, like it might shield her. “That new mission is moving forward.”

That got Rio’s full attention trained on her.

She sat up straighter, expression sharpening into an intrigued grin. “Oh?”

Agatha nodded once. “They’re implementing the Apogee Protocol.”

Rio let out a low whistle. “Couples only, huh?”

“They want social data,” Agatha said crisply. “Long-term bonding in a closed-loop ecosystem. Conflict resolution under duress. Cohabitation strain. You get the idea.”

“And you immediately thought of me?” Rio grinned.

“I thought of someone with flight clearance, emotional discretion, and a proven track record of surviving prolonged exposure to my personality.” Agatha paused, not quite meeting Rio's eyes. “So yes. I thought of you.”

Rio tilted her head, suddenly quiet, then leaned forward, forearms braced on her knees. “So this is what? A fake marriage pitch? One last dance among the stars?”

Agatha bristled again. “It’s not marriage. It’s a professional alliance.”

“With free government-sponsored couples therapy built in and a mission patch that probably has hearts on it for the media,” Rio added dryly.

“It’s something that I thought would appeal to you,” Agatha huffed. “Not… that stuff. The science bit. The risky parts. You’d get to fly a fully outfitted capsule beyond the L2 orbit point. You’d be part of the first human trial of sustained cohabitation off planet. I thought maybe that would appeal to your scientific curiosity, which I seem to recall used to be one of your more intriguing traits. Or your ego. Which I enjoy encouraging less. But here we are.”

Rio studied her before smiling. “You know what happens when two celestial bodies get too close, right?”

Agatha met her gaze evenly, fighting every urge to roll her eyes. “They stabilize.”

“Sure. Sometimes. Alternatively, they tear each other apart.”

Agatha didn’t blink. “Well, I’m betting on the former.”

Rio sat back, grinning still. She seemed at least a little impressed. “Okay then, what’s your scheme?”

Agatha blinked. “You mean my plan?”

“You’re asking me to sign up for a mission that only accepts established couples. As in: long-term, documented, emotionally compatible, nauseatingly cohabitated… bonded pair do not separate.”

Agatha couldn’t stop the eyeroll this time.

Rio’s voice fell into a puckish, teasing cadence. “How exactly are you planning to explain that to the board? ‘Oh yes, we’ve actually been married for years, we just decided to keep things a little spicy by pretending to hate each other in public’?”

Agatha’s mouth flattened into a tight pressed line. “We wouldn’t be the first couple in NASA history to keep their relationship under the radar.”

“No, we wouldn’t,” Rio said, eyes dancing, “but you’re not known for being especially subtle in your disdain for me. You think anyone in Flight Ops is going to believe we’ve been secretly together this whole time?”

Agatha crossed her arms. “Why wouldn’t they? We’ve worked together. We trained together. There’s precedent.”

Rio let out a low laugh. “Agatha, I remember you once referred to me in a staff debrief as, and I quote, ‘a second rate pilot with a god complex so big it creates its own gravitational orbit.’”

“That isn’t true. I would never call you a second rate pilot. I called you a first rate dick,” Agatha grumbled.

Rio chuckled again—bright and amused and maddeningly unbothered as she swiveled her chair back and forth. “You’re such a romantic.”

Agatha ignored her and kept going. “We’ve spent enough time together in the past to build a plausible timeline. There are joint assignments we can reference. Conferences we were both at...”

Rio finally stood, pacing a slow circle around the simulator console. “Okay, so what’s our cover?”

“We’ll just say we got back together,” Agatha said before she could stop herself.

Rio paused mid-step.

“Back together,” she repeated, more softly now.

Agatha lifted her chin. “It’s the most believable option. Everyone already knows we… had history. It won’t surprise anyone that we tried again.”

Rio turned, leaning her hip against the side of the simulator. Her arms folded. “Remind me, Agatha. How many ‘try agains’ have we had at this point?”

Agatha’s mouth twitched like she was trying not to frown—or admit anything at all. “Three.”

Rio raised an eyebrow. “That’s generous.”

Agatha hesitated. “Two serious. One… ill-advised.”

“That was your idea,” Rio said, voice deceptively breezy.

“It was an unexpected international layover,” Agatha snapped. “And need I remind you, you booked the very last room at the airport hotel.”

Rio’s smile softened a fraction as she shrugged. “That was still one of our better nights. Wouldn't you say so?”

Agatha didn't trust herself to respond to that.

Instead, she adjusted the angle of her clipboard like it might give her an excuse to look somewhere else. “We’ll tell them we reconnected after your promotion to lead test pilot. That we’d been in touch on and off, and finally decided to stop pretending we weren’t still... admirers.”

Rio let out a thoughtful hum. “You mean emotionally, or just that you like the way I fly?”

“Statistically speaking, you do have one of the lowest error rates in the program.”

“So, definitely the flying then.”

Agatha shot her a look, but the edge was dulled by the faint flush climbing up her neck.

Rio pushed off the console and took a measured step forward, reaching forward and running her thumb down Agatha's lanyard. “If we do this, we’re going to have to get our stories straight. Make it believable.”

“I’ve started a list already,” Agatha said.

“Of course you have. What's our anniversary?”

Agatha winced but nodded. “We’ll need to pick one.”

Rio’s voice dropped to something quieter, private even though it was just the two of them in the room. “Do you still remember the first time?”

Agatha didn’t move.

Then, carefully, she responded, “The briefing room. You were late.”

“You were furious.”

“You said I looked pretty when I was mad.”

Rio grinned and tugged the lanyard once. “Still true.”

Agatha exhaled, the sound halfway between a sigh and surrender. “Let’s not romanticize it. We lasted six weeks.”

Agatha stood at the edge of the platform in a neoprene suit, helmet tucked under one arm, her other hand curled too tightly around her air pack strap.

Her breath already felt too loud inside her own head. Or maybe that was the blood rushing in her ears. Maybe if she had a spontaneous nosebleed they’d let her try again another day.

She hated water. She hated tanks. She hated how the moment the mask went on and the weight settled on her shoulders, she started hearing things. Not voices. Only echoes. A door locking from the outside. Damp tile under bare feet. The taste of copper and chlorine. The waiting silence before a scream.

“You good?” came Vidal’s voice from just behind her.

Agatha’s posture didn’t change. “Peachy.”

“Your knuckles are white.”

She loosened her grip just enough to pass inspection. “There’s spotters all around here. I’m not afraid of drowning.”

Rio’s voice dropped lower. “Well, in my experience, it’s not the fear of drowning that gets in your head during these things. It’s the thought of being alone in the dark when it happens.”

Agatha bit her tongue to keep from scoffing. What did a girl from Texas know about the dark?

She could picture it easily; Rio’s kind of dark. The kind only found out west, sprawled across acres of open land with nothing around to soak up the sky. The kind that comes after the long lazy glow of a sun that always seems to be setting. A silence so big it felt peaceful. Endless.

The kind that allowed you to see and count all the stars in the sky.

She’d grown up in the Sunshine State and the irony wasn’t lost on her. Her childhood had been wall-to-wall daylight, in theory. But she knew the dark well.

Knew the way it crawled in through the windows when the power cut out. The sound of her mother flicking off every light bulb to keep the AC running without tripping the breaker. The silence of a room that was hot and still and waiting.

She remembered the hush of the house during a blackout that lasted four days. No generator. No candles after nine. Just a battery radio squawking about grid failures and a hurricane that’d settled over them like it meant to stay.

Agatha remembered counting her breaths under the sheet she’d wet in the sink in an effort to stay cool, trying to make her body small enough not to sweat, not to cry, not to be noticed.

So no. She didn’t need Rio Vidal to tell her what it meant to be alone in the dark.

She pulled on her mask and gloves and followed the instructor’s signal to descend.

It was supposed to be a standard EVA sim: entry, orientation, minor mechanical corrections. Fifteen minutes at most. But time did something strange under pressure. And the suit felt heavier than usual. Her hands found the tether. Her legs adjusted.

The helmet fogged. The hiss of her oxygen took on a warped rhythm. Her breath rebounded in her skull, loud and too fast, like it was trying to get out. And somewhere deep in her bones, something old remembered what it meant to be shut in and forgotten.

Agatha clutched the bar and tried to count. Five seconds in. Hold. Seven seconds out. Just like the therapist taught her. One more breath. Then another.

Her glove slipped a little and body jolted in panic, a silent kick that sent her drifting sideways into the edge of the mock hull. Her shoulder hit the frame and the impact reverberated through her ribs.

She tried to right herself but couldn’t find the tether.

She couldn’t breathe.

“Harkness, you’re drifting...” the rest of voice in her calms was lost to the pulse of her eardrums.

“I need...” she gasped, but the word garbled in her throat.

“Pull her,” came a different voice.

“Copy that. Assisting.”

The safety diver was already moving, but someone else reached her first.

A gloved hand on her wrist. Steady pressure. A second hand landed on her on her shoulder, guiding, not dragging. Rio’s visor was inches from hers, eyes wide behind the shield, lips forming something slow and deliberate.

With effort, Agatha focused.

One breath. Then another.

“Agatha. Follow me.”

Rio’s gestures were calm, practiced. She didn’t crowd. She didn’t pull. She waited until Agatha nodded, then led the slow ascent.

The moment the surface broke, Agatha tore the helmet off. She pushed it back hard enough to clatter against the platform. Her hands braced the metal edge and she coughed, dragging air like she was learning how for the first time.

To her surprise, no one laughed. It was worse than that. No one said anything at all.

Rio hauled herself out beside her and crouched down, eyes level, not touching.

Agatha didn’t meet her gaze.

“I was fine,” she rasped.

Rio didn’t argue.

But she stayed right there, close enough to catch her if she swayed.

Agatha hated that part most of all.

She stripped off her gloves with shaking fingers and let them fall to the platform with a wet slap.

Behind her, the rest of the sim team went through the motions of reset and teardown. Nobody said anything. No teasing. No pointed looks. Just the mechanical rhythm of people pretending not to notice.

That silence? It was worse than laughter. Worse than pity.

It was charity.

Agatha knew the shape of it too well. The forced discretion of people who didn’t know what to say to someone who’d just cracked open. She’d seen it in pews and hospital waiting rooms. Heard it in the lowered voices outside confessional doors.

She straightened her spine. Shook out her hands. Tried to force the memory of her mother’s voice from her mind, the way it hissed like steam whenever she showed weakness. Don’t you embarrass me.

“I can drive you,” Rio said, quiet.

Agatha blinked. She didn’t even remember Rio standing up. “I’ll walk.”

“You’re in a wetsuit.”

“Then I’ll change. And walk.”

Rio didn’t push. Just nodded, like she knew the fight wasn’t really about the ride.

Still, when Agatha finally left the locker room dressed, hair damp, eyes dry, Rio was leaning against the wall by the exit, hands in her pockets.

No smugness. No teasing. Just that steady, unreadable look that made Agatha want to claw something open.

“I’m taking you home,” she said simply.

Agatha opened her mouth, ready with a deflection, a dagger, a joke.

But then Rio reached out. Not touching, but close enough that Agatha could feel the heat of her palm.

“Come on. Let’s get out of here.”

And maybe it was the leftover adrenaline. Maybe it was the quiet hum of sincerity under Rio’s words. But Agatha let her.

She let her drive.

She didn’t speak the whole ride. Just leaned her head against the window and watched the light blur past, a kaleidoscope of sodium lamps and tail lights smeared against the night. Her breath stayed shallow. Her hands stayed clenched.

When they pulled up to her place, Rio shifted in her seat and said,

“You did fine. Everyone gets the yips sometimes.”

Agatha didn’t look at her. Just unbuckled her belt with slow, careful fingers.

“I didn’t,” she said.

Rio didn’t argue that, either.

She wanted her to. That’s what they did. They’d traded barbs and competing test scores for over a year now. And Agatha knew how to handle that. She was no stranger to having a rival.

But silence? That unnerved her. Especially coming from Rio, who usually couldn’t keep her mouth shut when there was an opportunity to poke the bear.

Say it, Agatha thought, jaw tight. Say something about me choking. About how the pressure got to me. About how I’m not cut out for this after all.

But Rio didn’t. She just shifted in the driver’s seat, quiet, composed, like she knew what Agatha was trying to bait her into and had no interest in taking the hook.

That made it worse.

“I have beer,” Agatha said, too loud. Too clipped. Her voice flattened. “Come in.”

A challenge dressed up as an invitation.

Rio glanced over, the corner of her mouth tilting like she’d heard every unspoken note behind it.

“As you order, Captain.”

Agatha snapped her head toward her. “Don’t call me that.” Her voice was sharp. “Not when I haven’t earned it.”

Rio blinked, then nodded once, slowly. The smirk softened. Not gone, but tempered. She reached for the handle and got out, boots crunching the gravel.

Before Agatha could react, her door swung open.

Rio stood there, still holding it.

“Isn’t that the goal?” she said, with a tilt of her head. “To earn it?”

Agatha stared at her.

It would’ve been easier if Rio had mocked her. Smirked like usual and said something cocky and dismissive so she could snap back and rebuild the distance between them.

Instead, Rio’s voice was gentle in a way that pressed against all the wrong bruises that she had no way of knowing were there.

The wind tugged at Agatha’s damp hair. Her shirt clung to her back. Everything felt too close to the surface.

“Whatever you think you saw down there,” she said as she stepped out, “keep it to yourself.”

Rio stepped back to give her space but didn’t flinch.

“Sure,” she said. “But just so you know... what I saw was someone who fought like hell to stay calm. Even when she didn’t feel it.”

Agatha scoffed and pushed past her. “You don’t know anything about me.”

She meant it like a dismissal. An ending. But Rio just shrugged and followed a step behind.

“Not yet,” she said.

They sat in silence for a while on the back patio, surrounded by the ambient hum of the neighborhood. Somewhere, a sprinkler trickled back and forth at a steady clip despite the HOAs water restrictions. The occasional buzz of a passing mosquito announced itself and moved on.

Agatha took the more weathered of the two vinyl chairs. One of those aluminum-framed things with sun-bleached straps and a rusted joint that creaked ominously when she shifted her weight. She didn’t trust Rio not to lean the wrong way and snap it in half, and she didn’t feel like being judged for her shitty patio setup on top of everything else.

Rio, predictably, sprawled in the sturdier seat like she owned it. One leg kicked out, the other hooked loosely over the side. Her beer rested in the triangle of denim between her thighs, label untouched.

Agatha had already peeled halfway through hers. She scratched at a corner with her nail, not looking at Rio. The bitter tang still clung to the back of her throat. She hadn’t eaten dinner. Hadn’t planned to share the evening, much less the contents of her fridge. Not that there was anything in there worth sharing. A bruised peach. A half-empty bottle of sriracha. A Tupperware that had been shoved to the back of the fridge a month ago that she couldn’t bring herself to open.

She didn’t expect company. Hadn’t planned for it. If she had, she might’ve tidied up a bit more.

Not that the house was a mess, exactly. Just lived-in in the way things got when one came home late and left early and dropped their jacket over the banister instead of hanging it up because it wasn’t worth the energy. A few stacks of paperwork slouched against each other on the dining table. A takeout bag folded but not thrown out. A black bra, lacy and damning, was still draped over the back of the couch from her hasty change this morning.

Rio had whistled low as she stepped inside. “Cute place.”

Agatha had bristled. Not at the tone, neutral, maybe even sincere, but at the word itself. Cute. Her house was not cute. Her house was a holding space. A closet you could walk around in. A barely-passable adult life stuffed into 1,200 square feet of linoleum tile and cheap overhead lighting.

All she could see were the flaws.

She used to hate having friends over as a kid. Every visit was a performance: her mother barking orders for hours beforehand like a drill sergeant. As if twelve-year-olds gave a damn whether the curtains were ironed. As if having guests over meant every surface had to gleam like the cover of a home magazine or else she’d be deemed defective by association.

She remembered rushing to hide her notebooks in the closet, to vacuum lines into the carpet, to smile and pretend the house didn’t smell faintly like mildew and lemon Pledge. She remembered how her mother would clutch a guest’s elbow with faux grace and mutter something like, “Excuse the mess,” while her eyes dared them to find any.

Standing here in her own space, her own mess, Agatha had felt that same knot tighten in her chest. She wished she’d had time to light a candle. Hide the laundry. Be someone cleaner. Neater. Someone who folded and put away their laundry instead of picking clothes out of the pile on the overstuffed armchair.

But Rio hadn’t looked scandalized.

She’d just wandered in with the casual confidence of someone who’d never been made to feel ashamed of their space and graciously allowed Agatha to steer them back outside through the sliding glass patio doors.

Now here they were.

Rio leaned back a little farther, balancing the chair on two legs like she didn’t know the laws of physics applied to her.

“You mind if I ask you something?”

Agatha grunted, noncommittal.

“Why’d you join the Navy if you hate water?”

She could have snapped something clean and deflective; some polished lie she’d used a dozen times at bars. But Rio didn’t ask like other people did. She didn’t sound curious so much as careful. And Agatha hated that. Hated how gentle she could be.

So she defaulted to sarcasm, sharp and dry. “If you find yourself submerged in water in the Navy,” she said, “you’ve already got bigger problems.”

Rio gave a quiet laugh and sipped her beer.

“That’s the easy answer,” Agatha added, not quite sure why she said it aloud. “The real one’s less poetic.”

Rio glanced at her but didn’t interrupt.

Agatha peeled a little more of the label. “Spite,” she muttered. “Everyone said I wouldn’t make it. Said I’d quit. So I didn’t.”

She paused, then added flatly, “Turns out spite is a pretty good motivator. Go figure.”

Rio hummed low in her throat, like she was weighing that statement.

“You gonna analyze that too?” Agatha barked.

Rio just shook her head and tipped her bottle toward Agatha in an offhand toast. “Nah. Sounds like a solid reason to me.”

Agatha stared at her for a beat, suspicious. “You always this agreeable?”

“Only when it pisses people off,” Rio said with a smirk.

Agatha snorted, surprised by the warmth in it. She took another sip and leaned back into her chair, letting it creak.

The beer was lukewarm now.

Somewhere above them, the sky had turned dark without them noticing. No stars. Just a pale yellow halo where the streetlamp caught the edges of the clouds. Not a good kind of dark, Agatha thought. But not the worst either. Agatha leaned back a little farther in her chair, feeling the worn strap groan beneath her spine. Across from her, Rio’s chair creaked in response as she shifted, letting it fall back onto all four legs. Her bottle dangled between two fingers, beads of condensation rolling down the glass and catching on the scuff of her jeans.

She didn’t speak. Just rocked forward and propped her elbows on her knees, head tilted like she was still watching the sky, though Agatha wasn’t sure there was anything left to see.

“You ever feel like everything you worked for might not be enough?” Rio asked, voice quieter than usual.

Agatha blinked, surprised by the question, and more so by the tone. She’d expected another quip. Something easy to swat away.

“I stalled a dive sim once. First one we did. Lost orientation and puked in the suit.”

Agatha turned to look at her. “Seriously?”

Rio nodded. “Awful experience. I felt like I couldn’t get my hair clean for weeks.”

A breath escaped her. Half laugh, half disbelief. “And they let you fly after that?”

“They didn’t want to.” Rio smiled crookedly. “Eventually they figured it was easier to train me than to get rid of me.”

Agatha studied her, unsure what to do with the unexpected honesty. She tore the label off the bottle and crumpled it in her hand, suddenly aware of how close they were without touching. The air between them had shifted, gone quiet at the edges.

A mosquito buzzed close. Then closer still. Agatha swatted once but missed.

Before she could try again, Rio leaned forward and brushed it off her shoulder.

Just her fingers. A single smooth motion. But it landed like a chord strummed low in Agatha’s chest.

She didn’t move. Didn’t breathe. Just sat there, hyper-aware of the exact weight of contact, of the moment Rio’s touch lingered half a second too long before retreating.

“There,” Rio murmured. “Got ‘em.”

Agatha looked away, back at the dark sky.

She hated how warm her skin felt beneath the spot where Rio had touched her. Hated how a single soft word could stick harder than any argument.

Neither moved.

Not even when the silence came back again, thick as the humid Florida night, and wrapped around them both.

Agatha cleared her throat.

“I’ve got another beer in the fridge,” she said, abrupt and a little too loud, already rising before the words were finished. “If you want it.”

But Rio didn’t move. She just watched her with that maddening, unreadable expression… like she was two steps ahead and choosing not to say so.

Agatha took one step toward the door.

“You know,” Rio said lazily, “I almost feel bad. Interrupting your evening.”

Agatha paused. “What?”

Rio tipped her head toward the house. “Well. I just assumed the lace on the couch wasn’t for me.”

Agatha stopped walking. Her spine went stiff. “It’s none of your business.” A smile curled at the edge of Rio’s mouth. Not cruel. Not smug. Just amused, perhaps with a hint of something else.

“What if I want it to be?”

Agatha turned halfway toward her, caught off-guard by the softness in her voice. Not a dare this time. Just a question. One Agatha wasn’t sure how to answer.

The cicadas were louder now, thrumming against the edges of the quiet. Her hand flexed at her side, itching to move, to do something , but she didn’t.

Rio let the silence linger.

Then, with a small exhale, she leaned back again and propped her bottle on the arm of the chair like it wasn’t shaking up the entire axis of Agatha’s night.

“No pressure,” she added lightly with a shrug. “Just… making conversation.”

Agatha made it two more steps toward the sliding door before she realized she hadn’t answered.

She lingered in the doorway, fingers curled lightly around the handle, her weight shifting like it was caught between instinct and indecision.

“I think you should—” she started, voice low.

Rio was already rising, brushing her palms on her thighs. “Got it,” she said easily. “I’ll go.”

Agatha blinked. “—get another couple beers.”

Rio’s brow lifted and she stilled in place, rocking back onto her heels.

“And then meet me down the hall,” Agatha finished, barely louder than the hush of the cicadas beyond the fence. “Second door on the left.”

“Copy that, Captain,” Rio said, smiling like she’d won something.

Agatha rolled her eyes, but it didn’t have any heat behind it. She turned before Rio could say anything else, before she could say anything she wasn’t ready to hear, and disappeared inside.

The hallway felt longer than it ever had before.

Her room was chaos. Not dirty, just unmade in a lived-in way. Which was worse. Her jacket flung over the chair. Half a basket of unfolded laundry on the floor. Her nightstand cluttered with a mix of earplugs, receipts, and a nearly empty bottle of melatonin.

“Shit.”

She scrambled.

The laundry got shoved into the closet with one arm, the door yanked closed with the other. The bed got half-covered by dragging the comforter across it in one hasty motion. The underwear she’d changed out of this morning was still on the lampshade. She snatched it, balled it up, and launched it toward the closet too.

No time to light a candle. She grabbed her phone and dimmed the overhead to something marginally softer.

Then she sat on the edge of the bed, trying not to look like she’d sprinted a 100-meter dash and praying Rio wouldn’t notice her pulse was a drumline in her throat.

She didn’t have to wait long.

The door creaked open and Rio stepped inside like she’d always belonged there. Two beers in one hand. A sleeve of saltines tucked under her arm. And, most damning, a bright orange can of spray cheese clamped in her elbow.

“I can explain…”

Rio grinned, crossing to her. “If you don’t want any, I’ll eat the whole thing myself. I haven’t had this in forever.” She popped the top with a satisfying hiss and let the cap roll somewhere under the dresser.

Then she tilted her head back and sprayed a ribbon of neon cheese straight into her mouth.

Agatha stared, half horrified. “You’re disgusting,” she said faintly.

Rio wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and held the can out like an offering. “It was in your pantry.”

She flopped onto the bed beside her with a sigh of satisfaction, one leg curled beneath the other. The mattress dipped under the shift of her weight, and her thigh brushed Agatha’s.

Then the TV blinked to life.

A loud orchestral sting filled the room, followed by a crawl of yellow text on a starfield.

“Oh hell yes. I love this one.”

Agatha groaned and buried her face in her hands. “Of course you do.”

Rio reached for a cracker, using the other hand to pull out the remote from where she’d sat on it. “Let me guess. Too sentimental? Too black and white for your morally gray sensibilities?”

Agatha peeked through her fingers. “Too long. Too loud. Too many men who think they’re clever. And Yoda is a fucking freak.”

“You wound me.” Rio loaded the cracker with a shameful swirl.

“And yet you’re still here.”

Rio chewed thoughtfully, then shrugged. “You got beer, a good bed, and Princess Leia with a blaster. Seems like a good time.”

Agatha wasn’t sure if she was supposed to laugh, but she did. Quietly. Pressed behind the rim of her beer bottle as she took another sip.

It tasted better this time.

Less bitter. Or easier to swallow with someone sitting close enough to bump shoulders with.

Rio leaned back on one elbow and nodded toward the screen. “You want me to turn it off?”

Agatha hesitated. Then shook her head. “Leave it on.”

They let the music fill the silence between them.

Agatha’s eyes stayed on the screen, but her attention was elsewhere. The soft clink of Rio’s bottle against the nightstand. The rustle of the comforter shifting under weight not her own. The faint scent of whatever citrus thing Rio used in her hair.

“You always like this?” Agatha asked after a while.

“What, charming?”

“No. Comfortable.”

Rio tilted her head like she was considering it. “No one’s ever called me that before.”

“Hmm,” Agatha said. “Maybe it’s the chemicals in the cheese getting to my brain.”

Rio huffed a laugh. “Or… maybe it’s the company.”

She said it too easily. Like it wasn’t dangerous. Like it didn’t send a flare of heat straight down Agatha’s spine.

Agatha reached for the remote, but her hand brushed Rio’s instead. They both stilled. The contact was brief, Agatha felt stupid and small for even noticing it, but it hung in the air between them, heavy with everything they weren’t quite saying.

Rio didn’t move.

Neither did Agatha.

The title crawl ended and a planet came into view, but she couldn’t have named which one. She could barely remember how to breathe.

“I’m not good at this,” she said quietly.

“Good at what?”

“Company.”

Rio’s voice dropped. “You’re doing fine.”

Agatha’s fingers twitched against the worn comforter. “You’re not going to make a move, are you?”

“Nope.”

“Why not?”

“Because I want you to.”

Agatha turned toward her slowly, like she wasn’t entirely sure she was still in her own body. Rio met her gaze without flinching, eyes dark and steady and soft in a way that made her want to scream.

It wasn’t a grand gesture. There was no dramatic music swell. Just a simple lean forward and the quiet, deliberate press of their mouths meeting.

The kiss was slow, surprisingly so. Like Rio was waiting for her to bolt. Like she knew how many doors Agatha had slammed in people’s faces for trying less.

But Agatha didn’t pull away.

She leaned in closer, tracing her tongue over Rio’s bottom lip.

Her beer bottle clinked gently as it hit the floor. Rio’s hand skimmed her waist, warm through the fabric of her shirt. When Agatha tilted her head, she felt the scrape of Rio’s teeth and let out the smallest sound, half-choked, half-daring.

The movie kept playing. Forgotten like the crackers and cheese.

Later, Agatha would think of the way Rio’s laugh had felt pressed against her skin. The low rasp of it in her ear when she pulled her closer by the hem of her shirt. The way neither of them said anything for a long time after except each other’s names, whispered like they might disappear.

But for now they moved in sync like two bodies in orbit, gravity doing the rest.

And when the lights from the TV flickered across her ceiling in blue and gold, Agatha closed her eyes and let herself be seen.

Just this once.

Just enough.

Agatha woke to stillness.

The sheets were tangled on her side, but the other half of the bed was already made. Not perfectly, but enough to suggest someone had smoothed it down with a flat palm before slipping out.

The beers were gone.

So were the crackers. The cheese can. Even the remote had been returned to the nightstand like it had never been wedged under Rio’s thigh in the first place.

Agatha lay back against the pillow and let out a quiet sigh.

Of course she left.

Not that she’d expected anything else. That wasn’t the deal, was it? The deal was beer and sarcasm and one night of losing herself in something easier than pretending she didn’t want to be known.

Still, it stung more than it should have.

She rubbed her eyes and sat up, blanket falling around her hips. Her limbs felt too warm, too loose—like her body hadn’t caught up to the disappointment yet. She didn’t bother dressing. Just padded barefoot into the hallway prepared to grumble her way to the bathroom and start the day she was already regretting.

She stopped cold at the kitchen threshold.

Rio stood barefoot at the counter, still in last night’s jeans and a borrowed tee that hung loose around her shoulders. Her hair was pulled up into a messy twist, a few wispy strands free at the back of her neck doing nothing to conceal the hickey left there. She was frowning at Agatha’s coffee machine like it had offended her deeply.

“Jesus,” Rio muttered. “This thing older than both of us?”

Agatha coughed to draw her attention.

Rio glanced over her shoulder, caught her staring, and grinned. “Morning, sunshine.”

Agatha crossed her arms, playing it cool even as her heart did something humiliating behind her ribs. “What are you doing?”

“Trying to convince your coffeemaker to brew but it seems like it’s waiting for a divine miracle.”

“You could’ve just left.”

“Could’ve,” Rio agreed, turning back to the machine. “But then who would’ve cleaned up the crumbs from your bed?”

Agatha rolled her eyes and retreated to the living room pile of semi-clean clothes. She yanked on a pair of shorts and bent to find a shirt when she heard Rio say:

“You should wear the black bra.”

Agatha straightened and shot her a glare over her shoulder. “In your dreams, Vidal.”

Rio shrugged, utterly unfazed. “That’s a yes, then.”

Agatha pulled the shirt on and stepped back into the kitchen, accepting the mug Rio held out to her. Their fingers brushed, brief and warm.

“You made this?” Agatha asked, sipping warily.

“I did my best with your cursed machinery.”

“It’s drinkable,” Agatha admitted. “Barely.”

“High praise,” Rio said.

Then, softly, like it wasn’t a big deal, she leaned in and pressed a kiss to Agatha’s cheek. Not teasing. Not smug.

Just… there, warm and real.

Agatha didn’t move for a long second. She stood with the mug pressed to her lips, her eyes fixed on the floor, heart suddenly louder than it had any right to be.

Rio stepped back and turned to open the fridge like nothing had happened.

But Agatha’s cheek still burned.

And her coffee didn’t taste bitter at all.

“Is there any coffee in this building that doesn’t taste like it’s been burning since the sixties?”

Agatha glared at the offending cup like it had personally betrayed her. Across the room, the intern nearly dropped the tablet he was holding.

“I—I’m so sorry,” he stammered. “I didn’t know your preferences yet. I’ll —I’ll run to Starbucks. Or Blue Bottle? Or I think there’s an indie place with cold brew—”

“I don’t care where it’s from. I just want something edible,” she snapped, pinching the bridge of her nose.

He blinked. “Oh! Of course. They said you might be intense but I didn’t think—”

Agatha raised an eyebrow.

Billy coughed. “Right. Got it. I’ll have it before the presser.”

She paused, mid-sip of swill. “What press conference?”

Billy’s face lit up. “Oh! I just figured you already knew. You and Commander Vidal were confirmed this morning as the first official participants of the Apogee program. There’s a live Q&A at 10. NASA’s socials are already calling you ‘The First Interstellar Couple.’ Isn’t that amazing?”

Agatha made a noise that sounded very little like amazement.

“Who agreed to that ?” she hissed, lowering the mug like a weapon.

Billy froze. “I—I assumed you signed off. It’s been everywhere since 6 a.m.”

She barely had time to respond before the knock came. Two short, sharp raps against the door.

“Is that what you’re wearing?”

Rio stood there in her flight suit, collar popped slightly from the way she always tugged it loose at the neck. Two steaming to-go cups in one hand. A cocky smile that could level cities.

Agatha groaned and snatched one of the cups out of her grip.

“Good morning to you too, sunshine.”

Billy made a faint squeaking noise and retreated without another word.

Agatha didn’t even bother to watch him go. Her eyes were still on Rio.

“You knew about the press thing?”

“I did,” Rio said.

“And you didn’t think to tell me?”

Rio took a long sip of her coffee. “I know you like to take your bad news with a shot of espresso.”

Agatha glared over the rim of her cup. “You’re enjoying this.”

“A little,” Rio said again, and had the gall to wink over the rim of her cup.

Agatha scowled and turned away before her expression cracked into something else. She didn’t trust her face around Rio, hadn’t in years.

She set the coffee down with a controlled thunk and moved to the side cabinet where she kept her emergency wardrobe. There was a crisp white button-up hanging behind the door, still in its dry cleaning sheath, pressed and untouched.

She unwrapped it quickly, peeled out of her sweater, and swapped it for the shirt with practiced motions. The cotton was stiff but familiar, the kind of thing she only wore when forced to present a façade of togetherness. Important meetings. Budget appeals. PR stunts dressed up like science.

Behind her, Rio stayed uncharacteristically quiet.

Agatha had almost forgotten she was still there.

“Huh,” Rio said, that familiar low drawl curling around the syllable. “No lace?”

Agatha’s hands froze on the second button.

“Don’t,” she said sharply, without turning around.

There was a pause.

Then the unmistakable creak of worn office tile as Rio stepped closer, then past her.

“After you, milady,” she said with a too-formal bow and a flourish as she opened the door, like a knight about to walk into battle alongside a queen who hated being looked at.

She'd watched Rio stop mid-sentence. Watched the cocky grin drop off her face, replaced by nothing. And then Rio had dropped folding toward the concrete.

On instinct, Agatha dove to position her own body between Rio's head and the concrete.

It was nothing like the movies, she'd had to fight every instinct that screamed hold her still and instead just make the world softer around her.

And then it stopped. And Rio lay there breathing, surfacing slow from somewhere very deep, her eyes unfocused, her mouth working around words that wouldn't come yet. And then a clear fear.

"Hey," Agatha had said in a voice too gentle to be recognizable as her own.

Rio's brow furrowed but she didn't respond.

"Hey. You're alright. You're on the floor of the centrifuge bay and you're alright and nobody's here but me."

She'd watched the understanding arrive in Rio's face slow and then all at once, watched the fear curdle into something far more vulnerable.

In real time, she watched as Rio realized that Agatha, her rival, her friend, her Agatha, now also bore another relationship to her.

Witness.

A career-ending, dream-ending witness.

"You can't," Rio had whispered. "Agatha. You can't tell them about this. They'll pull me. They'll ground me forever, you know they will."

"I know."

"It's controlled. I swear. I haven't had a breakthrough in... the lights, it was the lights, it's only ever the fucking lights."

"I know, Rio." She'd gotten an arm under her shoulders, eased her up against the base of the centrifuge. "Breathe. You don't have to convince me. I'm not going to tell them."

One line in one report and the medical team would do the rest. Observed tonic-clonic event, candidate Vidal.

She'd thought about it for exactly as long as it took to feel sick to her stomach.

Then she'd helped Rio to the locker room, and waited while she washed her face with shaking hands. She'd driven her home in a remarkable silence, and never said a word about it again.

Not to the surgeon. Not to the cohort. Not to anyone.

"Why," Rio had asked her, weeks later, in the dark of Agatha's bedroom with the cicadas going outside.

Agatha stared at the ceiling. She could feel Rio watching her in the dark, that particular patient stillness that meant she wasn't going to let this go.

"Why what?"

"Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything. I'm lying in my own bed. You're the one conducting an interrogation at midnight."

It was quiet for a long time. Then Rio's hand had found hers in the dark, fingers threading through and squeezing once.

"That might be the most romantic thing anyone's ever done for me," Rio teased, "I've never had someone risk federal perjury before."

Agatha had laughed until her ribs hurt. She'd thought then, with a clarity that scared her more than the episode had, I need to be careful.

Or this is going to be the one I ruin.

She'd been right about that too.

In the shaft, twelve days and nearly a decade later, Rio's voice pulled her back.

"You went somewhere," Rio said. The work light caught the edge of her face. "You know I can see you right? That I watch you as closely as you watch everyone else?"

"I'm easy to look at," Agatha said, before she could stop herself from deflecting.

Rio's breath caught, but not in that chuckle Agatha loved to draw from her. A sharp, wrong inhalation. Agatha's head snapped up.

"Rio?"

"I'm okay," Rio said too quickly. "I'm okay, it's just... the air's getting thin in here, the scrubber, I think I'm-

She pressed the heel of her hand to her temple, and Agatha saw it then, the thing she'd been trained by love and terror to watch for: the small fixed flutter, the cocky face going smooth and absent, Rio reaching for words that had stopped arriving.

"Rio. Rio. Look at me!"

But she was already going.

Forty-two centimeters.

That was all the space she had to work with. Less, with Rio's body between her. Not enough room to turn around. Not enough room to think.

Agatha wrenched one arm under Rio's to pull her, the other reaching towards her comms unit.

"I need both of you at maintenance three now."

Alice's voice came back first, "Commander? What happened?"

"Vidal's down. I need hands to help me get her out."

"Copy. On our way."

Getting Rio through the hatch was the hardest thing Agatha had ever done on mission.

Rio was dead weight, and the shaft was built for maintenance, not mobility.

Alice braced at the hatch opening, arms locked, while Agatha maneuvered Rio's upper body forward inch by inch.

It took all three of them. It took too long.

By the time they had Rio strapped loose on the cot in the med bay, Agatha's arms were shaking and her jumpsuit was soaked through with sweat.

Agatha stepped back and let Jen work. Looking for a pupil response. Checking her pulse. The small penlight moved left to right while Rio flinched away from it.

Jen finished her checks and looked at Agatha.

"Commander, how long has this been going on?"

"That's not relevant to the current-"

"How long, Agatha?"

Agatha's mouth pressed into a thin tight line. "Years. It's photosensitive. The strobe in the corridor triggered it."

"And you knew."

"Yes."

Jen exhaled through her nose. She looked at Rio, then to Alice who raised her eyebrows, then finally back at Agatha.

"I'm the medical officer on this mission. I have a duty to-"

"You have a duty to this crew," Agatha interrupted. "All of us. If you report this, they pull her. Not just from the mission, Jen. From everything. Permanently."

"I'm aware of what happens."

"Then you're aware that she completed the repair. Under duress but she did it. The mission is intact. She did her job."

"That's not the point."

"That's exactly the point. The fault was environmental. The crew resolved it. That's the truth, and it's the only truth that needs to be on the record."

Alice uncrossed her arms. "She's right, Jen."

Jen shot her a look. "You don't get to-"

"I get to have an opinion about whether we end someone's career over something that didn't compromise the mission." Alice shrugged. "The scrubber's fixed. Rio's alive. If it had gone differently, we'd be having a different conversation. But it didn't."

"Environmental fault," Jen repeated. "Resolved by the crew. Two-person repair. Nominal recovery."

"That's all," Agatha said. "And I won't ask for anything from you again."

"That's all that goes in the log." Jen picked up her tablet. "But if it happens again, Agatha, we're having a very different conversation. And you won't like it."

"Noted."

Jen left. Alice lingered a moment longer, eyes on Agatha with something that might have been respect or might have been pity. With Alice it was always hard to tell.

"You good, Commander?" Alice asked.

"Fine."

"Liar." But she said it gently, and then was gone too.

Agatha finally exhaled and slid down against the wall until she was sitting next to Rio's cot. She fished the lighter from her pocket and placed her thumb to the wheel.

It wouldn't spark. It was never going to spark up here.

She kept turning it anyway.

Coming back felt like coming to after a crash landing. Rio's body felt fatigued as if she'd been flung around in the grips of a parachute. Her mind was cloudy and heavy enough to make lifting her head difficult.

That was the only way Rio had ever been able to describe it, the ground coming up faster than you'd planned for. She knew up from down, but it was like her body hadn't yet received the memo of her orientation.

The ceiling was the first thing to come into focus. Not the metal of the shaft but the padded white of the med bay.

Her tongue felt thick. Her shoulders ached like she'd been thrown, and she shifted uncomfortably on the cot.

The final thing to come into focus was Agatha.

Agatha was there.

Rio knew that before she could fully focus on her. She'd have known her presence in a blackout. There was no room that Agatha could occupy that Rio wouldn't feel her presence, still and steady, pulling all the oxygen into her orbit.

She was sitting beside the cot, knees drawn up, one hand resting on the edge of the mattress an inch from Rio's arm but purposely not touching it.

Her face was working very hard to betray nothing. Just the composed and contemplative Commander's face.

But Rio watched her closer than Agatha had ever allowed anyone else to, and she could read what was underneath.

There was a vein near her neck that drew tight when she was particularly stressed. The hand that wasn't on the mattress curled around something in her lap. The lighter, Rio realized. Agatha couldn't use it up here with the sparking mechanism removed (it was the only way NASA would allow it as enclosed pressurized spaces and flame were a famously bad combination), but her thumb worked the wheel of it anyway.

Rio took a deep breath before meeting the eyes that gave her away every time. They were fixed on Rio's face with a focus that revealed how tenuous her grip on composure was.

"There she is," Agatha said dryly. "Welcome back, Vidal. You missed a thrilling forty minutes of me doing your paperwork for you."

"Did I get it?"

Agatha blinked, clearly having expected different first words. "What?"

"The housing. The seal. Did I?" The words came slowly. She remembered the crack of it giving. And then the corridor lights, Agatha's hands. After that the white nothing she could never account for. "Did I finish it before?"

"You did."

Something flashed across Agatha's face, quick and unguarded before she shook her head to brush it off.

"You got it done, Rio. You always do. I don't think you need me to tell you that."

Relief hit her only for a second before it ricocheted back into dread.

"Agatha. The log. Does anyone, does command know..."

"No." Agatha replied immediately. " We agreed it was an environmental fault, resolved by the crew, two-person repair, nominal recovery. That's the record. That's all that's on the record."

A decade later and hundreds of thousands of miles traveled since, Agatha had once more stepped between Rio and the thing that could take the sky away from her.

"You're still doing it then?"

"Doing what?"

"Covering for me?"

Agatha looked away to the left, Rio noted, the way she always did right before she said something that wasn't entirely true.

"I'm filing an accurate report. The fault was environmental. You're not obligated to incinerate your own career to satisfy somebody's idea of full disclosure. Can you imagine? Feeling obligated to fully disclose anything to the US government? Please, don't make me laugh."

"That's not what I mean, and you know it."

"I don't, actually."

"You were scared," Rio said it plainly, watching her. "Down there. When the light changed. I've never heard your voice do that before. Not in the tank, not in the sims, not once in how many years."

Agatha's thumb stilled on the lighter wheel.

"I'm your commander," she said. "Of course I was concerned when a crew member-"

"Don't."

She was too tired to do the dance. Almost a decade of too tired. The fog in her head had burned off enough to leave her raw, and honest, and she was tired of how careful they'd both become.

"Don't 'crew member' me. Don't tell me it was concern. Tell me the real thing."

"Rio."

"The real thing, Agatha."

For a long moment the only sound was the air handler ticking and the dead wheel turning under Agatha's thumb. Rio waited. She had gotten good at waiting for Agatha.

Patience, she'd called it once, on a stoop in October. Faith.

Agatha had called it greed and cowardice.

As usual, they'd both been a little right.

"You stopped breathing," Agatha said finally. "When you went down. For a short stretch. And we're in a tin can past the moon and if it had gone wrong there's no hospital. There's no surgeon. There's Jen and a wall defibrillator kit and me."

Rio's throat went tight.

"That's why you've kept your distance? This whole mission. The polite- aloof-commander thing. You weren't punishing me."

Agatha was silent for a long while before finally sighing. "No."

"You were managing the risk of me." Rio almost laughed, except it would have hurt too much.

"You decided that if you let yourself have us again you'd be too rattled to be any good if something happened to me up here. So you took it off the table?"

"You needed focus. To do what you needed. You didn't need any stressors. No unnecessary risks."

"I never asked you to do that," Rio said, her voice rising. "Not once. Not in years. But you keep deciding what the flying is worth to me and then tying yourself in knots to pay for it, and you never once asked if I'd rather have you."

"You don't get to want both Rio!

Rio flinched as Agatha's voice rose with desperation.

"Nobody gets to want both, Rio. You want the sky, you have to be the kind of person who can lose people in service of it. I am volatile. I detonate. I have ruined every soft thing that's ever made the mistake of getting near me, and you are the softest, and the dream is the one thing I've never been able to wreck for you, so forgive me for not wanting to find out if I could."

Rio reached out to her slowly, her arm trembling with the effort. She found Agatha's hand and folded her fingers over it.

"Agatha," she said. "I have loved you in some kind of way for seven years. You know that right?"

Agatha went very still.

"Loved," she said, guard creeping back up her face in a near sneer. "As in the past tense?"

"Let me finish," Rio said, tightening her grip. "I have loved you for seven years. And because you're you and I'm me, I expect I'll keep loving you for seven more and seven more after that. I just wish you'd make it a little easier on me is all."

"So I'm not easy to love?"

"I'm not saying-"

"Actually, don't answer that." Agatha snapped, reaching for an argument because an argument she knew she could survive. "Seven years you've supposedly loved me, but I've known you for nine."

Rio felt the corner of her mouth lift at Agatha's haughtiness. Despite the headache and the soreness and the white nothing still ringing at the edges of her.

Of course Agatha would rather bicker about the semantics of a couple years than allow herself to sit with the word loved.

"Well," Rio said. "It wasn't exactly star-crossed love at first sight, now was it?"

Agatha glanced at Rio with a small and true smile.

"Wasn't it?"
