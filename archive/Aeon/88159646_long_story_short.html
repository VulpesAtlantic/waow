Chapter 1: When sun meets moon

The mission ended faster than expected. The back alley still held a faint trace of gunpowder, mingling with the smell of damp garbage and the metallic tang of evaporated rain.

Leon stood at the mouth of the alley. The streetlight stretched his shadow long across the wall opposite. He watched her stow the last piece of gear, then turn to face him.

Their eyes met briefly, the way they always did at the end of a mission. A quick check, a mutual confirmation that the other was still able to walk. And then it was time to part.

"I'm heading out," she said.

"Okay."

He stayed put. She paused a beat too. Whether she was giving him room to speak, or hesitating herself, Leon couldn't tell. But he knew that if he didn't say it now, the next chance might be weeks away, buried under encrypted channels, mission briefs, and their separate identities.

"Next time," he said. His voice came out heavier than he'd intended. He cleared his throat. "Next time, could it be… not a mission?"

He said it fast, casually. But his right hand was shoved in his pocket, fingers curling beneath the fabric, nails pressing hard enough to leave marks in his palm.

Ada tilted her head. The streetlamp caught her face from the side—her expression didn't shift, but her eyes lifted to his.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean—" He looked at her and felt like a high school kid asking someone to prom, ridiculous enough to make him want to laugh. "Dinner. You and me. Not the kind we grab in between objectives. The kind that's just for eating."

He regretted it the moment he said it. Not the asking, but the word dinner. It felt too ordinary. Too small for everything that had passed between them. But then again, maybe everything that had passed between them was exactly why an ordinary dinner felt so out of reach.

Ada was quiet for a moment.

The silence stretched, long enough that he almost said forget it, his mouth opening just a fraction, but before he could make a sound, she spoke:

"When?"

His heart stuttered. He forced his voice steady. "Two nights from now. Seven. I know a place…" He gave her an address—somewhere nicer, quiet, dimly lit.

"Okay."

Just like that, she turned and walked toward the other end of the alley. Her heels clicked on the wet pavement, the sound fading with each step.

Leon stood there, watching until she disappeared around the corner, her silhouette swallowed by the night. Only then did he pull his hand from his pocket, flexing his fingers. The tips were pale, a shallow nail mark traced his palm.

He turned and headed for the hotel. The night was deep, the streets empty. He walked alone beneath the streetlamps for a long while, and her word kept looping in his head. She said okay. She said okay.

Then he thought: what does okay mean? Did she mean it? Or was she just being polite? Did she say that to everyone—just that one word?

Back in his room, he shrugged off his jacket, draped it over the chair, and lay down on the bed, staring at the ceiling. A water stain bloomed in one corner. He stared at it for a long time.

He unlocked his phone. Scrolled to his call log. No new messages. Locked it. Unlocked it again. Opened the thread with the number that had no name attached—empty, the last exchange from three days ago: Safe from him, Good from her.

She hadn't texted to confirm the time.

But she hadn't said some other time, either.

Okay meant okay. Right?

He set the phone on his chest, turned onto his side, and closed his eyes.

Leon arrived twenty minutes early.

He checked his phone outside the restaurant—6:40. He should've circled the block one more time, but he'd already been walking for half an hour, and any longer and he'd look like a suspicious loiterer. He pushed through the door, gave the host his reservation name, and was led to a table by the window.

As he sat, he tugged at his collar. A dark shirt, one he'd grabbed on a whim at the mall, hanging in his closet for the better part of a year without being worn. He'd checked himself in the mirror twice before leaving. Decided it looked fine. Or maybe it looked too deliberate. A glass of water sat on the table. He picked it up and took a sip. Then pulled out his phone— still no messages.

7:05. 7:10. 7:15.

He picked up the phone, unlocked it, opened the thread, typed three words: I'm here. Sent. The screen lit up and dimmed.

7:30. A second message: Are you caught up in something? No reply.

7:40. He stared at the water glass, condensation beading down the side, leaving a wet ring on the table. A third message: It's fine. Another time works.

He called the waiter.

"Can I see the menu?"

He flipped through a few pages and ordered for two. He ended up finishing every dish. The food was good, though he didn't taste much of it. When the dessert arrived, the seat across from him was still empty. He ate that too.

On his way out, he left a bill on the table, and a generous tip he didn't bother counting. He draped his jacket over his arm and pushed through the door.

The night air hit him. He stood outside the restaurant, and his phone buzzed in his pocket. He pulled it out, and it was her number.

Sorry. Something came up. Just got free. Are you still there?

He stood on the curb and read the message for a long time. The wind was cool; he hadn't put his jacket back on, and the thin fabric of his shirt clung to his skin. He raised the phone, and typed: Still here.

The cursor blinked on the screen.

He looked at those two words.

Deleted them.

Typed: It's okay. You're busy.

Deleted that too.

Finally, he sent: Next time then. Stay safe.

He slipped the phone back into his pocket and started walking toward the parking lot. After a dozen paces, he stopped, pulled it out again. The thread was quiet, just his own bubble, floating there alone.

The next afternoon, his phone rang. Leon was in his hotel room, folding the shirt from last night into his suitcase.

"Hello."

"It's me." Her voice came through the speaker, lower than usual.

"Yeah."

"Last night," she said. "The mission came in suddenly. I couldn't get out. No time to let you know."

"It's fine," he said. "I understand."

The line went quiet for two or three seconds. They were both just listening to each other's breathing. Leon noticed her breath was light and steady, but deeper than normal.

"Another time?" she asked.

Leon blinked. He'd expected this call to be an explanation, a closing of the door. He hadn't expected that question. His fingers tightened around the phone.

"You still have time?"

"You pick," she said. "Nothing too far."

His heartbeat stumbled. He steadied his voice, and gave her a time—the day after, afternoon, a small place that didn't take reservations, tucked on a corner.

"Okay," she said. Then another beat of silence. She seemed to be waiting for him to say something. But his mind was blank, he didn't know what to say. So she hung up.

Leon listened to the dial tone, lowering the phone from his ear. He sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, face buried in his hands, palms pressed to his forehead.

Across the city, a black sedan sat idling at the curb.

Ada was in the driver's seat, phone in hand, screen already dark. She looked at her own reflection in the black glass.

Then she tossed the phone onto the passenger seat and started the engine.

She almost never stood people up.

Last night was the first time. She didn't like that she'd made him wait forty minutes. She wasn't used to making people wait. And she wasn't used to not knowing how to say I'm sorry on the phone afterward.

She wouldn't say it anyway. She just folded that feeling away, tucked it into her pocket, and made the call to ask: Another time?

As she pulled out of the lot, the phone on the passenger seat lit up again. She glanced over— not Leon. She looked back at the road.

This time, Leon arrived ten minutes early.

He slowed as he approached the little restaurant. Because he saw her.

She was standing across the street, leaning against the railing, looking down at her phone, like a pedestrian waiting for the green light.

She wore a deep red dress, the hem falling just above her knees, a thin line of collarbone visible at the neckline. Her hair was down, falling over her shoulders, the ends stirring slightly in the night breeze.

He stood there and looked at her for about three seconds. Then she looked up. When she saw him, her phone screen went dark, she slipped it into her clutch, and crossed the street toward him.

She stopped in front of him.

"You were here—why didn't you go in?" he asked.

"Just got here," she said.

A strand of hair blew across her face. She lifted a hand and tucked it behind her ear, fingers threading through the strands. Leon's gaze followed the motion. Then he stepped aside and held the door open for her.

They ordered. The menu was simple—one main each. The waiter left, and the table held two glasses of water and a small dish of pickled radish on the house.

Leon raised his glass and drank. Ada did the same. Their movements were almost synchronized—lift, sip, set down—and then their eyes met, and they both looked away.

About thirty seconds of silence settled over them.

Around them, other tables hummed with conversation, chopsticks clicked against bowls, and the sizzle of the kitchen drifted out. But their corner felt sealed under glass.

Leon tried to think of something normal to say. He opened with: "That mission you had—"

Then he stopped. That's not something you talk about over dinner.

"—never mind," he said.

Ada glanced at him, then down at the table. "How have—"

She stopped that too. She'd meant to ask how have you been, but she didn't know when to use that line. They'd never needed small talk.

"…Nothing. " she said.

They both looked down and took a bite. Leon felt like a teenager on a first date, and his palms were sweating. He wiped them discreetly against his trousers under the table.

Then he glanced at the table, then out the window, and suddenly laughed.

Not loud, but his lips couldn't hold it back. A small breath of air escaped.

Ada looked up. "What?"

He said, "We're not very good at this, are we?"

She thought about it, a faint crease between her brows. "I don't think we are," she said.

He looked at her.

Then he stood up and extended his hand across the table.

"Then let's stop pretending," he said. "Let's walk."

He stood beside the table, hand out, the warm light behind him tracing a soft edge around his silhouette. She looked at him for two seconds, then placed her hand in his.

Her fingers were cooler than his, light in his palm like a thin leaf. He closed his grip around them.

They paid and stepped out, no car and no direction discussed. She slipped her hand from his, and his heart dipped. But she was just adjusting, and then she took it again.

She wasn't looking at him. Her eyes on the road ahead. She took a couple of steps, then said, "I got here twenty minutes early."

He slowed. "Oh."

"Stood across the street for twenty minutes before coming in."

He stopped. She stopped too, turning to face him.

"Why?" he asked.

"I don't know. Maybe I was afraid you wouldn't be there."

He looked at her. Under the streetlight, her pupils had lightened to the color of amber.

"I'm here," he said.

"Yeah. I know that now."

They kept walking. Their hands were still clasped, but she shifted her grip, from his hand holding hers to their fingers interlaced.

They came to a bridge.

It wasn't long—maybe two or three minutes to cross, spanning the river. Lights lined both sides, old-fashioned bulbs casting a warm yellow glow that flickered gently in the night air. Below, the sound of water ran low and steady.

They reached the middle and slowed in unison, then stopped. Side by side, they leaned against the railing and looked down. The river reflected the lights from both banks, broken by the breeze into shifting patches of gold.

The wind came off the water, cool and damp.

Goosebumps rose on Ada's bare arms. She drew her arms in but didn't let go of his hand.

"Cold?" he asked.

"A little."

Leon shrugged off his jacket and draped it over her shoulders. The fabric settled around her, still carrying his warmth, the sleeves hanging past her hands.

"Your jacket has a smell," she said.

"What smell?"

"Like you."

She turned her head to look at him. The river's light caught in her eyes, flickering, as if something burned softly behind her pupils. She wore his jacket now, shoulders smaller beneath it, one hand holding the collar closed, the other still laced with his.

He looked at her profile and felt something rise in his throat.

But all the I missed yous and I'm glad you're heres and I was actually really disappointed last night stuck somewhere between his chest and his mouth. They were too heavy. He was afraid they'd crush the night.

So instead, he leaned over and pressed a kiss to her temple.

His lips touched her skin just as the wind came again, lifting a strand of her hair to brush against his jaw.

She didn't pull away and didn't make a sound.

But she shifted closer, shoulder against his arm, her weight tilting, and she leaned in. They stood on the bridge quietly. The river kept fracturing the light, the breeze kept rolling in.

After a long while, she said, "Next time, don't book such an expensive restaurant."

Leon blinked. "That one wasn't expensive."

"I meant the last one." She looked up at him. "Next time, don't. By the time the food gets to the table, it's cold and it's a waste."

He thought about saying you didn't even order anything, but instead, his mouth just curved up.

"Okay," he said. "Next time, I'll cook."

She gave him a are you serious look. "You can cook?"

"Not really," he admitted. "But I can learn."

"Fine," she said, a smile in her voice. "I'll watch. And I'll judge."

He looked at her again. There she was, wrapped in his jacket, smiling, standing on a bridge.

He wanted to bottle this moment. Tuck it into his eyes and carve it into his brain.

"Come on. Let's not catch a cold." he said.

So they kept walking. Past the bridge, the streetlights grew closer together, the crowds thickened. Her hand was still in his, his jacket still on her shoulders.

The night before, he'd prepped everything he could.

Vegetables chopped and covered in plastic wrap. Sauces lined up on the counter—soy sauce, dark soy, oyster sauce, sugar, salt. He'd even scrubbed the wok twice and cleared the counter of everything unnecessary.

Ada arrived carrying a bottle of wine.

She set it on the dining table and stopped at the kitchen doorway.

Leon wore an apron, dark blue with a single pocket at the front. He was bent over, studying the heat, when he heard her come in. He straightened and turned.

"You're here," he said.

"Yep."

She leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. No sign of offering to help.

He turned back to the stove. The flame licked the bottom of the wok; oil heated, and when he tipped the vegetables in, they hit with a loud hiss. His stirring was unpracticed, the spatula looked awkward in his hand, like a weapon that didn't quite fit. Two tosses and a drop of oil jumped out, landing on the back of his hand. He flinched and took a half-step back.

She still didn't move to help. Just watched, the corner of her mouth tilted up.

In the end, it was edible. At least. A plate of shredded pork with green peppers, a plate of stir- fried greens, two bowls of rice. They sat across from each other at the small table, the window dark now, the apartment lit in warm tones.

She glanced at him over her bowl; he was looking down at his rice, eating, but the tips of his ears were red.

Halfway through, Leon set down his spoon and stared at the leftover rice in his bowl. "You didn't run this time."

Ada's chopsticks paused mid-air. "What?"

"Last time you didn't come." His voice was quiet. "But then you did come. And now you're still sitting here."

She looked at him and set her chopsticks down.

"Leon."

"Yeah."

"I'm not going to not show up every time."

"I know," he said. He raised his eyes to meet hers. "But you haven't said—"

He trailed off. The look in his eyes finished the sentence: You haven't said what this is. What we are.

Ada held his gaze for a few seconds, her fingers tapping lightly on the table.

"I came twice," she said. "I wore a dress. I stood across the street for twenty minutes because I was afraid you wouldn't be there. I took your jacket home and didn't return it."

She paused. "Do you really need to hear those words?"

He held her eyes. "I don't need to. But I want to."

She sighed.

"Then I'll say it—"

She stood up, her chair scraping back, and walked around the table to him. He looked up from his seat as she bent down and kissed him.

Her lips were soft, cool, with a faint taste of wine.

When she straightened, her hand pressed against his shoulder, and felt a trace of warmth through the fabric of his shirt.

"There, I said it."

Leon sat there, looking up at her, a smile slowly spreading across his face.

"You didn't say anything."

"I basically did."

He stood. Now they were close—close enough to see their own reflections in each other's eyes. "Say it again."

She lifted a hand and traced a line along his jaw.

"Your cooking is passable," she said. "Keep going."

He laughed, head dropping.

"Okay."

They did the dishes together afterward.

Ada stood at the sink, rinsing plates under the faucet, water splashing in fine sprays. Leon stood beside her, drying each plate with a kitchen towel and stacking them to the side.

They talked—about nothing much. He asked where she'd bought the wine. She said the convenience store downstairs, and the cashier had looked at her like she was shoplifting. He laughed and said she did look like a thief. She elbowed him, and he raised the plate out of reach.

When they finished, she stood at the kitchen doorway, drying her hands and hanging the towel back on the rack. He stood behind her, looking at her profile.

"Tonight," he said.

"Hm?"

"Stay?"

She looked at him for a few seconds: "......Okay."

That night, she lay on the other side of his bed, breathing softly, her hair spread across the pillow like a patch of night against white cotton.

Leon lay beside her, awake. He was on his side, watching her silhouette in the dim light. Her back was to him, shoulders rising and falling with a steady rhythm, as if she'd really fallen asleep. But he noticed her hand was resting beside the pillow, fingers slightly curled, ready to grasp something at a moment's notice. A habit, he knew, that even sleep couldn't break.

He didn't touch her. Just stared at her back for a long, long time.

The next morning, he woke to light hitting his face.

The curtains hadn't been drawn all the way, so a stripe of sunlight had squeezed through and fallen right across his eyelids. He frowned and turned over, only to find the other side of the bed empty.

His eyes snapped open.

The sheets were rumpled. A single dark hair lay on the pillow. His heart picked up, then he heard sounds from the kitchen—the soft clatter of dishes, the faucet turning on and off.

He got up and walked over, barefoot on the floor.

In the kitchen, Ada stood at the stove. She was wearing his old white T-shirt, the hem hanging mid-thigh. She was transferring leftovers from a bowl onto a plate.

She heard his footsteps and turned.

"You're up."

"…Yeah." He stood in the doorway, watching her. Sunlight streamed through the window, landing on her shoulders and arms, the thin fabric of the shirt turning almost translucent.

"You—" His voice was still rough. "When did you wake up?"

"Half an hour ago." She slid the plate into the microwave, set the timer, and leaned back against the counter. "You slept hard."

"Rare."

"Mm."

Leon stepped closer. One step. Two. He stood before her, looking down. She tilted her face up, hair loose and falling across her brow. He reached up and tucked that strand behind her ear.

His fingers lingered at her temple for a moment, then traced down until his palm cupped her jaw, his thumb brushing lightly across her cheekbone.

She tilted her head just slightly, pressing her face into his hand.

"Leon," she said. "You kept turning last night."

"You were awake?"

"I was."

He smiled. "Then why didn't you say anything?"

"What was I supposed to say?"

He lowered his head until his forehead touched hers, their noses almost brushing, their breath mingling. Then the microwave beeped.

She glanced back, then at him. "You eating?"

"Yeah."

She took the plate out and set it on the table, then pulled two bowls from the cupboard.

He stood there watching.

She glanced at him. "Why are you just standing there?"

"Watching," he said.

"Watching what?"

"Watching you in my place."

Her hands stilled for a moment. She straightened, holding a bowl, and turned to face him. Her expression was neutral, but her eyes held something, like the shattered light on the river, flickering, there and gone.

Later, she left.

She changed back into her own clothes; the T-shirt went into his closet.

She stood at the door, put her shoes on, hand on the handle. He stood behind her.

She looked back over her shoulder.

"Next time," she said. "No more restaurants."

"You'll cook?" he asked.

"You'll cook," she said. "And I'll judge."

Then she pulled the door open and walked out, closing it behind her.

Leon listened to her footsteps fade down the hallway, the elevator ding, and then silence. He looked at the cup she'd used on the counter, the bowls she'd pulled out and put back, the dark strand of hair on the pillow. He walked to the window and looked down. Her figure emerged from the building, crossed through the sunlight, and climbed into the car parked at the curb. She got in, closed the door, and drove away.

He stood there, watching the car merge into traffic, growing smaller, until finally disappearing at an intersection. After a while, he turned back and walked to the kitchen, picked up her cup, rinsed it, and set it back on the rack.

He grabbed his phone and opened the unnamed thread. He thought for a moment, then typed a line.

You still have my jacket.

A moment later, her reply appeared.

Not giving it back.

He stared at those two words for a long time, and grinned. He looked toward the window again. The sky was a clear blue. People moved along the street below. The wind carried in the clean, cool air of morning.

It looked like nothing more than an ordinary day.

Chapter 2: When the snow falls

His refrigerator was basically empty. The last time he'd opened it was three days ago—that expired carton of milk needed to be thrown out, but he'd forgotten. By the trash can, a few takeout containers were piled up. He remembered eating them, but couldn't quite say when. A water glass sat on the coffee table, a ring of dried residue clinging to its rim. The curtains were half-drawn; the other half had gotten stuck. Sunlight pushed through the gap and fell across the floor, illuminating flecks of dust drifting in the air. Leon sat on the couch and watched them for a long time—floating slowly up and down in the light beam, like aimless little creatures.

His phone rang. He glanced at the screen. No name saved, but he'd long since memorized the number. He picked up, and before he could say anything, she said, "Open the door."

Leon blinked. He got up from the couch and walked to the door. Through the peephole, he saw a familiar hand. He opened it.

Ada stood at the doorstep in a thin black jacket, her hair tousled by the wind. She looked at him, her gaze traveling from his face down to his body and back up again. "You've lost weight," she said.

"Just passing through?" He stepped aside to let her in.

She didn't answer, just walked past him into the living room. She stood in the center, looking around. Three days' worth of takeout boxes on the coffee table. Dust on the floor. A smudge on the refrigerator door where his finger had dragged across it. She turned and walked to the kitchen. She pulled open the fridge and stood there for about five seconds, surveying its contents: the expired milk, two eggs, half a bottle of water, a pack of ham that had been opened God knows when, its edges curling up. She sighed, closed the door, and walked out.

Leon sat back down on the couch. He heard the front door shut. He didn't follow. He looked down at his hands resting on his knees, there was a faint scar across one knuckle. He didn't know where she'd gone. But he waited.

About half an hour later, the door opened again. She came back carrying two plastic bags stuffed with eggs, milk, tomatoes, a package of meat, a bundle of greens, and a bag of pasta. She set the bags on the kitchen counter and began unpacking, placing things into the fridge one by one.

Leon sat on the couch and watched her do all of this. She hadn't spoken since saying you've lost weight. Her back was to him as she bent over, rearranging the fridge. Her hair was longer than the last time he'd seen her, tied up carelessly, leaving a small patch of bare skin at the nape of her neck.

"Why did you come?" he asked. His voice came out rougher than he'd expected. He couldn't remember when he'd last eaten, and his throat felt dry.

She didn't turn around. She was running the hot water, the sound of it rushing into the sink. "Do you want me to leave?" she said without looking back.

"No."

After a moment, the sound softened as she turned the tap down, her voice dropping like she was talking to herself: "Then I won't."

She finished with the fridge, folded the plastic bags and tucked them into a gap in the cabinet. Then she walked to the sink, washed her hands, shook off the water, and dried them on her trousers. She slipped off her jacket, draped it over the back of a chair, and stepped up to the stove. She turned on the burner.

Leon rose from the couch and leaned against the kitchen doorway. She cracked an egg into a bowl, a piece of shell broke off and fell in. She fished it out with chopsticks, working clumsily, taking a while to get it. When she cut the tomatoes, her knife work had its own rhythm—uneven, unhurried, the pieces varying in size. Oil splattered when it hit the pan, and she flinched back.

He watched from the doorframe. "Can you actually cook?"

"No."

"Then why are you doing it?"

"I'm making something you can eat."

"Are you sure it's edible?"

She shot him a look over her shoulder.

And...it wasn't good. The eggs were overcooked, the edges burnt, and the tomatoes had barely released any juice. The rice was undercooked, she put too little water, the grains still hard in the center. She took a bite first, and frowned. Leon sat down across from her, picked up his bowl, and took a bite. The burnt edge of the egg mixed with the sourness of the tomato, the undercooked grains crunched between his teeth. He chewed for a long time. Then he swallowed.

"It's…not great," he said.

"I know."

He took a second bite. Then a third. He finished the whole bowl. When he set it down, she was sitting across from him, barely having touched her own. She looked at his empty bowl, then at his face. She lowered her head and ate some of her own undercooked rice too. They sat facing each other, eating bad food, but neither of them complained again.

He woke in the middle of the night. He lay still, hands folded across his stomach, staring at the ceiling. He could hear her breathing was steady, but just a fraction faster than someone fully asleep. She wasn't asleep either.

"How long have you been awake?" he asked.

"Not sure. Didn't check the time."

"Can you sleep now?"

She turned over, shifting to face him. In the dark, he couldn't make out her expression, but he could feel her gaze. She moved a little closer. "Can you?"

"Not sure."

"When you can't sleep, what do you think about?" she asked.

He thought for a moment. "The past."

"Which parts."

"All of it. Raccoon City. The missions. And that one time—" He stopped. That time he'd asked her to dinner and she'd been forty minutes late. He knew it wasn't her fault, but three years later, it still surfaced sometimes.

"Which time?"

"Nothing."

"I asked."

He turned his head to look at her. In the darkness, her eyes still seemed to hold a glint of light. "That time at the restaurant. You were forty minutes late. When you said 'are we still on,' I… I wondered if you actually wanted to come."

She was quiet for a moment. Her breath fell against the pillow, and he could feel the faintest stir of air. "I came because I wanted to. That time was an accident. The ones after weren't."

"Then will you keep coming?" he said.

"You said you don't want me to leave."

"I don't."

"Then there's your answer."

He reached out and lightly touched her fingers where they rested on the pillow. Then he took her hand and held it between them on the sheets, his palm over her knuckles. Her fingers slowly relaxed inside his grip, and he could feel the warmth of her skin seeping through. The night was still, the shadow of the curtains swayed gently across the wall.

At some point, he fell asleep. His fingers were still loosely holding hers. When he woke again, morning had come. He found himself facing her direction. And she was still there, lying on her side, her face turned toward him, lashes brushing against her cheekbones.

He tried to pull his hand back, but the moment he shifted, her eyes opened. She looked at him, taking two seconds to come fully awake, and then the corner of her mouth lifted. "You're up."

"Yeah."

"Your hand is sweaty."

"Mm." He didn't let go.

After a moment, she sat up and turned, hair slipping from her shoulders to her back. She sat on the edge of the bed, facing away from him, morning light cutting through the gap in the curtains and falling across her shoulder blades.

"How did you sleep?" she asked.

"Pretty well. You?"

"Decent."

She stood, her feet meeting the floor, and glanced back at him. "I'll make breakfast. You stay put." Then she walked out of the room, and he heard the fridge opening, water running, pots and pans meeting the stove. He looked down at his own hand, fingers still slightly curved, still holding the shape of hers.

It was early autumn of 2017.

After that, she'd come every few weeks. She'd just appear at his door, no reason given, no warning. She'd check his fridge, sigh, and head out to the store. He'd sit on the couch and watch her come back with plastic bags, placing eggs, milk, and vegetables into the fridge one by one. Chapter 5: It's spring again

When spring came, her things had already filled every corner of his apartment. At first it had been just one suitcase and a canvas bag. She'd said, "I'll leave these here for now." He'd said, "Leave them as long as you want." Then, little by little, her possessions began to migrate into every crevice of his space—a lipstick appearing on the left side of the bathroom sink, a half- read old book tucked beneath the coffee table, a pair of seldom-worn flats wedged on the third shelf of the shoe rack, mixed in with a row of sneakers. A thin sweater draped over the back of a living room chair. When he noticed, he folded it and set it on the couch. She came home, picked it up, and put it on.

He'd catch himself staring at these traces sometimes. The lipstick cap with a faint fingerprint pressed into it. The gold-embossed bookmark tucked between pages. She was on page one- twenty now, last time it was ninety-eight. She must have been reading a few pages before bed each night until she drifted off.

Mornings, she usually woke first. Sometimes she'd find his leg pinning hers, like a tree root wrapped around her. She couldn't move, but she didn't want to. Light slipped through the gap in the curtains and fell across his shoulder, catching a faint mark just above his collarbone. He slept deeply, his breath warm and even against her arm. Only when he turned in his sleep and pulled his leg away did she slowly extract herself and head to the kitchen.

Eventually, they started cooking together. When she stir-fried, he'd lean against the doorframe and watch, occasionally offering, "Lower the heat" or "You added the salt too early." She used to snap back with "You do it if you're so good." But she stopped, because what he said was usually right.

One afternoon, they made over forty dumplings together. A pinch here, a press there—the edges sealed neatly, pleats even and fine, and hardly any burst. He sat at the table with his bowl, took a bite, chewed, and swallowed. The filling was fresh, the wrapper springy, a dip of vinegar bringing it all together just right.

One night, he fell asleep before her. She lay on her side beside him, looking at his face.

She thought of the first time she'd seen him. So young, so green—someone who shouldn't have survived a place like that. And he was such an idiot. Had been for over twenty years. All that time, he'd never changed—always that stubborn, earnest, waiting for her to come back. No one had ever waited for her like that. She used to think she didn't need it. Later, she realized she'd just been afraid to admit she did. And he'd been standing there all along. She rested her hand lightly over his, then closed her eyes. Outside, the wind stirred the curtain, moonlight shifted an inch across their bodies.

After a while, she fell asleep too.

The next evening, he was washing dishes in the kitchen. She leaned against the doorframe and watched him. When he finished, he set the bowls in the drying rack, shook the water from his hands, and turned, to find her still there, arms crossed, her gaze resting on him but not quite on him. It had drifted somewhere further.

"What are you looking at?"

She blinked, refocusing. "Nothing."

"You're thinking about something."

"Yeah." She dropped her arms, walked past him,and picked up a bowl from the drying rack. She ran her finger along its rim, then placed it back in the cupboard. Her movements were natural, but she lingered an extra second with her fingers on the cabinet door, not closing it. He noticed the pause. She rarely did unnecessary things. A stopped hand meant something unspoken. So he waited.

"Leon," she said, back to him, fingers still on the cabinet. "Have you ever thought about the future?"

"Like what?"

"Like ten years from now." She closed the cabinet and turned to face him. "Where will you be?"

"Wherever you are."

"What if I'm somewhere not so great?"

"Then I'll come find you there."

"That's the same as saying nothing." She walked out of the kitchen, settled onto the living room couch, picked up the remote, and turned on the TV. He watched her back from the kitchen. She was channel-surfing, two seconds per station, images flickering across the screen in rapid flashes.

That night, she didn't bring up "the future" again. But later, lying in bed, he replayed the way she'd asked. It hadn't felt like a spontaneous question, more like something she'd held in her mouth for a while, finally testing the temperature at a moment that seemed neither too heavy nor too light. He didn't press her. But he knew she'd say more. She wasn't the type to ask and forget.

A few days later, they were curled up on the couch, watching a movie. She was wearing his old hoodie, feet tucked beneath her, her knee pressed against the outside of his thigh. Halfway through, she spoke.

"Leon, I have a question."

"Yeah?"

"Do you think we'll get married?"

He nearly choked. A half-empty beer sat on the coffee table; he'd been reaching for it. His hand froze mid-air. "—What?"

"I asked, do you think we'll get married?" Her eyes were still on the TV. "Think about it."

He pulled his hand back and looked at her profile. "…I think so."

"Why?"

"Because I can't imagine spending my life with anyone else." He looked down at his own fingers resting on his knee. "So if you're not planning on someone else either, then theoretically—"

"Theoretically," she said.

"Yeah."

"And practically?" She finally turned from the TV to look at him.

"…You never said you wanted to," he said.

"I never said I didn't."

"Then—" He held her gaze. "Do you?"

She turned back to the screen. After a moment, she said, "When I had a gun pointed at you in Raccoon City, I never imagined I'd be discussing this with you one day."

He watched her silhouette. She sat on the couch in his old hoodie, feet tucked on the cushion —a world away from the shadow he'd carried in his mind since she was twenty-four. That version of her had been a figure in the dark, dangerous and unreachable, like a photograph sealed in fog. Twenty-odd years later, the image had faded, but he could still trace its sharp, fine edges. And now this person sat beside him, knee pressed against his thigh, fingers tapping lightly on the couch, like an ordinary person trying to figure out what to say next.

"So?" he said.

She let out a deep breath, then swung her feet off the couch and turned to face him. Her cheeks were faintly flushed, her lips pressed together once. "Leon."

"Yeah."

"Will you marry me?"

His brain short-circuited. He could only stare at her, eyes wide with disbelief. She didn't look away, didn't brush it off with her usual lightness. She just sat across from him, hands on her knees, waiting. He felt frozen. He swallowed hard before finally managed, "…Are you proposing?"

"Yes."

He stared, his rigid face slowly breaking into a smile. "…You don't even have a ring."

She smiled and reached into the hoodie pocket and pulled out a velvet box, deep blue, the size of her palm. She opened it. Inside lay a plain silver band, unadorned, catching the light in a soft gleam. He picked it up and brought it closer. It was just a clean, simple circle.

His pupils lit up. "When did you buy this?"

She turned her gaze to the TV. Someone on-screen was delivering an inconsequential line, the sound drifting over from far away. "…Two years ago."

"You waited two years to say something?"

"I was waiting for you to say it first."

"You were so sure I would?"

She turned back to him. In the flickering light from the TV, her expression was still calm. "You would. You just needed time."

He held the ring, his fingers slowly closing around it. A plain silver band, no engraving. But their names weren't something you could fit into an inscription anyway—they'd long since carved each other into bone. A smooth silver circle was enough. A closed loop, no beginning and no end. As if someone had drawn the circle long ago, and they'd only been walking the path all along.

"So?" she asked. "What's your answer?"

"I do. Will you put it on me?"

She straightened on the couch, took the ring, and slid it onto his left ring finger. Her hand trembled slightly as her fingertip brushed over his knuckle. The ring passed the joint and settled at the base, snug but not tight, the silver cool against his skin.

He looked down. "Your hand is shaking."

"Shut up."

"I really never thought—"

"I know."

"You never said—"

"I know."

He looked up at her. Her gaze was fixed on the ring. "Ada."

"Yeah?"

"I love you."

She leaned in and kissed him. Her breath came a little uneven as their lips met, a warm, quick exhalation against his mouth. Her fingers found his cheek, palm warm against his cheekbone. They kissed for a long time. The sky was getting dark outside, the TV was still playing in the living room, and they had no idea how that movie ended.

When she pulled back, her forehead still pressed to his, their breaths mingling. "I love you too." she said.

A warmth flooded through him. He froze for a second, then dropped his head with a laugh, his shoulders shaking. When he looked up, his eyes were faintly red-rimmed. He gathered her carefully into his arms, chin resting in the hollow of her shoulder, and slowly tightening his arms.

"You finally said it. Why didn't you say it earlier?"

"Couldn't talk when you said it."

"Why?"

"My mouth was occupied."

He laughed against her temple, his nose brushing hers. "Say it again."

She pulled back half an inch and looked into his eyes. The movie had ended, the screen was frozen on the credits, light scrolling slowly up the wall. "I love you. I know I still owe you two. But we have time."

Outside, it had started raining without either of them noticing. Raindrops tapped against the window, soft and rustling. He glanced at the glass, the water streaking down in thin lines, the streetlights beyond blurring into smeared gold through the curtain of rain. He thought of that rainy day. He'd been a rookie cop on his first day, running through wet streets in soaked clothes, not yet knowing her.

"It's raining," he said.

She looked at the window too. Rivulets of rain crisscrossing the glass. She turned back to him. "Just like that year."

"Yeah. I was thinking the same thing."

"What were you thinking back then?"

He let out a slow breath, stirring old dust from his memory. He was quiet for a moment. "Honestly? I didn't have time to think. But it felt like that rain lasted a long long time."

She leaned against his shoulder, her voice soft: "I used to never remember rain."

"Why?"

"Because rain stops. Remember the rain, and you remember the place. Remember the place, and you remember the people there." She paused. "But I've always remembered the rain in 1998. Because you were in it."

He looked at the top of her head. His fingers tightened gently around hers. Outside, the rain fell steady and fine, like an old reel of film, every frame washed in rain, every sound playing back all at once: gunfire, helicopter rotors, quiet nights, and those mornings when he thought she wouldn't return. The rain rinsed through those images again and again, like negatives worn down by time, finally stored away in a dry, dark box. "So every time it rains after that —" she said, "I think of you."

The rain began to ease, the sound thinning from a steady hush to scattered drops, until only the drip from the eaves remained. The streetlights glowed, reflecting off the wet pavement in scattered gleams. The thick clouds above were slowly breaking apart at the edges.

Night air drifted in through the window, carrying the smell of wet earth and fresh leaves, like a sound deep within spring itself. She looked up at him, then reached out and touched the ring on his finger, her fingertip tracing slowly along the silver circle. "You should give me one too." she said.

He thought for a moment, then stood and walked to the bedside table in the bedroom. He crouched, opened the bottom drawer, pushed aside a few folded clothes, and pulled out a small black velvet box from the very back. It had been there for a long time, the velvet at the corners slightly worn. He carried it back to the living room, stopped in front of her, and lowered himself to one knee. He opened the box. Inside lay another plain silver band, no engraving, identical to hers in its simplicity, but slightly brighter. "My turn to ask," he said. "Ada Wong, will you marry me? Will you spend the rest of your life with me?" He lifted the ring from the box. "I know you'll still have missions to run, and you might be gone for a long time. But every time you come back—" He paused, lowered his head, and slid the ring gently onto her finger. "—come back to me, okay?"

The silver band settled at the base of her finger.

She crouched down, took his hand, and brought it to her lips. She pressed a soft kiss to the ring at his finger.

"I'll come back to you. Every time." She lifted her head. "I said yes."

She sat back on the couch and looked down at the ring.

"Do you like it?" he asked.

"Yeah."

Their hands were still interlaced, the two plain silver bands resting side by side.

"Get up," she said. "Stop kneeling."

He smiled, stood up and sat beside her. She held her left hand up to the light, turning it to catch the gleam—white light sliding across the metal in tiny flashes.

"Leon."

"Yeah?"

"Have you ever thought—"

"What?"

"That we wasted a lot of years."

"...No." He said, "Those years weren't wasted. Without them, I wouldn't have known you. I wouldn't have known how much you matter." He turned his head and pressed his lips to the crown of her hair. "So—it was all meant to be."

She didn't answer, but she leaned in closer. She placed her left hand in his palm, the ring pressed against his skin, the silver band warming slowly in the heat of his hand, carrying the temperature of her fingertips.

"I'm sleepy." she said.

"Then let's go to bed."

"Carry me."

Her tone wasn't demanding, but he laughed anyway.

He stood, bent, slipped one arm under her knees and the other around her back. He set her down on the bed, the mattress dipped as she settled into the pillows, her hair fanning across the dark fabric, a few strands falling beside her collarbone. Her breath brushed against his lips, carrying the faint warmth of the cool night air.

He lowered his head and pressed his lips to the side of her neck. Her fingers slid into his hair, palm pressing against the back of his skull, and the other hand gripped the hem of his shirt.

"…The ring," she murmured.

"Yeah?"

"Take it off."

He paused, then whispered against her ear. "You take it off."

"Then leave it on."

He let out a soft laugh and lowered his lips again, from her neck to her collarbone. Her fingers traced down his spine, her knees bent slightly.

The night stretched on. Wind passed by the window, moonlight inched slowly across their intertwined shadows.

The sound of traffic faded into the distance. The city grew still. In the rain-washed sky, a patch of deep blue emerged, with two or three faint stars glimmering silently.

Tomorrow would probably be a clear day.
