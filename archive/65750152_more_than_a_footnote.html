More Than A Footnote

by visadero

Summary

Agatha Harkness is a tenured academic with a sharp tongue, a grueling syllabus, and a reputation to maintain.

Her ex returns to campus as a visiting scholar. With her crooked smile, an infuriatingly charming teaching style, and lectures that summon applause.

Their offices share a wall. Their past shares something messier.

And if their students are right, they’re definitely not over each other.

As Rio walked through the door, a wall of sound surrounded her.

“Ding-dong, the witch is dead! Which old witch? The wicked witch!”

It was an odd song choice for her girlfriend, but grading-induced hysteria wasn’t unheard of. They’d both watched a number of their colleagues fall victim to it.

Perhaps Agatha’s chronic mantra of “if they wanted their essays back quickly, they should write something worth reading” had finally caught up to her.

But whimsy wasn’t exactly in her nature. There was another current in the air. Something brittle. Something mean.

Agatha was often mean. But not often without reason.

Rio dropped her bag by the door and followed the music to its source in the kitchen. Agatha was there, barefoot on the cold tile, wine bottle in one hand and a glass untouched on the counter.

She looked like someone who had been laughing for a bit too long. Someone who had laughed until it turned into something that hurt the ribs and brought tears to eyes.

Mirth had twisted into mania. Agatha’s eyes were a little too wide and her face flushed from more than wine.

Leaning against the doorframe, Rio cleared her throat, “You throwing a party I wasn’t invited to?”

Agatha spun, her smile razor-sharp. “Didn’t you hear? She’s dead.” She raised the bottle in a mock toast. “The old bitch finally croaked.”

There was no need to ask who.

Rio had only met Agatha’s mother once, and that had been enough. The woman had looked at Agatha like she was her greatest disappointment, and hadn’t paid Rio any attention at all.

Rio didn’t smile. “When?”

Agatha shrugged and took another swig from the bottle before pausing the music. “A couple hours ago. Stroke or heart failure or karma, I don’t know. I didn’t ask for details.”

Her laugh was loud and sharp. It threatened to rattle the dishes in the cabinets.

Rio stepped forward. Quietly. Carefully.

“Agatha.”

“Don’t,” she snapped, not turning around. “I don’t need to talk about it. I don’t. I’m fine. I’m —” Her voice cracked on the word, and she roughly swallowed it down. “She was a fucking monster. I should be dancing on the table.”

“You’re not,” Rio said gently.

Agatha’s hand tightened on the neck of the bottle. “I hated her.”

“I know.”

“She never once—”

“I know.”

Rio took a few tentative steps forward, palms up, careful not to spook her.

“You don’t have to grieve her,” Rio said. “But you’re allowed to feel something. Even if it’s just relief pretending to be something else.”

For a second, Agatha looked like she might cry. Or laugh again. Or shove Rio away just for having the audacity to see her a bit too clearly for her liking.

But instead, she just whispered, “I don’t know what I feel.”

And Rio finally reached for her, quietly wrapping her arms around her waist, pressing their foreheads together.

“That’s okay,” she said. “These things take time to unpack.”

Agatha didn’t cry.

Not when Rio held her in the kitchen. Not when they climbed into bed without brushing their teeth. Not even when Rio rolled over and found her still awake at four in the morning, staring blankly at the water stain on their ceiling.

By morning, the silence had calcified into something brittle.

The wineglass on the counter was still untouched. The bottle was half-empty. The world hadn’t ended.

And Agatha, as ever, got up and got to work.

She stood at her desk surrounded by open suitcases, half-folded black clothing, and a trove of books and legal documents. She didn’t look fragile. Agatha was never that. She wouldn’t allow it. But she looked frayed, like the edges of her were starting to come undone from the inside out.

“This is a mistake,” she muttered, flipping through her planner. “I shouldn’t be going. I should cancel, let the lawyer handle it. I have a deadline to make, I have classes to teach.”

“Agatha.” Rio sat on the arm of the couch, nursing a mug of coffee. “You can’t not go. The estate’s yours now.”

“A miserable rotting Victorian mausoleum filled with spite and generational trauma is now mine.” Agatha corrected. “And I still haven’t finished the third chapter.”

“Can you get an extension?”

Agatha laughed dryly. “They already gave me one. Remember? Two actually. I can’t ask again. This is supposed to be the book that proves I’m more than just a precocious conference darling. If I miss this deadline, everyone will think I already peaked and flamed out.”

Rio knew better than to offer empty comfort. Knew better than to say “you’re brilliant” or “no one who has ever met you could possibly forget you.” That wasn’t what Agatha needed.

What she needed was control. Rio had long accepted that in some ways, Agatha needed it to breathe. And right now, she was losing it.

She stood up and crossed the room. “Okay,” she said simply. “Then let me help.”

Agatha frowned. “With what?”

“The chapter. Just the outline. I’ll organize your notes, maybe pull some quotes…”

“I don’t need—”

“I know you don’t,” Rio said gently. “But you’ve been stuck for weeks, and if you don’t trust anyone else to touch it, trust me.”

Agatha hesitated, visibly torn. She opened her mouth to object—then closed it again.

Finally, she nodded once. Tight. “Just the outline.”

“Just the outline,” Rio agreed.

Agatha left for Boston. Rio tidied up the apartment. And then she got to work.

She reads Agatha’s scribbled notes. Half-sentences. Fragments. A line was scratched out so hard it had torn the page.

“All grief is a kind of theater. A performance of what is to be expected by either religious doctrine, societal practice, or personal manifestation.”

She remembered Agatha saying that line out loud a few weeks ago. Half-asleep, draped over a couch in the library. Rio had asked her what that had to do with the archetype of the crone in popular culture. Agatha had rolled her eyes and fired back that most women were only celebrated, missed, or recognized when they were dead.

The shift in their relationship had taken place on that same lumpy couch three years ago.

Rio had been halfway through a panic spiral about her thesis when she realized every book she needed— every single one —had already been checked out.

And there she was.

The thief herself, chewing on a pen cap, sprawled out over both cushions, hair a wild halo and marking pages in three open books at once.

“Excuse me,” she’d said, tight-lipped, “I had a hold on all of those.”

Agatha had blinked up at her, feline-like, slow and amused. “Oh, did you?” she asked sweetly, not moving an inch. “That’s awkward. I have a hold on this entire subject area.”

Rio bristled. “You know that’s not how this works. I had to get special approval. It took me weeks to-”

“And I just had to bully the punk at the research desk.” Agatha had tilted her head, considering. “Tell you what,” she said, eyes sparkling. “I’ll give you the Didion and the Dickinson if you take me to dinner and admit I’m right about your thesis being structurally unsound.”

Rio had stared at her.

“Fine,” Agatha had added, flipping a page idly. “We can skip dinner. You can take me home tonight, and we’ll do breakfast tomorrow. I’m flexible.”

Rio hadn’t meant to say yes. She’d meant to make a very stern point about library ethics and what a terrible idea it was to hook up with someone in the same cohort.

But instead—

There had been tequila shots at a dive bar two blocks from campus, a game of pool they both pretended wasn’t foreplay, and a very spirited debate about something that didn’t really matter that ended on the floor of Agatha’s apartment, half out of breath and tangled in an aggressively floral bedspread.

At one point, they took turns reading from Wuthering Heights in increasingly ridiculous accents until Agatha made her laugh so hard she wondered if a rib had cracked.

By the time morning came, Rio was nursing a headache and a growing suspicion that this was either the best or worst decision she’d made all semester.

Before she could answer that, she’d ended up across from her at a coffee shop the next morning watching Agatha dramatically describe the “total betrayal” of her supervisor vetoing the original title of her thesis: “Getting Off On the Funeral Pyre: The Erotics of Death in Contemporary Witch Iconography.”

“It’s evocative!” Agatha had declared, stabbing her sandwich for emphasis. “He wanted me to change it to something with the word ‘femininity’ in it.”

That date had also ended with them in bed.

It had been messy, breathless, funny. Easy.

And then it wasn’t just a night, or even two. It became a pattern so quickly it never felt like one formed at all. No choice was made, it just was.

They graded papers side-by-side on Agatha’s couch, sharing a single blanket. Rio would look up mid-rant about a student's incoherent thesis statement to find Agatha already holding out a highlighter.

They edited each other’s footnotes in bed. Debated Woolf in the shower. Argued about death and gender and Catholic funerals while brushing their teeth.

They ordered takeout more often than they cooked, and when they did attempt the latter, they usually ended up laughing on the kitchen floor while something burned in the oven.

Somewhere in the middle of all of it, Agatha started staying over. First with a toothbrush. Then with a drawer. Then with her grouchy pet rabbit, who immediately decided Rio’s pillow was his to nibble on now.

It wasn’t until months later, when Rio handed over to Agatha a new key and half her closet space, that she realized something strange.

She had never actually seen Agatha’s apartment.

Not once.

She’d never walked its halls. Never seen where Agatha kept her books or if she owned a coffee maker or what color her shower curtain was.

It struck her suddenly, like a gap in a story she hadn’t realized was missing.

The day they went to pack it up, it felt more like sorting through a stranger’s things than a lover’s. Most of the boxes were already labeled. There was little furniture. Even fewer decorations. A mattress on the floor and shelves lined only with books and folders. It felt like someone had lived there only on paper. Not in any real, inhabiting sense.

Agatha had shrugged when Rio said something. “It was temporary,” she said, like it explained everything.

But Rio wondered if there’d ever been a place Agatha had let herself live fully. If maybe she’d only just started.

With her.

Rio looked at the page in front of her again.

“All grief is a kind of theater…”

She added a line beneath it. A transition Agatha would have made mentally, but forgot to actually write. And then another. And another. The outline became a paragraph. The paragraph became a page.

She didn’t mean to keep going. But she kept writing. All through the weekend. All the way up until Agatha came home two days early.

Rio heard the door slam.

She didn’t move from the kitchen table, where the laptop still sat open, cursor blinking at the end of the last paragraph she'd written. Agatha’s voice had been easier to slip into than she’d expected. Familiar. Like a second skin.

“Rio.” Agatha called over the thump of her duffel hitting the floor.

Rio stood slowly, stepping into the hall like someone approaching a frightened and possibly feral animal.

She found Agatha leaned over the coffee table where Rio had spread revised manuscript pages out in a fan. Her hair was tied up, but messy in the way it got when she’d been tugging at it. Her dark navy coat was still on.

Agatha didn’t look at Rio, eyes fixed on her words, their words, on the page. “You said you’d work on an outline.”

Rio swallowed. “I did.”

“This is not just an outline.”

“I know.”

Agatha turned a page, then another.

“You wrote an entire chapter of my book.”

Rio shrugged. “It was a weird week without you here.”

She stepped closer, testing the air. “Look, it’s still all your work. Your research, your structure, your voice. I just… coaxed it into existence.”

Agatha’s eyes finally met hers. There was no heat in them but Rio couldn’t make sense of the expression. Eventually, Agatha blinked. “It’s good.”

Rio braced herself. “Is that an actual ‘good’ or a ‘Rio I actually mean it when I say you shouldn’t have’?”

“No. It’s too good,” Agatha muttered. “You wrote it like I would’ve. If I weren’t exhausted and mentally...”

She rubbed her temples like she could knead the guilt out of her brain.

Rio moved closer. “Then let me give this to you. Let me help you. You deserve to get through this deadline with some of your sanity intact.”

Agatha finally looked at her again. The expression was softer now, but still tangled up in too many things: exhaustion, hesitation, some kind of gratitude she wasn’t ready to name. And maybe just a flicker of something more.

“I don’t know if I do,” she said.

Rio’s voice dropped. “You do. Let me help.”

There was a pause, long enough for Rio to wonder if she’d overstepped everything.

But then Agatha reached forward and started gathering the pages into a careful stack, one by one. Not speaking. Not looking up. Just taking them back like they were hers all along.

Ten Years Later

Agatha had only agreed to attend the faculty mixer on the promise of a decent cheese board. That was it. That was the only reason she’d put on mascara and a blazer and heels that clicked too loudly and made her ankles hurt.

Lilia had sworn that the caterer was French this year.

That was a fucking lie.

Agatha was mid-bite of something that might’ve been Gouda, or maybe was just damp Havarti, when she saw the ghost.

Not a ghost.

An ex.

She did not choke, but only because she was well accustomed to taking in a bit too much all at once.

Beneath a fluttering banner that read Welcome Back Faculty! stood Rio Vidal.

Smiling. Laughing. Thriving.

Her hair was much shorter now, curled neatly behind her ears just barely brushing her collar. She wore a sage green shirt was unbuttoned just low enough to suggest charm without crossing into scandal. She looked confident. Relaxed. Like she belonged here.

Agatha’s stomach curled in on itself. It could’ve been the cheese, lactose didn’t agree with her these days. But probably not.

Lilia stood beside Rio, gesturing enthusiastically as she introduced her to a knot of professors Agatha only barely tolerated. She caught a snippet as Lilia’s voice floated across the room.

“…visiting scholar for the year…”

Agatha set her cocktail napkin down with surgical precision. She had tenure. She was not supposed to be surprised by things. Certainly not ambushed at the charcuterie table by the ghost of her ill-advised twenties.

Rio hadn’t seen her yet. Or if she had, she was doing an Academy Award-winning job pretending otherwise.

Agatha’s fingers curled around the stem of her wine glass. Her pulse was doing something undignified. She took a steadying sip.

It didn’t help.

The last time she’d seen Rio was nine years ago, standing in the doorway of their apartment with a fellowship acceptance letter in one hand and her heart in the other.

The fight had lasted hours—about timing, about distance, about whose dreams got to come first. About whether love should be enough, or if wanting more made them selfish.

Agatha hadn’t asked her to stay. Rio hadn’t asked her to come. They were both too proud. Too afraid. Too convinced that if the other one really loved them, they wouldn’t have to ask.

Agatha had slammed the door. Rio hadn’t come back.

Until now. Here she was—back in Agatha’s department, shaking hands and dazzling half the faculty, as if she didn’t have a single unkind memory between them.

Asshole.

Agatha’s presence at that mixer lasted fifty-seven minutes. Fifty minutes longer than expected, honestly.

She smiled and swirled her glass. Nodded at all the right moments. She hadn’t screamed. She hadn’t bolted. A triumph, by all accounts.

But when Lilia clinked her fork to her glass and cheerfully announced, “Let’s raise a glass to our returning star, Dr. Vidal, who will be guest teaching a seminar on death in popular culture. I’m sure many of you are already familiar with her work The Littlest Death: Mourning, Memory, and the Dinosaur ,”

—Agatha’s filter gave out.

She didn’t mean to say it aloud. Truly. But the words slipped out before her brain could intervene.

“ Land Before Time gave me mommy issues.”

The room went still.

Lilia blinked, her smile tightening around the edges. “I’m sorry, Agatha? Was that… a comment?”

Agatha blinked. “Hmm? Oh. Nothing.” She sipped her wine. “Just—nothing. I was referencing… Freud.”

Someone actually nodded, as if that made sense. Agatha took that as her cue to vanish.

She was already backing toward the exit when she felt it. The hum at her back. A shift in the air. A gravitational pull.

“Trying to sneak off before saying hi?”

Agatha turned.

And there she was.

Rio.

In that infuriating sage green suit, shirt collar open just enough to make it impossible to focus on anything else. Her hair was shorter now, neatly curled behind her ears, cropped in a way that said intentional and unbothered . It had never been this short when Agatha had known her.

This was hair Agatha had never fisted in her hand. Had never brushed aside before kissing the corner of her mouth. This version of Rio hadn’t once pressed Agatha to a wall or murmured anything with the weight of “I can't get how you taste out of my head.”

She was new.

Except not.

Her mouth hadn’t changed. Still curved into a slow, crooked smile that always meant trouble first, remorse maybe later if I feel like it . That slant of wickedness was familiar. So was the tilt of her head, the way her eyes flicked down and then up again—assessing, measuring, daring.

Agatha’s stomach flipped.

It had been nearly nine years.

She thought about how all the skin cells in a body are replaced every seven.

Which meant, technically, this was a body she had never touched. Not once.

And yet her own remembered things she didn’t give it permission to—how Rio’s fingers used to tap against her leg when she was thinking, the exact spot beneath her jaw that made her breath hitch, the sound of her laugh in the middle of an argument, warm and infuriating all at once.

“You’ve got to be kidding,” Agatha muttered, throat dry.

Rio smiled wider.

“You know, I should have expected this from you. Sulking by the cheese table. Whispering critiques like curses.”

Agatha narrowed her eyes. “I wasn’t sulking.”

“No,” Rio said, stepping closer, “you were brooding. Entirely different discipline. But you’re pretty familiar with that one too.”

Agatha refused to give her the satisfaction of seeing her flinch. “What are you even doing here?”

“Lilia invited me back. Visiting scholar for the year.” She spread her arms slightly, as if presenting herself on a game show. “New grant. New seminar. Same campus ghosts.”

Agatha took a long sip of her wine, then paused. “You knew I was still here.”

Rio’s grin dimmed just slightly. “I suspected.”

“Of course you did.”

They stood there a second too long, staring each other down, sizing each other up.

Then, in a burst of poorly timed movement—Agatha turning to walk away, Rio shifting to block her path—they collided.

A splash of wine arced through the air in slow motion.

Agatha gasped. Cold rosé bloomed across her chest, dripping down the front of her cream silk blouse.

“Unbelievable,” she hissed, snatching a cocktail napkin from the table and blotting furiously. “Do you do this on purpose? Or is it just muscle memory by now—ruining things?”

Rio glanced down at the stain, then back up, calm as ever. “You know, that might’ve been the last decent pour in the room. Tragic loss, really.”

Agatha blinked. “You’re concerned about the wine ?”

“Well,” Rio said, all effortless charm, “you’re already soaked. That’s done. The wine could have been so much better enjoyed.”

Agatha gritted her teeth. “It’s always a joke with you, isn’t it?”

“No,” Rio said, that infuriatingly slow smile tugging at her mouth. “Sometimes it’s a coping mechanism.”

Agatha’s eyes narrowed. “I don’t care about how you’re coping. I need to change clothes.”

Rio’s gaze flicked to the curve of her shoulder where the wine had left pink streaks like an arterial spray. “Shame. That blouse looked expensive, I’d offer you my jacket but I remember you prefer to peel the layers away yourself.”

They stood like that for another beat—too close, too familiar, tension thick enough to spread across the room and pull some curious looks in their direction.

Then Rio jerked her chin toward the hallway. “Does the faculty lounge still have that sad little bathroom with the creaky sink?”

Agatha arched her brow. “Why? Are you offering to blot me dry?”

“Not unless you ask nicely.”

Agatha rolled her eyes so hard it was a miracle she didn’t strain a nerve. “In your dreams.”

Rio leaned in, voice low and warm with a slow, teasing bite. “Oh, darling. In my dreams you don’t run away after. That’s how I know they’re dreams.”

If Agatha still had a drink, she would have thrown it. Instead, she let out a sound of pure frustration, spun on her heel, and stalked toward her office, shoulders stiff and stained blouse clinging to her ribs like a scarlet letter.

Behind her, Rio didn’t follow.

Agatha had once naively believed tenure came with certain unspoken privileges. Privacy. Respect. Maybe even an office with walls thick enough to muffle the smug clicks of Rio Vidal’s mechanical keyboard.

She had been mistaken.

The wall they now shared offered no defense. Her morning ritual of coffee and the Strands puzzle had been upended by the sound of typing that sounded more like machine gun fire.

Not the thoughtful, occasional tap of a seasoned academic crafting words one by one. No. Rio typed like she was playing percussion for those awful punk bands she liked. Agatha could hear her pause, backspace, laugh to herself, and then start again faster.

It was criminal. It was a nuisance.

She had considered filing a noise complaint. Or pulling the fire alarm. She’d consider hurling herself at the window if her office weren’t on the ground floor.

Agatha hadn’t spoken to Rio since the wine incident. She intended to keep it that way.

It didn’t last long.

A week into the fall term, she found herself cornered in the hall by an overeager third-year student. Camille? Camilla? She didn’t bother learning their names this early. Not until they had earned it.

“I just wanted to say,” the girl chirped, “Dr. Vidal’s lecture on grief constructs was amazing . Like, mind-blowing. And the way she tied it back to your chapter on mythic mourning? Literal chills.”

Agatha blinked. “My… chapter?”

“Oh! From Ritual Remains. She quoted it. Well, paraphrased. But, like, she definitely knew it inside and out. You two have such, like, complementary energies.”

Agatha smiled in the brittle, professional way that meant “I hope your blind enthusiasm leads to you falling into a ditch.”

But it was too early in the semester to deal with student grievances, so instead she bared her teeth in a smile and nodded.

“That’s very… observant.”

Camille grinned and scurried off, probably to Rio’s already over-capacity class.

Back in the hallway, a cluster of students—some enrolled, some not—spilled out from the doorway of Rio’s seminar. Dr. Vidal apparently allowed them to stand in the back. Sit on the floor. Lounge, really. Some brought snacks.

Snacks.

Unheard of.

It is as if a bunch of quasi-adults couldn’t manage their appetite for a couple of hours without a protein bar, and the crinkling of plastic wrappers undermines the sanctity of higher education.

Agatha’s seminars had a syllabus that rivaled most legal contracts. No phones. No laptops without written justification. No speaking unless you’d done the reading— the full reading , not just a skimmed abstract and spared a hopeful glance at Wikipedia.

Rio’s class, on the other hand, sounded more like a TED Talk crossed with a socratic sleepover. Students laughed. Debated. One girl leaned her head on another’s shoulder while splitting a Pop-Tart.

Agatha’s students called her “Professor Harkness.” They came to her office hours like they were walking into a lion’s den: prepared, rehearsed, braced. Only the strong survived.

Rio’s students called her by her name . Just Rio. With a familiarity that made Agatha’s skin itch. A majority of them followed her around campus like ducklings imprinting on their cool, emotionally available proxy older sibling.

And then there was the applause. Not the hesitant, polite kind. But real, enthusiastic applause that rolled down the corridor like thunder.

Applause. In an academic seminar. Ridiculous

Agatha’s students left her classroom with fifteen pages of notes, a minor existential crisis, a renewed fear of being publicly eviscerated for sloppy argumentation.

Rio’s students left glowing. Inspired. Occasionally crying.

It was charisma masquerading as pedagogy.

It was a mockery. It infuriated her.

Agatha turned on her heel, jaw tight, footsteps echoing in protest as she strode away.

Behind her, laughter spilled out into the hall again—light, unbothered, completely immune to the grinding machinery of academic rigor Agatha had built her career upon.

She kept her eyes forward as she pushed through the hallway. Didn’t glance into the classroom. Definitely didn’t notice that Rio, mid-discussion, caught her in her peripheral vision and smirked.

Agatha made it another week and a half before she cracked.

It started innocently enough. She was restocking her office tea drawer—earl grey, peppermint, the rooibos blend that made her feel kind when grading papers—when she overheard three students loitering.

“…she played a song at the start of the lecture. Said it was about emotional decay or something—”

“She said Dr. Harkness’s work is like a scalpel—precise, cold, and ‘dead sexy.’”

“Well, she would know, wouldn’t she?”

Agatha dropped the tin of chamomile.

That night, she made a plan.

If Rio was going to turn her class into some kind of grief-themed open mic night featuring Agatha’s theories, the least Agatha could do was verify the academic integrity. Quietly. Anonymously.

When she strode onto campus the next morning, her usual sharp blazer was gone. Instead, she pulled on a battered hoodie she found stuffed in the back of her closet. It was navy and slightly oversized. She didn’t even remember where it came from.

She tugged the hood up, added a pair of massive sunglasses, and slouched her posture until she resembled every other half-asleep or hungover grad student.

A touch of lipstick, she decided, would be suspicious.

She blended in.

Almost.

The classroom was packed. Students lined the back wall, some on their laptops, others scribbling in notebooks. Someone had brought popcorn.

Agatha slipped in and took a seat near the far back corner, clutching her travel mug.

Rio was already mid-lecture, pacing at the front. Her sleeves were rolled and her tie loose, and Agatha understood—begrudgingly—why she’d quickly received the chili pepper next to her name on that awful professor rating site.

Not that Agatha had checked.

It wasn’t a big deal. She had one too.

And it wasn’t like she regularly scrolled through RateMyProfessor with a glass of wine and an annotated copy of her own syllabus to cross-reference any inaccuracies. She merely… glanced. Occasionally. For calibration purposes.

Her reviews, at least, had a certain gravitas. A mix of fear, awe, and academic trauma.

“Dr. Harkness is terrifying but brilliant. I cried after the midterm and still consider it the most formative intellectual experience of my life.”

“Hot in a way that makes you want to do better out of fear.”

“I don’t know what’s more terrifying, her blue eyes or her exams.”

“If you survive her, you’ll never be afraid of anything again.”

Rio’s comments, on the other hand, read more like fever dream love letters than course feedback.

“Dr. Vidal is the reason I declared a major in cultural studies. Also the reason I broke up with my ex.”

“She will give you extra credit if you tell her your favorite dinosaur and why in extreme detail. Also… really hot.”

“Somehow she made the phrase ‘liminal trauma loop’ sound seductive. I still don’t know what that means. But I do know I’d die for her.”

Agatha’s eye twitched.

She watched Rio lean casually against the edge of the lectern, gesturing with a pencil she didn’t appear to be using, laughing at something a student said as if this were brunch and not a graduate seminar.

This was not teaching. This was performance art.

And she was good at it.

Which, frankly, made it worse.

Rio’s voice brought her crashing back.

“...and that brings us back to the idea of socially legible grief,” she was saying, “which we’ve all participated in, whether we’ve realized it or not. Whether it's mourning a public figure’s death, or crying in the first fifteen minutes of a kid’s movie, or, hell, even how we talk about extinct species. Some loss is performative. But that doesn’t mean it’s not real.”

A student raised their hand. Rio fielded the tepid question gracefully, leaning on the lectern with her elbow like she had all the time in the world. Agatha watched, irritated by how at ease she was. How easily the room bent toward her.

Agatha didn’t even realize she’d relaxed slightly, drawn into the magic of a master class, until Rio’s eyes swept across the room.

And stopped.

Dead on her.

There was no hesitation. No flicker of surprise. Just a slow, mischievous grin curling across Rio’s mouth, like a cat who’d just spotted something small and twitchy and delicious.

She didn’t miss a beat.

“You,” she said, pointing toward the back. “In the hoodie with great taste in music. What’s the last movie that made you cry?”

Agatha’s spine locked up like a sprung trap.

She had been lulled, ambushed really, by Rio’s cadence, the kind of easy authority that made students forget to check their phones. Now all eyes turned toward the back, swiveling to find the girl with the band hoodie and excellent taste in music.

She should’ve just said a title. Any title. Up . Titanic . That documentary about orphaned baby penguins.

Instead, she stared at Rio.

Rio, who leaned casually against the lectern like she was asking a friend about their weekend.

“You’ve got that look,” Rio said lightly. “The ‘I’m too emotionally repressed to admit Million Dollar Baby broke me’ look.”

A few students laughed. Agatha’s eye twitched.

She cleared her throat. “I don’t… cry during movies.”

She was rattled.

And Rio still had her fucking jacket.

Today, Agatha waited.

Then she cut in line.

Agatha gave the waiting gaggle of grad students a look sharp enough to strip paint and opened the door without knocking.

She didn’t know when Rio started wearing glasses.

But immediately wanted to know why she didn’t wear them all the time.

She cleared her throat. “We need to talk.”

Rio didn’t answer right away. She took off the glasses slowly, folding them and tucking them inside her interior jacket pocket. Her expression didn’t give anything away—no smug smile, no obvious irritation.

Just a single raised eyebrow.

“Here about the jacket?” she asked mildly.

Agatha rolled her eyes. “Don’t start.”

Rio gestured toward the chair across from her desk. “Then why don’t you sit down and tell me where you want to start instead?”

Agatha hesitated. Her hand twitched at her side.

Then she stepped inside. Shut the door. And sat.

“I owe you an apology,” Agatha said, finally. “For the other night. For… everything.”

Rio nodded. “I would like to understand what happened there. I texted you to make sure you got home okay and heard nothing. I was worried about you.”

“I didn’t come here to rehash all of it,” Agatha said quickly, “but I know I messed up. Then. And now.”

Agatha exhaled. “I know you must still be furious about the chapter. That I never credited you properly. And now you’re back, you’re so respected in the field, and I can’t stop thinking —what if you decide to tell someone? What if this is it, the end of everything I’ve built because I didn’t know how to say thank you when it mattered?”

Rio tilted her head, confused. “Wait… is that what you think I’m upset about?”

“You said it yourself,” Agatha snapped. “In the parking lot. You said I took your words and buried them.”

“I did,” Rio said. “But that’s not—” She paused and looked at Agatha, clearly searching for the right words.

Agatha looked down at her lap. “You showed back up, and I haven’t been able to breathe right since.”

Rio swiveled back and forth in her chair a few times, drumming her fingers against the desk.

Then Rio asked, softly, “Have you ever noticed how the most honest conversations happen in cars?”

Agatha blinked. “What?”

“You know,” Rio said, pushing her chair back and rising to her feet, “you’re not looking directly at each other, there’s the hum of the engine between you, the pressure of moving forward—it’s easier. Somehow. You’ve got to have the conversation because there’s no way out of it.”

“That’s—” Agatha started to argue that she could always throw herself out of a vehicle to end an uncomfortable chat entirely, but stopped. “Okay. Sure. Maybe.”

Rio reached for her coat. “Come for a drive with me.”

Agatha’s brow furrowed. “Now?”

“Why not?”

Agatha hesitated.

She could have said no. Should have, maybe. There were papers to grade, emails to ignore, a bottle of wine waiting at home she could have poured and used as an excuse to do nothing at all.

But something in Rio’s steady voice unsettled her in a way that made standing still feel more dangerous than moving forward.

So she nodded once. Barely. And followed.

The hum of the engine filled the silence as Rio pulled out of the parking lot. The late afternoon light cast everything in honey-colored warmth, but the quiet between them stayed sharp.

Agatha sat stiffly in the passenger seat, hands in her lap, like she wasn’t sure what to do with them.

Rio didn’t say anything for a while. Just drove.

They passed the old bookstore. The turnoff that used to lead to their favorite coffee shop. The intersection where Agatha once screamed at a jaywalker mid-argument and Rio laughed so hard she nearly rear-ended a Prius.

Finally, Rio spoke.

“Do you remember why we broke up?”

Agatha stared out the window. “We fought. You got that fellowship. And we both said shitty things.”

“That’s the ending,” Rio said. “I’m asking if you remember the beginning of the end.”

Agatha’s jaw clenched. “I was scared, okay? You wanted me to follow you across the country, leave everything I’d been building. For what? To sit in someone else’s borrowed apartment and maybe write during the hours you weren’t working?”

“You think that’s what I was asking?” Rio glanced at her. “To give everything up for me?” “You asked me to come with you.”

“I asked you to come with me,” Rio said, voice quiet but firm. “Not to disappear into me.”

Agatha looked down at her lap. “It felt like the same thing.”

Rio nodded slowly, eyes back on the road. “Yeah. I know it did. You were drowning back then. The book, the department politics, your mother… You didn’t want to be saved. You wanted to survive on your own or not at all.”

Agatha didn’t argue. She couldn’t. Not when it was true.

“I thought if I left, you’d hate me,” Rio continued. “But if I stayed, I think I would’ve started to resent you. And that felt worse.”

Agatha swallowed. “So you left.”

“I did.”

They drove in silence for another minute. The road narrowed into a two-lane highway flanked by trees.

“I used to think,” Agatha said finally, “that if you didn’t bleed for someone, it wasn’t real. That love always had to hurt. And that it if it didn't, it wasn't something that could last.”

“And now?”

Agatha looked out the window. “Now I think I was young. And wrong.”

Rio smiled faintly. “You were both. So was I.”

The road curved.

“You could’ve told me that then,” Agatha said, voice quieter now.

“You wouldn’t have heard me.”

Agatha nodded. “No. I wouldn’t have.”

The car went quiet again, but the silence between them had shifted. It wasn’t sharp anymore. Just weighted. Familiar. The kind of quiet that used to stretch between them on late nights, when words weren’t necessary, when a glance or a hand on a knee said enough. But tonight, there was more to say. It just wasn’t ready to be said yet.

Rio’s fingers tapped lightly on the steering wheel, a steady rhythm she didn’t seem to notice.

Agatha exhaled slowly, then asked, “So where are we going, exactly?”

Rio didn't answer right away. Just kept driving, the road winding higher, the trees thinning out into patches of open sky. The air cooled with the altitude, and Agatha could feel her ears pop as they climbed.

She glanced sideways and tried for a joke. “You’re not about to murder me and bury me in the woods, are you?”

Rio smirked. “No. I can’t afford to get blood on the seats, it’s a rental.”

A few minutes later, they crested the hill, and Rio turned into a gravel turnout. A weathered wooden rail framed the edge of the overlook.

Below them, the town unfurled full of rooftops, amber-lit windows, and the glint of distant campus lights. Above them, the last streaks of pink slipped from the sky.

Rio killed the engine.

Agatha glanced around, finally recognizing where they were. She smiled, despite herself. “You know, we never came here just to talk.”

Rio raised an eyebrow, unbuckling her seatbelt. “You saying you have expectations?”

“I’m saying if you wanted to just talk,” Agatha replied, lips twitching, “you didn’t need to bring me somewhere I’m inclined to dive into the back seat out of muscle memory.”

Rio laughed, "I don't know if my knees could still take that, but who knows? What if this time I gave you a different view?" And Agatha felt something tight in her chest loosen, just slightly.

They got out of the car, cool air brushing their skin. Rio leaned against the hood, and Agatha joined her.

For a while, they just looked out over the view, the town that had shaped and scarred them both glowing soft beneath the twilight.

Rio broke the silence. “Do you remember the last time we were up here?”

Agatha considered, then nodded. “You brought hot cider. I brought a flask.”

“And then you fell asleep on my shoulder while I read to you.”

“I was pretending to be asleep,” Agatha said. “I just liked listening to you.”

Rio looked over at her. “You were always better at pretending than admitting.”

Agatha didn’t flinch this time. “Yeah. I know. I pretended to like all your shitty poems,” she teased.

“And for months I acted like sleeping with socks on wasn’t absolutely psychotic behavior.”

The wind picked up. Leaves rustled below them.

Rio’s voice softened. “You know. When Lilia reached out, I thought about what it would be like to see you again. I didn’t come back to dredge things up. I came back because I couldn’t stop wondering what we lost. And if it was still there.”

Agatha met her gaze. And for once, there was no mask. No edge.

Just her.

She reached out, tentative but sure, and laced their fingers together.

“I don’t know what this is,” she said. “But I’m tired of pretending it isn’t still something.”

Rio squeezed her hand, thumb brushing over her knuckle in a quiet promise.

“That’s a start,” she said.

The semester continues on, clicking forward like Rio’s goddamn keyboard.

Agatha doesn’t get used to it.

But doesn’t complain anymore either. She just starts bringing noise-canceling headphones to work. But usually leaves one ear open, just in hopes of catching Rio’s laugh from the office next door.

Agatha buys a second toothbrush for her apartment, and Rio breaks her lease.

Agatha lets her students eat in class— “just this once.” Then again.

They still argue. Of course they do.

But now they usually end with someone biting back a smile.

They spend most nights together. And somewhere between January and February, most nights become all of them.

As the end of the semester looms, Rio receives half a dozen new teaching offers. She accepts Lilia’s without fanfare. Just leave her signed contract on Agatha’s kitchen counter beside the kettle.

The final proofs of Agatha’s new book arrive in a neat little box. She reads them all in one sitting, pen in hand, but finds very little to change.

The dedication makes her pause. She rewrites it three times.

On the fourth, she finally feels that it’s good enough.

The bookstore was packed.

Not in the polite, tepid way most academic launches were, but genuinely filled. Students lined the back wall, faculty took seats in the front, and someone had even arranged for flowers, which Agatha suspected was Lilia’s doing.

A stack of her newly released book, To Bury the Witch: Feminine Myth in a Culture of Loss, sat neatly beside the lectern, glossy covers gleaming under the too-warm track lighting.

Agatha stood in front of the crowd in her favorite blazer—black, sharp-shouldered, no- nonsense. But her lipstick was softer tonight. A red with more warmth than bite.

She wasn’t here to devour. She was here to offer something.

Her fingers curled around the edge of the podium. The low murmur of the crowd settled as she cleared her throat.

“Thank you all for coming,” she said. Her voice didn’t tremble. “I’ve spent the last three years thinking about how we grieve, what we’re allowed to mourn, and what we pretend not to. This book is... an attempt to pull apart the mythologies we build around endings. And maybe, in doing so, find a way forward.”

A quiet hush settled over the room.

From the back, Rio stood with her arms loosely folded, a lazy half-smile on her lips. Her glasses, she’d taken to wearing more at Agatha’s insistence, were slipping down her nose.

Agatha caught her eye and smiled back.

She turned a page.

“I’ve been asked a lot about the dedication,” she said lightly. “So I’ll end tonight by reading it as long as you all promise not to ask any follow-up questions.”

The titter of laughter gave her a moment to compose herself.

She glanced down at the inside cover, her thumb pausing on the edge of the page.

“For R.V. – We wrote this one together. You just let me hold the pen.”
