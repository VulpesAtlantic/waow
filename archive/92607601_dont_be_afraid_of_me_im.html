Summary

“Shall we?”

Rio jumped at the voice, playful but heavy and echoing in her mind and breaking her stupor, that voice sank in her flesh, in her bones and Rio knew, before turning around that the stranger, her stranger was standing next to her cab.

“You.”

“Me,” the stranger smiled.

And Rio opened the door for her.

 

Or: New York, 1992, Rio is a lonely cab driver. Trapped in her little yellow box, she canvasses the city. Long hours, working nights, strange encounters, she endures her life more than she enjoys it.
But one night, she picks up a client not like the others.

Or: Taxi driver Rio obsesses over serial killer Agatha

Chapter 1: One

 

Rio had thought about stopping for a cup of coffee at the convenience store near the end of 127th Street; it was late and perhaps the bitter black coffee would chase the growing sleepiness that crept along her spine since her 6th fare of that night; although she could still taste the slightly sour remnants of her last cup on the back of her tongue, Rio decided that the usual twitchiness of her muscles that came with drinking yet another double shot of espresso was worth enduring to make her night go by quicker. 

By the time the decision had been made to park her cab along the road, leave her seat even for a minute, and stretch her legs on the warm curb of this street where people were fanning themselves with all kinds of papers or magazines in the hopes of escaping the insufferable heat of their homes, someone had already hopped into the back of her taxi.

“Drive,” said the woman. 

Because it was a woman, Rio glanced at her in the mirror but only caught sight of her profile, hidden behind long strands of dark hair. 

The stranger didn’t look at Rio, didn’t buckle her seatbelt, didn’t say anything else. 

She only fixed her gaze on the street, the busy street of a spectacularly warm summer night where Rio hoped to have purchased her fourth cup of coffee. 

But Rio didn’t drive, because Rio needed directions.

She needed to know whether to drive west along 127th St or to turn around east, she needed to know if, at the next intersection, she would have to turn left or right, if she went straight, did she go until the river or would she need to stop before that. 

People didn’t tell Rio to just drive, they gave her an address or a direction, sometimes it was just “North” or “South” but Rio could work with that; she was good with directions.

So no, Rio didn’t drive, she sat in the rumbling car until the woman – the stranger – gave her more to go on, she was strange, this stranger, she sat at the back of Rio’s cab with her shoulders straight and her head held high, perfectly calm for someone with such a vague request, seemingly content to be in the backseat of this cab on this particularly warm night on this busy street, as if, being here, with Rio, was exactly the place she ought to be.

People aren’t themself in Rio’s cab; people are rarely themself in any situation that involves a stranger driving them around, people fidget, people feign ease, people are late or stressed, they talk – sometimes too much; people are too tense or too relaxed, people are drunk or worse, but this woman, this stranger was everything those people aren’t.

She was herself and only herself, calm in the back of a cab at midnight in the middle of the city on a warm, warm summer’s night.

“Drive,” she said again, somehow calmer and yet firmer, as if Rio’s hesitation was entirely predictable and not at all an inconvenience to her mysterious plan of driving aimlessly around the city.

So, Rio drove. 

The windows were down, letting in the warm air of the night.

Rio limited the use of her AC as its noise and its smell squeezed her brain in ways she’d rather not drive with, it elicited an ache behind her eyes and she always had the distinct sensation that the vile, artificially cold air would soon creep into her lungs, into her bloodstream, into the very cells of her being and change her on a fundamental level.

Rio knew that this was false, this was a fantasy; she knew, because people told her so when she exposed her concerns to the other cab drivers, none shared her fears, all laughed, discreetly, they thought, but Rio knew well enough that the other cab drivers looked at her funny.

She turned left on Madison Avenue and kept going despite the steady silence of the woman, who was still turned to the window watching the streets, maybe, as the warm summer air gently swept through her hair. 

The woman didn't react when Rio took another turn, when she stopped at a red light, when she drove through the busy streets of the city.

Rio could have easily forgotten her, her silence and her stillness absolute in the back of her car, but the woman’s silence and stillness had the opposite effect.

Instead of forgetting her, there was little Rio could do to divert her attention from this woman.

Her silence and her stillness infused the whole car until Rio had to breathe her in, she imagined this silence, this stillness swelling in her lungs, seeping into her blood and spreading in her body through the thousands and thousands of arteries, veins and capillaries that made her.

Oddly, Rio didn’t fight this intrusion in her system, it didn’t feel twitchy like the coffee or toxic like the AC, the silence and the stillness of the stranger, coated her burning nerves with a welcoming coolness, and with her eyes fixed on the road, Rio didn’t feel sleepy or twitchy anymore, just silent and still.

The woman shifted, just a bit, just a hair and Rio understood that with this miniscule shift that the stranger wanted her to change directions, so Rio did, almost immediately.

She turned a corner, then another and drove until she found a bridge.

Even now, years later, Rio still couldn’t tell you what the bridge was called.

Taxi driver every night of every week of every month for five years, along the same streets of the same city that never sleeps just as Rio never sleeps, always driving endlessly, people to places, places to people and still, Rio could not tell what bridge she crossed to drive the stranger away from Manhattan.  

The car went east perhaps or north, Rio recognized the streets that she knew so well, the buildings that she used to pass by, every night of every week of every month of every year.

After enough time a place sinks into you like wine on a linen cloth, it settles into your synapses and into your brain, it leaves a stain that remains even after thousands of washes.

Driving around, Rio’s brain created something more than a map, something deeper than topology, an understanding of the city, of its systems, of its ways.

But then and there, when Rio turned the wheel, she didn’t follow the map in her brain, when Rio slowed at a stop sign, drove again, turned left or right, she followed the woman, the stranger, sitting at the back of her cab, the stranger drove through Rio, through the city into this very warm night.

And Rio, for the first time in her entire existence, didn't think.

Her silly, endlessly buzzing little brain was finally quiet.

The woman drove Rio deeper into the city, in places Rio knew but didn’t recognize.

She drove twitchy little Rio deep into her sense of calm. 

She drove to the rhythm of the pulsing city, above its masses, slithering between buildings, driving for the sake of driving.

The air became cooler as they reached wider streets, the city became quieter as people went to bed.

Only then remained the silence and the stillness of the car, silence and stillness now shared between them like the gift that it was.

Her yellow cab was now the only constant in the sea or darkness, a last bastion of silence and stillness, so precious, eternally driving between the faceless buildings of this mad city-

“Stop here.”

It stung her ears, sharp and violent in the deep silence.

But Rio stopped.

The woman didn't move for a second and Rio almost thought –hoped! that she misheard, but she couldn’t have misheard because she stopped and there were no thoughts in her stupid little brain so if Rio stopped it was because the stranger decided it.

Rio felt her eyes on the back of her neck, they stung almost as much as her voice, calm, firm, decided, like everything the woman, the stranger ever did.

Her neck prickled, she waited for the silence to be broken again, she couldn’t tell why but her breath accelerated, her heart beat furiously in the depth of her ribcage and the woman simply waited.

It took everything, everything in her blood and in her nerves to turn around.

The stranger was closer than Rio would have guessed, she was here, here before Rio and Rio never did see her face before, God she didn’t see her face but she saw it now, how blinded can Rio be by a face? It was a face, just a face, Rio screamed in her head, but it wasn’t.

The woman, smirked, aware, very aware and Rio tore her gaze away from that face –what a face! – to the woman’s hand outstretched to her, a few bills offered to her.

“Keep the change,” She said, and Rio took the bills.

The movement felt off, so off even though it was her job, a transaction that took place every night of every week of every month of every year since she started; Rio drove, the passengers paid, but then, when the woman, offered her money, Rio wasn’t so sure what to do with it anymore, this drive wasn’t a fare, because it wasn’t Rio that drove them around but the stranger through Rio.

The woman, the stranger, left without a word, only a last glance in her direction, a glance so real that it got embedded into the synapses of Rio’s stupid little brain, for ever and ever in her grey matter, the blue of her eyes, bluer than the deep, deep sea, when the water is silent but so powerful.

Rio watched her cross the street, somehow nonchalant in her hurry, she walked, elegant, powerful, knowing that the world would sooner stop spinning before she would ever be inconvenienced and so she caught her bus.

Her bus?

Her bus.

The stranger hopped on a bus.

Who takes a taxi to a bus stop?

Long seconds or maybe minutes pass while Rio, sitting in her cab, kept her eyes fixed on the street where the woman disappeared into a bus, an ordinary bus.

She watched as said bus left the curb, left the street, left Rio, alone in her car lost, now that the woman, the stranger, had left in an ordinary bus.

Why take a bus when one can take a cab? This woman, this stranger was so out of the ordinary that imagining her in an ordinary bus made Rio uneasy. 

The image conjured in her head clashed with everything she thought to have perceived of this woman.

Although, when she thought more about it, her thoughts still sluggish and fuzzy, like having been woken up after a long restful night, she couldn’t really say what made the stranger clash with the ordinary bus.

She didn’t dress particularly fancy, she was a young woman, a young woman like so many in the city, that took buses to go to places, so why was the thought of that woman, that stranger, in a bus among others so jarring to Rio?

Rio didn’t start for another minute, now the ordinary bus was long gone, the woman, the stranger, was driven away by another driver into the depths of the city, where she would go on and live life as Rio stayed here alone in her cab.

That was it! For her to be driven by another driver, that someone else, someone random would be privileged to drive the woman, the stranger, when Rio felt as if she and only she could understand the cataclysmic implications of driving her around – no, not to drive her around but to be driven by her into the bowels of the city.

Someone climbed in her cab and Rio growled at this new passenger, they paid her no mind, throwing an address at her then minding their own business and Rio drove.

Gradually, her senses came back together and it was a few blocks away that she reconnected with the map in her mind.

What a relief! she thought as she led her cab into the avenues, in the streets, over  the bridges, what a relief to know where she was driving, to have a clear direction, to understand so completely how the city operated.

The stranger from before had led her into murky waters, what a lovely thing to know where one is going, Rio thought to herself, to be safe, to be locked in the route.

The night continued as it always did, Rio drove, people came and went, all the same, sitting at the back of her cab, requesting fares that appeared so boring to Rio now.

She felt safe in her little cab, touring a city she knew by heart, streets she had driven through hundreds if not thousands of times.

How dull.

Rio had never held such grudges for those unimaginative routes, a club to a home, a bar to a hotel, a hotel to a home, a home to a club, again and again and again, the same fares with the same people, drunk and joyous in the warm night, in the city.

When the sky turned pink and the fares became much quieter, her cab filled with people dozing off, Rio thought of the stranger again, she looked at the summer sky, reflecting on the windows of the tall buildings, grey and pink and beautiful. 

Rio thought about beauty and the stranger.

If the stranger could see the pink light right now, would she find beauty in it? 

Sunrises are easy to find beautiful, when the light hits the top of the buildings but not yet the street, when it is day above and night below, when most sleep but not Rio, Rio is struck by beauty.

It is her favorite part of the job, not driving, not the city, certainly not the people, but the beauty.

It is offered to her, every night of every week of every month, the beauty is there for her to look at.

But now, on this night, where for the first time ever, Rio, at the wheel of her cab, didn’t drive but was driven by a stranger, she couldn’t see the beauty without thinking of her, of her hair, of her eyes so blue, of her silence and of her stillness.

Beauty is here to be found, but for the first time, that wasn’t enough for Rio.

She wanted, more than anything, to show the pink sky, the pink light on the tall buildings to the stranger and say “see? there is beauty here,” and the stranger would look at the pink sky, at the pink light on the tall buildings and agree that yes, there is beauty here.

Suddenly it was all that mattered to Rio, to show beauty to the stranger and look at the beauty with the stranger’s eyes, she ached so profoundly for it that the drunk man sitting at the back of her car tapped on the partition and pointed out that they were driving in the wrong direction.

Rio barked something to the effect of him not knowing the way to his own ass as she swerved expertly into the growing traffic.

To her own stupor, though, he was right and Rio had to admit that thinking about the stranger made the stranger drive through her again, and terrifyingly and beautifully, for a second, Rio had lost the map imprinted in her mind.

She dropped him off, and the idiot tipped her generously as he stumbled out of her cab, her last fare for the night and Rio reflexively drove to the diner.

 

 

Open at all hours, the diner smelled of plastic and vinyl and vinegar and coffee and grease. 

Rio went because all cabbies went there to have breakfast or dinner, she went because the others went and they shared meals and stories and cigarettes and Rio didn’t like the diner, nor did she like its food but she went because that’s what one does when one drives all night or all day and one is alone with strangers that climbed into their cab.

The cabbies are easily recognizable, a bunch of them were already sat in the diner, at a table near the back, they were laughing and eating and Rio mechanically went to them, they didn’t stop laughing or eating when she sat at their table, they didn’t even really look in her direction except maybe Jennifer Kale, who eyed her like a cat eyes another cat, not hostile yet but cautious.

Rio sat and ordered dinner but she didn’t eat when the plate was brought to her, not because she wasn’t hungry but because the act of eating seemed too mundane for the night she just had so she raised her eyes to the table, looked at the other cabbies and asked “Have you ever had a stranger drive through you?”

And the others stopped talking, stopped eating and stared at her, Jennifer Kale rolled her eyes, like she had been expecting Rio to speak and for Rio to say something nonsensical and absurd, but Rio hadn’t said something nonsensical nor absurd, she had asked a very fair question, one she wanted an honest answer to. 

Unfortunately the others didn’t seem to share the sentiment, as they looked at her and stopped talking.

Someone said something in an overly kind tone, but Rio didn’t want kindness, she wanted an answer.

The others offered suggestions of what they thought she meant, but Rio could not have been clearer with her question. 

She thought to rephrase it but couldn’t find words that made her point clearer and the others didn’t understand what she meant. 

They tried to, did she mean an accident? No, not an accident, did she let a client drive in her place? No, that is forbidden, did a passenger comment on her every turn? (some were annoying like that) no, her stranger hadn't said anything.

But the others were soon disinterested in her question, they offered their own stories of annoying passengers and strange encounters, they all had stories, each more interesting than the last but Rio didn’t want stories, she wanted an answer and no one gave her one.

By the time her meal had cooled and she had pushed every bit of potato around her plate, Rio had given up on getting an answer, so she rose and went to pay and went to go home and sleep, maybe sleep before starting her next night when, at the cash register, Alice Wu-Gulliver laid a hand on her arm and leaned closer.

Rio didn’t jump because Alice was kind and she was always fair to Rio even when the others laughed at her.

“Are you ok?” Alice asked, and Rio nodded, she was tired she said.

People that were odd always said they were tired, Rio had learned. 

Saying you were tired excused a lot of strange behaviors and Rio didn’t think her behaviors to be strange but the others seemed to think so, so it was probably the case.

Alice didn’t remove her hand from Rio’s arm, in fact she leaned even closer and said something Rio hadn’t dared to hope for, she said: “Tell me about the stranger.” 

Something in Rio’s chest bloomed. 

She hadn’t realized until now how she wanted to tell people about the stranger, so Rio spoke, she tried to make her voice soft and her words go slow as to appear casual.

So Rio didn’t say much and Alice Wu-Gulliver listened, Jennifer Kale joined them and surprisingly listened as well when Rio told them that the stranger didn’t say much and that Rio drove her around the city with no aim until oddly, the stranger got off, paid her and hopped into a bus.

“A bus?”

A bus.

“What bus?”

And Rio cursed herself for not having looked at the bus’ number, she didn’t know anything other than it was an ordinary bus, one that drove through the city like she drove through the city.

“You’re sure it wasn’t a money thing?” Alice asked, and Rio shook her head, the stranger had paid and tipped.

“She took the bus because she didn’t want you to know where she lived,” said Jennifer, final.

Rio frowned, why would the stranger not trust her—

“It’s not about you, it’s about her, she was probably fleeing something.”

No, the stranger was calm, so calm in fact that Rio got calm driving her.

Jennifer and Alice shared a look that Rio didn’t understand, and Rio was getting tired for good this time and she wanted to be home and to sleep, hopefully sleep before the next night.

“Rio drove a serial killer around,” Jennifer snickered. 

Rio frowned, she didn’t like that, didn’t like that Jennifer Kale would joke about the stranger, her stranger, who was so silent and so still and  drove Rio’s cab through Rio in the warm night in the city.

So, Rio shook her head and went home to her bed.
