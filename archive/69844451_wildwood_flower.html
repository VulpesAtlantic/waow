“No worries,” Carol said. “You’re getting close, aren’t you?”

Agatha nodded, but she didn’t want to think about it.

“I’ve been meaning to ask you, would you like some baby clothes?” Carol asked. “My partner and I were talking the other night, and we’ve got a lot of clothes that our daughter has outgrown. I know you don’t know what you’re having, but a lot of the clothes are gender neutral. They’re yours if you want.”

Agatha blinked in surprise. “Your partner?”

Carol smiled broadly. “Yeah, Maria. She carried, and we used a donor. Our daughter Monica just celebrated her second birthday last month. Here…”

Carol reached into her back pocket and pulled out a wallet like men normally carried. She dug around inside for a moment before pulling out a photo. It showed Carol and a Black woman with short hair on either side of a curly haired little girl. A handwritten banner was hung up behind them with Happy Birthday Monica painted in bright yellow text, and a Lion King themed birthday cake sat on the table in front of them.

“She’s a cutie,” Agatha said with a grin.

“Yeah,” Carol said with a wide smile, taking one more look at the photo before tucking it back into her wallet. “So, what do you say? If you don’t want the clothes we’ll probably take them to Goodwill, so…”

“I’ll take them,” Agatha replied. “Thank you.”

“Cool,” Carol nodded. “I’ll bring them next week.”

Carol went back to organizing the register, but the conversation played in Agatha’s head for the rest of the day. Carol had a partner. Carol was gay. Rio was also gay. Did Rio have a partner? Was that who Agatha had heard upstairs in her apartment?

Agatha shook that thought from her head. It didn’t matter that Rio was a lesbian. It didn’t matter if she had a partner or not. It didn’t affect Agatha, so she didn’t care.

She considered the other employees she had met: Billy with his eyeliner, America who sometimes mentioned her moms plural, Yelena who— well, Yelena didn’t seem to be interested in romantic relationships at all, with men or women. Did Rio only hire gay people to work here? Was that even legal? And if that was the case, why had she decided to hire Agatha?

Agatha liked boys. Men. Whatever. Or, at least, she liked them well enough. She didn’t particularly care for sex, but she knew a lot of women who felt that way. It didn’t mean that Agatha liked women.

But, Rio and Carol had both said that they didn’t think Agatha would fit in here. So maybe they knew. And Agatha had been a bit insistent on getting the job. So, Agatha thought, perhaps it was nothing more than that. Perhaps Rio simply hired people who society often looked down on and cast out. If that was the case, Agatha thought, she certainly fit in.

When Agatha left the restaurant at three, she didn’t head back to the Walmart. Instead she retrieved the dogwood sapling from where she had stashed it at the back of the parking lot and crossed the street to the library. It wasn’t a large building, but it was quaint. The circulation desk sat to one side of the entrance, and across from it was a cozy seating area. There was a children’s area, short shelves lined with picture books, complete with two large aquariums: one held colorful fish, and the other held two box turtles. Beyond those sat a bank of computers, their screens flashing green and gray, and several adult-sized tables for patrons to use while they worked and studied. The rest of the space was taken up by bookshelves, all neatly organized and dust free. Agatha expected nothing less from a space managed by Mrs. Davis.

The older woman was with a young man at the card catalog, squinting as she flicked through the cards. A moment later she clicked her tongue in disappointment.

“I’m sorry, Peter, but I’m afraid I don’t think we have that particular volume on arachnids,” she told him. “I can try reaching out to some other libraries in the surrounding counties, see if they have anything.”

“I’d really appreciate it, Mrs. Davis,” the young man said. “Thanks for all your help.”

Mrs. Davis patted the young man on the back, and then she turned. As soon as her eyes landed on Agatha, her face lit up.

“Agnes!” she exclaimed.

“It’s Agatha, actually,” Agatha corrected her.

“Oh, silly me. Agatha!” Mrs. Davis greeted, reaching up to pat Agatha’s cheeks. “It’s so good to see you again.”

For some strange reason, Agatha believed her. Mrs. Davis ushered her over to the seating area, fussing over her the whole way and insisting that it was fine if she needed to put her swollen feet up on the table.

“My ankles keep swelling,” Agatha grumbled as she leaned back into the sofa cushions.

“Mine did that with James, my youngest,” Mrs. Davis told her. “I also gained thirty pounds with him and never quite lost it , unfortunately. But you, dear, you’re glowing!”

Agatha’s hair was sticking to her sweaty neck, her ankles looked like sausages, and her bladder was the size of a peanut. That wasn’t exactly her definition of glowing, but whatever. “You still have your tree,” Mrs. Davis said, her voice soft. There was no judgment, just concern.

“There’s nowhere to plant it at my new apartment,” Agatha lied.

“Oh? Where are you living, dear?”

“Spring Hill Apartments. It’s the complex behind the elementary school,” Agatha told her. It was a lie, but hopefully not for long. Agatha had plans to go visit that complex on Monday, so the lie would turn into a truth very soon.

“Oh, yes, that’s a lovely place but not a lot of options for gardening,” Mrs. Davis nodded.

“It’s not doing so well,” Agatha said, running her hands along a drooping leaf. It fell to the floor as she touched it, and Agatha’s eyes went round. “So, um, I was hoping you might know a place where I could plant it. I’d hate to just let it die, you know?”

Mrs. Davis smiled. “I knew you were a good egg.” What the fuck did that mean? “You know, there’s a bare spot in my yard that would be just perfect for a dogwood.”

Agatha’s face lit up. “You’ll take it then?”

“Sure,” Mrs. Davis replied. “As soon as I get finished here, we can head back to my place and get it planted.”

Agatha’s smile faded. “We?”

Agatha waited two and a half hours for the library to close. She thought about slipping out, leaving the dogwood behind for Mrs. Davis to find, but the sofa was very comfortable and the air conditioned library felt much better than the heat and humidity outside.

About an hour into her wait, Agatha had to go to the bathroom. On her way back, she meandered through the shelves. She found a book on baby names and took it back to the sofa to look through.

You have to choose who they’re gonna be. It’s important to pick a name that means something.

Rio’s words echoed through Agatha’s mind as she flipped open the book. The first few pages listed the one hundred most popular names from the year the book was published, 1991, and Agatha skimmed those lists. She didn’t want to name her baby something too common. There were four girls named Jennifer and three named Amy in her grade level growing up, and the teachers were always getting them mixed up with each other. One good thing about Agatha’s name was that it was unique, so she never got mistaken for anyone else.

As Agatha looked through the pages, she pulled out a notepad to write down names that she liked and their meaning. The boys’ names came first in the book: Alexander- defender of men, Daniel- God is my judge, Nicholas- victory of the people, Stephen- crown, Theodore- gift from God, Wesley- west meadow.

Agatha stared at the list, rubbing her belly as she did. She didn’t know for sure if the baby was a boy or a girl, but something told her it was a boy. Maybe it was mother’s intuition, or maybe it was wishful thinking. Boys seemed much easier than girls. Sure, they could be rough and tumble, but Agatha could handle that. She didn’t think she could handle a girl. She worried that a girl might be like her, selfish and wicked like her mother always said. So, Agatha thought, a boy would be much easier.

“Who are you?” she whispered as the stranger in her belly kicked, a firm bump bump just above Agatha’s inside-out belly button. “Are you Alexander?” No kick. “Daniel?” Nothing. “Nicholas?” The baby didn’t respond, didn’t help her decision in any way.

Agatha opened the book again, flipping to the section with the girls’ names. Her eyes flickered down the page with A-names. She found the entry for Agatha, and sure enough, it meant “good.” She ran her finger further down the page. Alice, Anna, Aubrey… Finally, Agatha found a name to write down.

“Ava,” Agatha said as she copied the entry into her notepad. The name had several different meanings from different languages. “Bird, life, voice, desired…”

The baby kicked, one time, so hard that it knocked the air from Agatha’s lungs. Agatha’s hand went to her stomach, her mind

“Still looking for a name for your little one?”

Agatha looked up to see Mrs. Davis standing over her, a smile on her face. Agatha closed the book and stood unsteadily, her legs wobbling so much that Mrs. Davis reached out to help her.

“You should check that book out, if you need more ideas,” Mrs. Davis told her. “Let me set you up with a library card real quick before we go.”

“I don’t have any money to spare for that,” Agatha said.

“Oh, a library card is free, dear,” Mrs. Davis explained. “Tax-payer funded. You signing up for a card actually helps us in that regard. The more people that have library cards, the more funding we get from the government.”

Just a few minutes later, Agatha was a card-carrying member of the Westview Public Library. She used it to check out the baby name book, which she tucked between her notebook and the photograph Lilia had given her.

The walk to Mrs. Davis’s house wasn’t long. The First Baptist Church was just across the bridge, near the Amish store that sold handmade furniture. Agatha followed the older woman past the church and they cut through the cemetery into Mrs. Davis’s backyard as a shortcut.

Mrs. Davis’s house was freshly painted olive green with bright white trim. A screened in porch stretched across the back, and the wooden door closed with a loud clack behind them as Mrs. Davis ushered her inside. Agatha expected the house to be empty, but as soon as they stepped into the kitchen they were greeted by a man who, in Agatha’s opinion, looked like a thin version of Santa Claus.

“Oh, hello, sweetheart,” Mrs. Davis said as she hung her purse by the door. “Agatha, this is my gentleman friend, Arthur Prim. Arthur, this is my new friend, Agatha.”

“How do you do,” the man greeted, nodding at Agatha over the pan he was holding. He had a raspy voice that reminded Agatha of radio static. He looked back at Mrs. Davis. “I hope you don’t mind, I let myself in. Wanted to get a head start on dinner.”

“You know I never do,” Mrs. Davis said fondly. “Agatha, dear, will you be staying for dinner?”

“Oh, I don’t want to intrude,” Agatha replied.

“Nonsense. And Arthur’s made spaghetti, which means he’s doubtlessly made enough to feed an army. You’ll stay and eat with us, and then we’ll plant your tree.”

The kitchen was small but homey, with floral wallpaper, warm oak cabinets, and a yellow tile backsplash. Sheer curtains were hung around the window, and decorative plates were placed on top of the cabinets. Agatha sat at the kitchen table beside Mrs. Davis as Mr. Prim served dinner. Finally the man sat down on Mrs. Davis’s opposite side and they held their hands out expectantly, so Agatha nervously placed her hands in theirs.

“Dear Lord,” Mrs. Davis began, and Agatha fought the urge to roll her eyes, “We give thanks for our new friend Agatha, and pray for a safe delivery for her and her child.” Agatha’s stomach growled loudly, and the baby kicked hard, as if it knew that it was being prayed over. “And we ask forgiveness, Lord, for the fornication that Mr. Prim and I committed last night and again this morning.” Agatha’s eyes flew open and her jaw dropped in shock as she looked between the older couple, both of whom still had their eyes closed in prayer. “We pray that you will bless this food for the nourishment of our bodies, and our bodies to your service. We ask this in Jesus’ name. Amen.”

“Amen,” Mr. Prim repeated softly. He opened his eyes and looked up at Agatha. “Toast?”

Agatha nodded, her mouth still open in shock. What the actual fuck? But also… good for them.

“So, Agatha, what brings you to Westview?” Mr. Prim asked.

“Oh, um, I just needed a new start, I guess,” Agatha replied.

“Well, that’s certainly different,” he said. “Seems like most young people around here are trying their best to leave little towns like this. It’s good to see someone who actually wants to start afresh in one again.”

“Agatha’s a good girl,” Mrs. Davis said with a smile. “She’s working at River’s Edge.”

Agatha’s head snapped around so fast that her neck popped. “I didn’t tell you that.” Mr. Prim chuckled. “Oh, darlin’, you’ll find that Sharon here knows just about everything that goes on in this town. You can’t stub your toe without her finding out.”

“Arthur, hush. You’re gonna make her think I’m nosey,” Mrs. Davis scolded.

“Well, if the shoe fits, my lady,” he said, poking her shoulder fondly.

“Anyway, I’m glad you found yourself a job. And Rio’s good people,” Mrs. Davis told her.

“Good people,” Mr. Prim agreed, his words slightly muffled around a bite of spaghetti.

“I didn’t know that she— I mean, not that it matters, but—” Agatha cut herself off.

“Not that what matters?” Mrs. Davis asked, seeming completely oblivious.

“Nothing,” Agatha replied, looking down at her plate.

“Rio Vidal is the best cook in this town,” Mr. Prim interrupted.

“Calling her a cook is underselling it. She’s a chef,” Mrs. Davis said, her voice dripping with respect. She sighed. “Now that’s a young person who wanted out of this town. I imagine she’d be in some big city somewhere, working in a fancy restaurant, but, well, the Lord gives us obstacles.”

Agatha wanted to ask what she meant, but before she could Mr. Prim asked her about where she lived before Westview, and the conversation turned to other subjects.

After dinner, they all went outside, and Mrs. Davis showed Agatha a spot in the corner of her yard that she thought was perfect for the dogwood tree. She said it would be easy to see from the window of her guest room, although what that had to do with anything, Agatha had no clue. Agatha agreed, and Mr. Prim retrieved a shovel from the shed and started digging. Agatha tried to help, but her back started aching again after only shoveling twice. She did insist on placing the sapling into the hole, and tamping the dirt down with her white Keds that didn’t look so white anymore.

When they were finished, Mr. Prim retrieved his guitar and sat on the porch steps. Mrs. Davis got them all a glass of lemonade before joining him. He strummed some chords and Mrs. Davis hummed along, some folk song that Agatha thought she recognized but couldn’t quite place. But some songs were like that, she thought. You knew them in the marrow of your bones even when the words wouldn’t come to mind.

The sun was setting as Agatha made her way back across the bridge into town. She had remained with Mrs. Davis and Mr. Prim for longer than she should, listening as the two told stories about the town and its people. Mr. Prim had been a history teacher at the high school with Mrs. Davis, so he was just as entrenched in Westview as his girlfriend— or, rather, his lady, as he affectionately referred to her. In spite of her initial annoyance at the older woman, Agatha found herself enjoying the evening.

Now, though, as she waddled through downtown, she wished that she had left earlier. She checked her watch frantically as she walked in front of River’s Edge, the music from inside loud enough to vibrate the street. It was eight o’clock. She had an hour to make it across town, something that wouldn’t normally be a problem, but with the way her back was aching, Agatha wasn’t sure she could make it.

“Hey! Agatha!”

Agatha grit her teeth at the sound of Rio’s familiar voice. She thought about ignoring her, pretending she hadn’t heard her. But then she heard the heavy footfalls of Rio’s boots on the sidewalk behind her, so she turned back to her.

Rio’s hair was down, the short strands falling in layered waves just past her chin. She smiled widely at Agatha when she caught up with her, teeth gleaming in the golden light of the streetlights that had just come on.

“Did you and Mrs. Davis plant your tree?” Rio asked.

“Yeah, we did,” Agatha nodded. “After I had dinner with her and Mr. Prim.”

“Oh, you met Mr. Prim? He’s a cool guy,” Rio noted.

“Yeah,” Agatha agreed. “Listen, Rio, I really need to get going. I have to go home.”

“Oh!” Rio exclaimed, like she had just realized how late it was. “I can drive you, if you want. I know you don’t trust my motorcycle, but I can borrow Carol’s car if you just give me a minute.”

“No, that’s alright,” Agatha replied. If she had Rio take her to the apartments near the elementary school, she would have just as far to walk. She could ask Rio to take her to Walmart, tell her that she had something to pick up, but then Rio would probably insist on waiting for her so she could drive her to the apartments after. So Agatha was stuck walking.

“It’s no big deal. I’m sure Carol won’t mind,” Rio argued. “Just wait for a minute and—”

“I can walk, Rio, it’s fine,” Agatha protested, growing more and more agitated by the minute. She checked her watch again: 8:05.

“I just wanna help you, Agatha, c’mon,” Rio insisted.

Agatha frowned. Why was Rio so damn stubborn about this? Why did she want to help her so damn bad? Agatha thought back to whenever boys had offered to drive her places in the past. They only wanted one thing. Was that why Rio wanted to drive her home? Hell, was that why Rio hired her in her restaurant? Because she wanted to sleep with her?

“Rio, I’m not a lesbian!”

Rio froze, her brown eyes wide. “I— I never said you were.”

“I’m not going to sleep with you,” Agatha said firmly.

“I didn’t— I don’t expect you to,” Rio replied. “Do you really think that’s what this is, Agatha?”

“I don’t know,” Agatha huffed. “I just know that’s what boys do. They do something nice for you, and then they expect something in return.”

“Well, I’m not a boy,” Rio said, voice dripping with disgust.

“I know, you’re a lesbian,” Agatha said, as if that were an explanation.

“What does that mean?” Rio asked, clearly offended.

“I just…” Agatha was out of breath, and she wasn’t even walking. “I just want to make sure that you know I’m not interested in women that way. I’m not interested in you in that way. Is that clear?”

“Crystal,” Rio replied, her voice low and emotionless. “Anything else?”

“No.”

“So do you want a ride, or…?”

Agatha couldn’t believe this woman. “No, I’ll walk.”

“Fine!” Rio exclaimed, throwing her hands up in surrender. “Have it your way. Enjoy your walk, I guess.”

“I will!” Agatha shouted.

“Okay!” Rio spat back, frowning. “Goodnight!”

“Goodnight!”

Agatha turned away with a huff, channeling her frustration into walking faster. It was surprisingly effective. She made it to the Walmart by 8:50.
